# Frameworks Library

**Condensed, operational summaries of every framework in the corpus.** Use this as a lookup during an engagement; use `target-audience-research-report.md` when you need the reasoning and `source-map.md` when you need the citation and its reliability.

**Before using this file for the first time, read `../SYNTHESIS.md`.** These 48 cards are instruments, not a reading list — the synthesis explains what they are collectively for, which altitude each operates at, where three of them genuinely conflict, and which conclusions are corroborated across independent traditions rather than asserted by one author.

Each card: **What it is · Mechanics · Use when · Output · Watch out · Source.**

## A. Choosing the market

---

### A1 · Beachhead Market
**What it is.** The discipline of narrowing to one homogeneous segment and dominating it before expanding.

**Mechanics — three conditions for a real segment.** All three must hold, or you have not narrowed enough:
1. End users all use **the same product**.
2. The **sales process is the same** — interchangeable language, value proposition, channel.
3. There is **strong word of mouth between end users**.

**Eight evaluation criteria.** Is the customer well-funded? Accessible to your sales force? Do they have a compelling reason to buy? Can you deliver a whole product? Is there entrenched competition? Can you leverage this segment into others? Is it consistent with the team's values and passions? How quickly can you win it?

**Use when.** Any engagement where the client says "our audience is everyone with this problem." This is the corrective.

**Output.** One named segment, plus the two runners-up and the condition under which they get promoted.

**Watch out.** "Well-funded" is venture framing — soften it to "has an existing budget line" for local business and expert products. Aulet also warns explicitly against analysis paralysis: pick, then test.

**Source.** Bill Aulet, *Disciplined Entrepreneurship* Step 2 — d-eship.com/step2 (read in full).

---

### A2 · Market Types
**What it is.** Four market situations, each of which invalidates the others' playbook.

**Mechanics.**
| Type | Definition | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing** | Customers can name the market and the features they want | Compete on differentiation; keyword data works |
| **Resegmented** | Cheaper, or a niche existing suppliers failed to serve | Compete on focus; find the underserved outcome |
| **New** | Nobody could do this before (cost, availability, skill) | You fund education; **keyword volume is zero and that is correct** |
| **Clone** | A proven model transplanted to a new geography | Demand is pre-validated elsewhere; verify the local reason for absence |

**Use when.** Before choosing research methods. Market type determines which evidence is even applicable.

**Output.** A named market type, and the list of research methods it disqualifies.

**Watch out.** The most common error is running existing-market research (search volume, competitor reviews) on a new market and concluding there is no demand.

**Source.** Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*; steveblank.com — Customer Development Manifesto part 4.

### A3 · Three Tiers of Noncustomers
**What it is.** The best available generator of unexpected audiences.

**Mechanics.**
- **Tier 1 — "soon-to-be":** buy minimally, out of necessity, mentally already gone. Waiting to jump ship.
- **Tier 2 — "refusing":** evaluated the category and consciously said no — unacceptable, unaffordable, or too complicated.
- **Tier 3 — "unexplored":** never considered the category at all; sit in a different market entirely.

**The method.** Look for **commonalities across the tiers**, not differences. What one thing, changed, would convert several tiers at once?

**Use when.** The obvious segments are saturated, or the client wants growth beyond the current base.

**Output.** Three candidate segments, one per tier, each with the specific barrier that keeps them out.

**Watch out.** Tier 2 refusers are the richest and least interviewed group in commerce. Almost nobody talks to them. That is the opportunity.

**Source.** Kim & Mauborgne, *Blue Ocean Strategy* — blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/three-tiers-of-noncustomers (read in full).

### A4 · Four Market Criteria + the hierarchy
**What it is.** The most compressed usable test of an audience.

**Mechanics.** **Massive pain · purchasing power · easy to target · growing.**
Hierarchy: **Market > Offer > Persuasion.** Great at a higher level overpowers everything below; bad at a higher level stops the chain.

**Use when.** Fast triage. Thirty seconds per candidate segment.

**Output.** Pass / fail per criterion, with the failing one named.

**Watch out.** "Easy to target" is the criterion people skip and it is load-bearing — it means *do they belong to identifiable associations, lists, groups, channels?* "Growing" is often unverifiable for niche B2B; do not fake it.

**Source.** Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (read via secondary summaries).

### A5 · Market Attractiveness / Segment Prioritisation Matrix
**What it is.** The consulting-standard two-axis scoring model.

**Mechanics.** Score each segment on two axes with 5–10 weighted criteria each, weights summing to 100% per axis.
- **Attractiveness:** size, growth, profit potential / WTP, competitive intensity, pricing dynamics, regulatory risk.
- **Ability to win:** capabilities, product fit, brand relevance, channel access, cost-to-serve, time to credible scale.

**The rule that makes it work.** Write down explicitly what a "1" and a "5" mean for every criterion **before** scoring. Otherwise it is vibes with decimals.

**Use when.** More than four candidate segments; or when stakeholders disagree and you need to locate *where*.

**Output.** Scored grid, with disagreements converted into research tasks.

**Watch out.** Revisit against actuals (win rates, margin, retention) rather than treating the first scoring as permanent.

**Source.** Umbrex framework library; Malcolm McDonald; Notion Capital MPM.

### A6 · Bottom-up market sizing (TAM / SAM / SOM)
**What it is.** Sizing from countable customers instead of from a report headline.

**Mechanics.** `Market size = realistic price × number of reachable customers`. Cross-check against a top-down estimate; landing within ~2× suggests sane assumptions.

**Use when.** A segment needs to be proven big enough to matter — or small enough to dominate.

**Output.** A number with its assumptions visible and attackable.

**Watch out.** The real value is not the number. It is that **a segment you cannot count is a segment you cannot target** — sizing doubles as a reachability test.

**Source.** Forum VC; Qubit Capital; HubSpot.

### A7 · Market definition + the Market Map
**What it is.** Establishing the market boundary by need, then mapping where in the chain the buying decision is actually made — and quantifying it.

**The definition rule.** *"A market is the aggregation of all products or services which customers regard as being capable of satisfying the same need."*
**This is the same test as Christensen's abstraction test (B1)**, reached independently from the market-structure tradition. The strongest cross-source agreement in the corpus.

**Why it matters.** Correct market definition is required for: share measurement · growth measurement · specifying target customers · **recognising relevant competitors** · formulating strategy. *"Defining a market too broadly or too narrowly can both lead to meaningless statistics."* **Never accept a client's market-share figure without asking what market it is measured against.**

**The market map — mechanics.**
1. Plot the distribution and value-added chain from suppliers to final users; draw the routes to market.
2. At each junction, list the company/customer types found there.
3. Quantify each junction by volume or value.
4. **Starting at the final-user junction, record the % of the total market *decided* at each junction.** Guesstimate what you don't know and log it as follow-up.

**Worked example:** final users 300k, 70% decided there · contractors 75k, 20% · retailers 180k, 10% → **30% of annual sales are decided somewhere other than the final user.** A campaign aimed only at end users concedes that 30% uncontested.

**The rule:** *"Segmentation can and should be carried out at all major junctions on the market map, not just at the final user junction."*

**Also settles:** customers ≠ consumers. The parent buying cereal is an agent for the eventual consumer; the purchasing officer buys against a specification that originates with the end user. Confining marketing to whoever places the order is *"a major mistake"* — and the fertiliser case shows why: sell only to merchants and you are defenceless the moment your product advantage is copied.

**Use when.** Anything sold through channels, distributors, specifiers, contractors or agents. Also whenever "who is our audience?" has more than one plausible answer.

**Watch out.** Complex junctions should be sketched separately; do not try to fit everything on one page.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/market-mapping.md` §1, §3.
**Source.** McDonald & Dunbar. See `source-map.md` §10.1.

### A8 · Micro-segments, KDF/CPI and the segment checklist
**What it is.** The bottom-up engine: build segments from observed purchase combinations rather than from lenses applied top-down.

**Micro-segment structure:** *What is bought · Where · When · How · Who (descriptors) · **Why (benefits sought)** · size.* These are **actual purchase combinations occurring in the market**, not invented profiles.

**Volumes:** **30–50 micro-segments** normal; **7–10 final segments** is the practical ceiling.

**KDF vs CPI — the distinction that makes clustering work:**

| | Definition | Screen |
|---|---|---|
| **KDF** — Key Discriminating Feature | Only those features customers actually use to **choose between alternatives** | Exclude table stakes. A feature everyone offers discriminates nothing |
| **CPI** — Critical Purchase Influence | The **benefit** each KDF delivers — why the feature is wanted | *"For some customers it is only by **combining** certain KDFs that they attain the benefit"* |

**The 100-point allocation.** CPI importance is set per micro-segment *"by distributing 100 points between the CPIs."* Constant-sum, which forces trade-offs — rating scales let everything be "very important" and produce no clusters.

**Forming segments:** score CPIs → measure similarity → cluster → size clusters by summing constituent volumes.

**The Step-7 checklist — four questions, and the fourth is the one nobody asks:**
> 1. Is each cluster big enough to justify a distinct marketing strategy?
> 2. Is the offer required by each cluster sufficiently different?
> 3. Is it clear which customers appear in each cluster? *(all yes → clusters = segments)*
> 4. **Will the company change and adopt a segment focus?**

**Question 4 tests the organisation, not the segmentation.** A segment the company will not reorganise around is a slide, not a segment. Ask it during intake, not at delivery.

**Use when.** Established business with sales data, weeks rather than days, channel complexity. It is the **heavyweight tier** — McDonald concedes the method is *"hard work, and is time-consuming."*

**Watch out.** The "why" row is where desk work ends. If you cannot fill it from evidence, the table is `L1` — a structured hypothesis, not a finding.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/market-mapping.md` §4–§7.
**Source.** McDonald & Dunbar. See `source-map.md` §10.1.

### A9 · The commoditisation diagnostic
**What it is.** A test to run whenever a client says *"in our market, everyone buys on price."*

**How markets evolve:** early = homogeneous → rapid growth = **breaks into groups** → maturity = growth at the low-price end, **top end immune, middle suffers**.

**The trap.** *"Competing only on price is to assume that this is the main requirement of customers, whereas the truth is that this is rarely the case."* A lack of segmentation understanding *"forces them to trade on price, so encouraging the market to become a commodity market."* Commoditisation is **frequently a segmentation failure suppliers cause**, not a condition that happens to them — reaching it is *"by no means inevitable."*

**The evidence — ICI Fertilisers.** The whole industry, *especially the sales force*, believed 100% of farmers bought on price. Segmentation found **seven farmer types**, of which the genuinely price-driven "Arthur" was **10% of the market**. "Oliver" ran satellite soil analysis to maximise yield and bought cheaply *only because no supplier had understood his needs*. "David" wanted crops and cattle looking healthy. ICI became the only profitable fertiliser company in the country.

**The straddling error.** *"Select a segment and serve it. Do not straddle segments and sit between them."* A photocopier incumbent with **80% share** tried to compete in all segments with one product and **fell to 10% in three years**.

**Use when.** Any mature or price-pressured market; any client whose differentiation story has collapsed into discounting.

**The question to ask:** *"What proportion of your market genuinely buys on price alone, and what evidence establishes that number?"* A sales-force impression is not evidence.

**Source.** McDonald & Dunbar. See `source-map.md` §10.1.

### B1 · Jobs To Be Done — the job statement
**What it is.** Defining the audience by the progress they are trying to make, not by who they are.

**The definition.** A job is **"the progress that a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance."** Statement form: **When [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].**

The milkshake demonstration: the same demographic bought the same product for two entirely different jobs (long boring commute vs. treat for a child), needing two different products. Averaging them produces *"a one-size-fits-none product that doesn't do either of the jobs well."*

**Three validity tests — run all three before accepting any job statement:**

| Test | Fails when | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Abstraction** | Every candidate you could hire comes from **one product category** | Go up a level. *"12-oz milkshake"* → all milkshakes ❌. *"Occupy me on the drive, keep me full till 10"* → banana, doughnut, bagel, Snickers, coffee ✅ |
| **Verbs and nouns** | It is expressed in adjectives — *"convenience"*, *"seamless"*, *"reliable"* | Rewrite as what they are trying to **do**, to **what** |
| **Need / theme boundary** | It is true on most days regardless of circumstance (a *need*), or cannot be attached to a moment (a *life theme*) | *"Feel like a good dad"* is a life theme, not a job. Find the obstacle blocking it, in a specific circumstance |

The abstraction test is the sharpest instrument here. **The clearest sign you have reached the right altitude is when "do something else entirely" appears on the candidate list** — moving to San Francisco is a legitimate competitor to house insulation.

**The four wrong organizing principles** managers use instead of circumstance: product attributes · customer characteristics · trends · competitive response. None is wrong; all are insufficient and non-predictive.

**Use when.** Always, as the primary definition. Every other lens describes the people doing this job.

**Output.** 1–3 job statements per candidate segment, in the customer's own words, each passing all three tests.

**Watch out.** A job is not directly targetable — no ad platform sells "people trying to make progress." Bridge to trigger events and habitats. Strategyn disputes the milkshake as a rigorous segmentation exemplar, so present it as illustration, not method. And respect the stated boundaries: Jobs Theory does not apply where there is no real struggle, where existing solutions are good enough, or where the decision is essentially mathematical — **"cost or efficiency is not a core element of a job."**

**Source.** Christensen et al., *Competing Against Luck* (2016) — **read in full**; Christensen, Cook & Hall, "Marketing Malpractice," HBR 2005. See `source-map.md` §9.1.

### B1b · Big Hire / Little Hire
**What it is.** The split between buying and actually using.

| | **Big Hire** | **Little Hire** |
|---|---|---|
| What | The purchase | Actually consuming it |
| Tracked by | Almost all sales data | Almost nothing |
| Proves | Willingness to buy | **The job was real** |

> *"The Big Hire might suggest that a product has solved a customer's job, but only a consistent series of Little Hires can confirm it."*

The dress with the tag on. The app opened once. **A vendor counting downloads knows nothing about whether the job was solved.**

**Use when.** Assessing an existing customer base, or interpreting any transaction data. Ask for **usage**, not purchases.

**Watch out.** High Big Hire with low Little Hire is a churn warning and a sign the job was mis-identified.

**Paired question:** *"What has to get fired for my product to get hired?"* Something is always fired — including doing nothing. Firing something with emotional or social dimensions is far harder than firing a functional solution.

**Source.** *Competing Against Luck*, ch. 5.

### B1c · The Three Fallacies of Innovation Data
**What it is.** How organisations lose evidence they already had. Drifts, not errors — they happen to competent teams by default.

1. **Active vs. passive data.** *Passive data* is the unstructured context of struggle — frustrations, workarounds, nonconsumption; *"always present, but it isn't loud."* *Active data* is what operations generate — sales, customer attributes, benchmarks; *"very loud. It shouts at you."* Teams migrate to it because it feels rigorous, and start **managing the numbers instead of the job**. Note what active data organises around: **products and customer characteristics** — the two things an audience must never be defined by.
2. **Surface growth.** Selling more products to existing customers until you are doing many jobs for many people, each against a specialist who does that one job better. The *NYT* competing simultaneously with *The Economist*, television and the free *Metro*.
3. **Conforming data.** *"We focus on those signals that tell a story about the world as we would like it to be, not how it really is."* Every function arrives armed with supporting data; none is objective; none reflects the job.

**The underlying warning.** *"All data is man-made."* *"Data is not the phenomena."* The subjectivity of qualitative research is obvious; **the subjectivity of numerical data hides behind its superficial precision** — hence Tom Monahan's proposed **Museum of False Precision.** Even financial statements are *"the result of processes of estimation, negotiation, debate, and politics."*

**Use when.** Auditing your own research pass, and before presenting any score. **A pass built only on data the client handed you has consulted only active data** and will reproduce their existing view of their market.

**Source.** *Competing Against Luck*, ch. 8. Full treatment: `../knowledge/evidence-ladder.md` §10b.

### B2 · Outcome-Driven Innovation & the Opportunity Algorithm
**What it is.** A numeric definition of "underserved."

**Mechanics.** `Opportunity = importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0)`, scale 0–20.
- Above ~15 → **severely underserved**: build and target here.
- Below ~10 → **overserved**: disruptable from below with cheaper and simpler.

Segment by unmet outcome, not by demographics or behaviour.

**Use when.** Choosing between segments that all have pain; or looking for a budget/low-cost segment (the overserved end).

**Output.** Ranked outcomes with an underserved/overserved verdict.

**Watch out.** Full ODI needs quantitative outcome surveys most small businesses will never run. Use the *logic* — importance vs. satisfaction gap — qualitatively from review mining.

**Source.** Anthony Ulwick / Strategyn.

### B3 · Four Forces of Progress + the Switch Interview
**What it is.** The mechanics of why someone switches, and the interview that recovers them.

**Mechanics.** **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit → the switch happens.**
- **Push:** pain of the current situation.
- **Pull:** attraction of the new solution.
- **Anxiety:** fear of the new — will it work, will it be hard, will I look stupid.
- **Habit:** attachment to the current mess — "it's a mess, but it's *our* mess."

**Switch Interview.** Recruit buyers from the last 90 days. Start at the purchase and walk **backwards** to the first thought. Find the moment the passive thought became an active search. ~10 interviews of 30–60 min typically surfaces the 3–5 struggling moments covering most of a market.

**Use when.** There are recent buyers to talk to. The single richest source of audience truth.

**Output.** A timeline per buyer; a consolidated four-forces table; a verbatim quote bank.

**Watch out.** Anxiety and Habit are your **objections, known in advance**. They belong in content, not in the sales call.

**Source.** Bob Moesta & Chris Spiek — jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces; Intercom podcast.

### B4 · The Mom Test
**What it is.** The rule that stops research producing polite lies.

**Mechanics.** Never ask opinions or predictions. Ask about what has already happened:
- What did you do? When? How long did it take?
- What did it cost you?
- What did you try before? What happened?
- What did you pay for?
- Who else was involved?

**Use when.** Every interview, without exception.

**Output.** Facts with dates and amounts, not enthusiasm.

**Watch out.** Any question containing "would you" produces fiction. If the transcript contains no dates and no numbers, the interview failed.

**Source.** Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* / «Спроси маму» (read via RU secondary sources).

### B5 · Lead users & extreme users
**What it is.** The strongest single heuristic for finding unexpected audiences.

**Mechanics.** Users at the leading edge experience mainstream needs early and in extreme form, **and they build their own workarounds**. Includes users in *other markets* facing the same problem more harshly.
Evidence in the corpus: in studies of extreme sports clubs, over a third of members had built or modified their own equipment.

**Where to look.** Shared spreadsheet templates · Zapier/Make chains · scripts on GitHub · "I built a thing to solve X" posts · Notion templates.

**Use when.** Segment generation, always. Run it at least once per engagement.

**Output.** A list of workarounds found in public, each with its builder — who is a validated, self-identified buyer.

**Watch out.** Someone who built a workaround has already paid in hours. That is proven willingness to pay in the only currency that never lies. Their workaround is also a free product specification.

**Source.** Eric von Hippel, lead-user theory (1986); HBR "Let the Users Take the Lead."

### B6 · Five Stages of Awareness + Market Sophistication
**What it is.** The state an audience is in, which determines message, channel and cost.

**Mechanics.**
| Stage | They know | What they need | Acquisition cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing | To be shown the problem exists | Highest — you fund 3 sales |
| Problem-aware | They have the problem | To learn solutions exist | High, but free content works |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist | Why yours | Moderate — the sweet spot |
| Product-aware | Yours exists | Why now, why not the other one | Low |
| Most aware | Everything | A reason to act today | Lowest |

**Market sophistication** (stages 1–5) tracks how many competing claims the market has already heard. In a sophisticated market, only a **new mechanism** or a **narrower audience** creates contrast.

**Use when.** Before choosing channels and before writing any message.

**Output.** An awareness distribution across the segment, and the message register for each.

**Watch out.** Unaware audiences are the worst fit for free acquisition and the worst fit for paid. Narrowing to a problem-aware sub-segment is usually the right move.

**Source.** Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), via practitioner summaries.

### B7 · Лестница узнавания Бена Ханта (Hunt's ladder) — RU
**What it is.** The RU-canonical twin of B6.

**Mechanics.** Six rungs (0–5) from "no awareness of the problem" to purchase. **On the first four rungs the customer needs information only; the purchase happens at rung 5.**

**Use when.** Any RU engagement. Clients will expect this name.

**Output.** Same as B6, expressed in the vocabulary the client uses.

**Watch out.** Do not present Schwartz to a RU client and Hunt to an EN client. Use each market's canonical name — the mechanics are the same and the unfamiliar name costs credibility for nothing.

**Source.** RU marketing practice — Kontur, Yagla and others.

### B8 · Five Rings of Buying Insight
**What it is.** The corrective to persona theatre.

**Mechanics.** A usable persona captures the **buying decision**, not the biography:
1. **Priority Initiative** — what triggered the search *now*.
2. **Success Factors** — the outcomes they expect.
3. **Perceived Barriers** — why they might not buy *you*.
4. **Buyer's Journey** — who is involved, what they consult.
5. **Decision Criteria** — what they compare, on what dimensions.

**Use when.** Any time a persona is requested. Produce this instead of a demographic sketch.

**Output.** Five rings per buying role — not per demographic type.

**Watch out.** "Marina, 34, loves yoga and travel" attached to a B2B brief is the canonical failure. If the persona contains a hobby and no decision criterion, it is decorative.

**Source.** Adele Revella, Buyer Persona Institute — Buyer Persona Manifesto.

### B9 · How Brands Grow — the counterweight
**What it is.** The evidence that bounds this entire discipline.

**Mechanics.** In established consumer categories, brands grow chiefly by **reaching more category buyers, especially light buyers** — not by deepening loyalty in a narrow segment. Competing brands' customer bases are statistically near-interchangeable. Light buyers account for a large share of volume.

**Use when.** The client is an established brand in a mass consumer category with distribution already solved.

**Output.** A regime verdict: focus, or reach.

**Watch out.** Both regimes are real. Handing a mass brand a beachhead strategy and handing a bootstrapped consultant a "reach all category buyers" strategy are symmetrical failures. **Name the regime before recommending.** The evidence is strongest in repertoire FMCG and contested for considered and B2B purchases.

**Source.** Byron Sharp / Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, *How Brands Grow*, via summaries.

### B10 · Category Entry Points (CEP) — 7W + 3C
**What it is.** All the thoughts, needs and moments that lead someone to consider a purchase in a category. *"Something quick to drink after the gym."* *"Software to invoice without an accountant."*

**Why it belongs in an audience skill.** It generates segments through **situations instead of people**, which reaches candidates that people-first thinking structurally cannot. "Who buys a massage gun?" gives you a demographic. "In what situation does someone think about a massage gun?" gives you six segments — after a workout · after a desk day · pain appeared · a gift for parents · wanting to relax · a doctor recommended it — with different messages, different channels, and in the gift case **a different person entirely**.

**The 7Ws** (Romaniuk, *Better Brand Health*): **Why · When · Where · While · With/For Whom · With What · hoW-feeling.** Run all seven; *While*, *With What* and *hoW-feeling* are the three most often skipped and the most likely to be uncontested.

**The 3C screen** (LinkedIn B2B Institute): **Commonness** (how often it occurs) · **Competition** (how crowded) · **Credibility** (can we own it honestly).
```
CEP PRIORITY = Commonness × Credibility ÷ Competition
```

**The challenger play.** A leader attaches to as many CEPs as possible — broader, fresher CEP networks correlate with higher acquisition and retention. **Everyone else should find one high-commonness, low-competition, high-credibility CEP and own it completely.** You will not out-remember the incumbent on the biggest situation; you can be the only brand attached to a smaller one.

**Free bonus.** The **With/For Whom** column detects buyer ≠ user without any extra work.

**Key quotes.** *"Category entry points are not about the brand, they're about the buyer."* · *"The most important search engine is still the one in your mind."*

**Use when.** Generating candidates · the obvious segments are saturated · a consumer or B2B category where situations vary more than people do.

**Watch out.** A brainstormed CEP map is `L1` — hypotheses, not findings. And CEP originates in the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition that argues *against* narrow targeting (B9); using it for a focus decision is a legitimate but non-orthodox borrowing. Say so if challenged.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/category-entry-points.md`.
**Source.** Jenni Romaniuk / Ehrenberg-Bass; LinkedIn B2B Institute. See `source-map.md` §9.10.

### B11 · The Customer Profile — jobs, pains, gains
**What it is.** The recording instrument. JTBD (B1) is the discovery method — *how to find the job*. This is what a **complete** audience profile must contain, and how to fill it systematically.

**Scoping:** the canvas has two halves. This skill takes the **Customer Profile (circle)**; the **Value Map (square)** is offer design and belongs to `strong-offer-creator`.

**Pains — three types, three different responses:**

| Type | Response |
|---|---|
| **Undesired outcomes / characteristics** (functional, social, emotional, ancillary) | Fix or differentiate |
| **Obstacles** — prevent starting or slow it down | **Removal.** An obstacle is not persuaded away |
| **Risks** — could go wrong with serious consequences | **Reassurance** — guarantee, proof, references, pilot |

Obstacles map to Habit, risks map to Anxiety (B3). Confusing them wastes budget.

**Gains — four levels:** **required** (doesn't work without it) · **expected** · **desired** (surfaces when asked) · **unexpected**.
> **Required and expected are table stakes — the same object as McDonald's non-discriminating features (A8).** Deliver them; never claim them. **Differentiation lives at desired and unexpected.**

**Supporting jobs — three roles:** value **buyer** (comparing, deciding, queueing, paying) · value **co-creator** (reviews, co-development) · value **transferrer** (**cancelling, disposing, passing on, reselling**).
> The transferrer role is where switching friction hides — the dining room table, the old mattress at the kerb, *"what happens to my data if we cancel?"*

**Three techniques that make it fillable:**
1. **Quantify everything.** *"Waiting is a waste of time"* → after how many minutes? → *"losing more than X minutes."* *"Improve performance"* → *"raise X above Y."* Never accept an adjective.
2. **The mirror.** From each job, generate the gain, its inverse pain, the obstacle blocking the job, and the risk of failing it. Four entries from one job.
3. **Ladder with "why?"** — learn a language → improve the CV → earn more. The climbing procedure; the abstraction test (B1) is the stopping rule.

**Rank all three columns:** jobs by importance, pains by severity, gains by essentiality. **An unranked profile is `L1` regardless of length.**

**Six mistakes:** several segments in one profile · mixing jobs and outcomes · functional only · **listing with an eye to your own offer** · too few items · too vague.

**Use when.** Recording a segment for handoff; building the segment object; any brief going to an offer, positioning or product team.

**Watch out.** It is a recording and workshop instrument, **not a discovery method.** Pair with B1.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/customer-profile.md`.
**Source.** Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda & Smith, *Value Proposition Design*. See `source-map.md` §11.1.

### B12 · Buying roles and multi-sided fit
**Six B2B stakeholder roles:** influencers · **recommenders** (search, evaluate, issue a formal recommendation) · economic buyers (control budget, focus on savings) · decision makers · end users · **saboteurs** (obstruct search, evaluation or acquisition).

**The structural point:** *"Only the decision maker is reliably inside the buying organisation."* `[E]` Economic buyers (a government funding nursing-home equipment), end users (a chip vendor's customer two steps downstream), recommenders (an external architect or consultant) and saboteurs may all sit **outside** it.

**It applies in B2C too.** A family buying a games console contains an economic buyer, an influencer, a decision maker, users and opponents. Build a profile per role, not per household.

**Multi-sided fit.** Intermediary models (Haier → Carrefour/Walmart → consumers) and platforms (Airbnb: hosts and guests) need a **complete profile per side.** *"If you don't create a clear value proposition for the intermediary, the product may never reach the end consumer."*

> **Third convergence on one instruction:** McDonald's demand-pull warning, the market map's junction quantification (A7), and this. **Profile the intermediary as seriously as the end user.**

**Use with A7.** The market map finds *which junction* decides; these roles tell you *what that junction wants*.

**Source.** *Value Proposition Design*. See `source-map.md` §11.1.

---

### B13 · Test the circle before the square
**What it is.** A sequencing rule for validation, with a diagnostic reason.

> **If you smoke-test an offer and it fails, you cannot tell whether the offer was wrong or the audience was wrong.**

Test the **circle** (the customer profile — which jobs, pains and gains genuinely matter) **before** the **square** (the offer), and only then the **rectangle** (the business model).

*"Если начать с тестирования ценностного предложения, вы будете действовать вслепую."* `[E]`

**Three types of fit:**

| Fit | Achieved when | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem–solution** *(on paper)* | Facts confirm the jobs/pains/gains, and you've designed against them. **No evidence anyone wants your solution yet** | `L3–L4` |
| **Product–market** *(in the market)* | Facts confirm the offer creates value and gains traction | `L5–L6` |
| **Business model** *(in the bank)* | Facts confirm profitable, scalable delivery | Economics proven |

**Use when.** Designing any validation plan. Most advice jumps straight to the square, which is why so many *"we tested it and it didn't work"* conclusions are uninterpretable.

**Requirement:** every validation plan includes at least one experiment testing **the profile itself**, run before any offer test.

**Source.** *Value Proposition Design* §3.1. Mapped in `../knowledge/evidence-ladder.md` §8b.

## C. Measuring fit

---

### C1 · The Product-Market Fit Engine
**What it is.** PMF made measurable — and proof that segmentation is itself a growth lever.

**Mechanics.** Survey users who engaged at least twice in two weeks. Four questions:
1. How would you feel if you could no longer use X? — **very / somewhat / not disappointed**
2. What type of people do you think would most benefit from X?
3. What is the main benefit you receive from X?
4. How can we improve X for you?

**Benchmark:** ~40% "very disappointed" (Sean Ellis, across ~100 startups).

**The key move.** Superhuman scored 22%. Grouping responses by persona and filtering to the persona dominating the "very disappointed" group raised it to **32% with no product change at all** — then 58% over three quarters.
- Build the **High-Expectation Customer** profile from that group.
- **Ignore "not disappointed" entirely.**
- Mine "somewhat disappointed *who already value the core benefit*" for the roadmap.

**Use when.** There is an engaged user base. Useless pre-launch.

**Output.** Segmented PMF scores; HXC profile; benefit language verbatim; unexpected-audience candidates from Q2.

**Watch out.** **Question 2 is a free unexpected-audience generator** — answered by people who understand the value and have no reason to flatter you.

**Source.** Rahul Vohra / Superhuman, First Round Review (read in full); Sean Ellis's survey.

---

### C2 · ABCDX Segmentation — RU
**What it is.** Ranking paying segments by value received × speed of payment.

**Mechanics.**
| Segment | Profile | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Acute problem, buys fast, minimal objections, extracts maximum value | Work intensively; tie sales KPIs to A/B leads |
| **B** | Has the problem often but isn't actively solving it | Persuasion, trials, special offers |
| **C** | Recent or mild problem, indirect value, haggles, delays payment | Automate |
| **D** | No real need; even if they buy they get no value and generate negative experience | **Refuse** |
| **X** | Would be A-grade but needs product work you haven't done (corporates, government) | Roadmap signal, not current demand |

**Attribute split.**
- **Formalized** (public): decision-maker role, industry classification code, headcount, open vacancies, licences, certifications.
- **Non-formalized** (surfaces only in conversation): circumstances of the problem, monetary damage, deadline, budget.

**Use when.** The client has paying customers. The best RU-native complement to ICP work.

**Output.** Every existing customer classified; the shared attributes of A; the shared attributes of D (= your anti-audience).

**Watch out.** The D-segment is the sharpest articulation available of *an audience you should refuse*. Most businesses have never named theirs.

**Source.** Ilya Krasinsky (RU startup ecosystem); documented via Kontur (read in full), vc.ru, sostav.ru. Zamesin and Product Focus versions were unreachable this pass.

### C3 · Four Fits (and Product-Channel Fit)
**What it is.** Why growth stalls even when the product is good.

**Mechanics.** Four interdependent fits: **Market↔Product · Product↔Channel · Channel↔Model · Model↔Market.** Fail one and growth stalls regardless of the others.

**The governing rule.** **"Products are built to fit with channels. Channels do not mold to products."** You do not set the rules of Google, Meta, Gmail, or the app stores.

**Arithmetic.**
- Model-market: `ARPU × total market customers × capture % ≥ target revenue`.
- Channel-model: runs on the **ARPU↔CAC spectrum** — low ARPU cannot fund high-touch channels.

**Use when.** Scoring audience-channel fit; diagnosing a segment that looks perfect on paper and produces nothing.

**Output.** A verdict on whether this audience is reachable by a channel this business model can afford.

**Watch out.** This is why an unreachable segment is not a segment. It is a fantasy with demographics attached.

**Source.** Brian Balfour — brianbalfour.com/four-fits-growth-framework (read in full).

---

### C4 · B2B Buying Group & the four buying jobs
**What it is.** The structure hiding behind the phrase "our audience is the marketing director."

**Mechanics.** A typical complex B2B purchase involves **6–10 people**, each arriving with four or more independently gathered pieces of information. Over three-quarters of buyers describe the process as very complex or difficult. Buyers spend only ~17% of their purchase time with vendors — split across every vendor considered.

**Four buying jobs:** problem identification → solution exploration → requirements building → supplier selection.

**Roles to map separately:** user · buyer · economic decision-maker · influencer · **blocker** · champion.

**Use when.** Any B2B engagement, before writing a single message.

**Output.** A buying-group map with the concern, the fear and the required proof for each seat.

**Watch out.** The highest-leverage and least-contested asset is usually **the material your champion forwards internally** — nobody produces it. Content aimed at the *blocker* removes the real obstacle.

**Source.** Gartner, B2B buying journey research.

---

### C5 · Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter
**What it is.** Comparing willingness to pay across candidate segments cheaply.

**Mechanics.** Four questions per segment. At what price would this be:
1. **Too cheap** — so cheap you'd doubt the quality?
2. **Cheap** — a bargain?
3. **Expensive** — but still worth considering?
4. **Too expensive** — you would not consider it?

The overlap defines an acceptable price corridor.

**Use when.** Two segments score similarly and you need a tiebreaker; or when pricing per segment is in question.

**Output.** A price corridor per segment, plus the behavioural cross-check.

**Watch out.** Stated preference, not behaviour. Does not maximise revenue on its own. **Never use as sole WTP evidence** — triangulate against what they already pay, competitor prices that have survived years, and ad longevity in the segment.

**Source.** Peter van Westendorp (1970s); OpinionX, SurveyMonkey, Conjointly, Umbrex.

### C6 · Economic Value Estimation (EVE) + price segmentation
**What it is.** Pricing against the size of the customer's problem rather than against your own cost.

```
TOTAL ECONOMIC VALUE = REFERENCE VALUE + DIFFERENTIATION VALUE
```
**Reference value** = the price of their best alternative. **Differentiation value** = the worth of what distinguishes you from it. An EVE model is a set of **value drivers**, each with a description and an equation estimating impact on their P&L.

**Why it is in an *audience* skill.** Run EVE per candidate segment and the ranking frequently reorders. It measures **economic exposure**, not enthusiasm — and the segment where your solution touches the most money usually beats the segment that sounded most excited in interviews.

**The three-way gate it enforces:** `PAIN ≠ DEMAND ≠ WILLINGNESS TO PAY`. Extreme pain with no budget is a bad segment. Moderate pain sitting on top of $100k of exposure is potentially excellent.

**Price segmentation — never average.** If two groups have WTP of **$20** and **$100**, pricing at **$60** leaves money on the table with one and prices out the other. **You lose both.** Build two versions. This is the pricing twin of the milkshake error (B1); the detection method is a bimodality check on WTP responses, or the contradiction scan on comments (E6).

**The value metric.** What the price scales with — per seat, per transaction, per location. If two segments need *different* value metrics, they are genuinely different segments.

**Method selection:**

| Method | Measures | Best for | Evidence ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Van Westendorp** | Acceptable price *range* | **New** offers, no reference point | `L3` |
| **Gabor-Granger** | Purchase likelihood per price → demand curve | **Established** categories | `L3` |
| **Conjoint / DCE** | Feature-price trade-offs | Optimising price *and* features | `L3–L4` |
| **Competitor prices held 2+ yrs** | What the market sustains | Always — free | `L5` |
| **Actual transactions** | What was really paid | The tiebreaker | `L6` |

Van Westendorp + Gabor-Granger together deliver roughly **80% of the insight at 30% of the cost** of conjoint.

**The hard rule.** **All stated-preference methods cap at `L3` regardless of sample size.** Never recommend a price without pairing one stated method with one behavioural source.

**Context.** 72% of innovations fail to meet financial targets or fail entirely — which is why the WTP conversation belongs *before* the build, not after.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/willingness-to-pay.md`.
**Source.** Nagle, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*; Ramanujam & Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation*; method comparisons. See `source-map.md` §9.11–9.13.

## D. Finding and reaching them

### D1 · Bullseye Framework + 19 traction channels
**What it is.** Channel selection as an experiment rather than a debate.

**Mechanics.**
- **Outer ring — brainstorm:** all 19 channels, one cheap idea for each.
- **Middle ring — test:** 3–4 most promising, cheap tests in parallel.
- **Inner ring — focus:** when one works, pour everything into it until it stops scaling.

The corpus repeatedly cites roughly **a month and under ~$1,000** to make a channel decision.

**Use when.** After the segment is chosen and habitats are mapped.

**Output.** Three named tests with budgets, timelines and pre-declared success thresholds.

**Watch out.** The discipline is the point: opinions about channels are worthless next to a $200 test.

**Source.** Gabriel Weinberg & Justin Mares, *Traction*. (The author's own Medium post returned 403 this pass; summarised via multiple independent secondary sources.)

---

### D2 · Watering holes / audience intelligence
**What it is.** The operational definition of a habitat.

**Mechanics.** Find the specific publications, podcasts, YouTube channels, subreddits and accounts a defined audience actually consumes. Then "swim in that water" long enough to speak their language and make references they recognise.

**The standard.** A habitat is only named when you can state *the specific instance*. "LinkedIn" is a platform. "These six creators, this Slack group, the alumni list of this certification" is a habitat.

**Use when.** Every engagement, at the channel-mapping step.

**Output.** For each habitat: **name · size · activity level · rules on vendor participation · recognised voices · route to entry · free or paid.**

**Watch out.** The best discovery method is not a tool. It is asking interviewees: *"What do you read every week? Which chats are you in? Whose posts do you never skip?"*

**Source.** Rand Fishkin / SparkToro. Note: SparkToro-class tooling has weak RU/CIS coverage — use TGStat and VK-native tooling there.

---

### D3 · Trigger events & intent signals
**What it is.** Turning a static list into a queue.

**Mechanics.** Public, timestamped admissions of a problem:
- **Hiring** for a role whose duties describe your problem — *a hire is an approved budget*
- Funding rounds · new executives · office moves · new locations
- Licences, certifications and their expiries · regulatory deadlines
- Tech-stack changes · tender publications · lawsuits · bad reviews

**Use when.** B2B, outbound, ABM — and any time timing beats volume.

**Output.** A trigger list with source, detection method, freshness window and outreach angle.

**Watch out.** Vendor-published conversion claims in this area are unverified (reliability 2 in the corpus) — the *mechanism* is sound, the percentages are not. Public triggers are visible to competitors too. Windows are usually weeks.

**Source.** Landbase, Bombora, Demandbase, HeyReach (vendor content — mechanism reliable, statistics not).

---

### D4 · Community conversation taxonomy
**What it is.** A reusable analytical lens for any community research.

**Mechanics.** Isolate four conversation types:
- **Pain Points** — complaints and struggles
- **Solution Requests** — "does anyone know a tool that…"
- **Money Talk** — pricing, budgets, what people paid
- **Hot Discussions** — high-engagement threads

**Use when.** Reddit, Telegram, Discord, Slack, forums — any conversation surface.

**Output.** Threads sorted by type, with a repeat-question log carrying dates (frequency evidence).

**Watch out.** A question asked once is curiosity. Asked monthly for two years, it is a market with no supplier. **GummySearch, long the standard tool here, is reported shutting down** — verify before recommending it or any successor.

**Source.** Practitioner community-led-growth sources; GummySearch product framing.

---

### D5 · Ad-library archaeology
**What it is.** Reading competitors' validated audience bets for free.

**Mechanics.** Open the ad library. **Sort by start date. Ads running 60–90+ days are almost certainly profitable** — nobody keeps paying for losers. Extract the angle, promise and implied audience from each long-runner. Note which segments they have *stopped* advertising to.

**Use when.** Competitive research; validating that a segment has workable paid economics.

**Output.** An angle inventory per competitor with run-length, and an implied-segment list.

**Watch out.** Longevity is a proxy, not proof. Meta's library is the well-documented one; Google Ads Transparency Center, TikTok Creative Center and RU equivalents are assumed by analogy and not verified in this corpus.

**Source.** Meta Ad Library; agency practitioner guides.

---

### D6 · Do Things That Don't Scale
**What it is.** The default first validation experiment for any segment.

**Mechanics.** Recruit the first users manually, one by one. Airbnb, Stripe and Pinterest all did. **The point is not the users — it is what manual recruiting teaches you that a funnel never will.**

**The test.** Can you personally reach 20 people in this segment and get 5 conversations? If not, the segment is unreachable — whatever the scoring matrix said.

**Use when.** Always, before any scalable channel.

**Output.** Reply rate, a log of reactions, a verbatim objection list.

**Watch out.** Zero replies from 30 well-researched messages is not a copy problem. It is a reachability verdict.

**Source.** Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013).

## E. Making the message land

---

### E1 · Positioning — the five components
**What it is.** The order that makes positioning derivable rather than invented.

**Mechanics.** Run in this sequence:
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what they would do without you (often a spreadsheet, an intern, or nothing)
2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that alternatives don't
3. **Differentiated value** — the "so what?"
4. **Best-fit customers** — *who cares a lot about that value*
5. **Market category** — the context that makes the value obvious

Dunford's definition: *"Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."*

**Use when.** After the segment is chosen; and as a check on whether it *should* be chosen (criterion 18).

**Output.** A positioning statement that fails the swap test when a competitor's name is substituted.

**Watch out.** The **phantom competitor** error: teams list rivals they never actually lose to, and miss the incumbent that beats them — inertia. Also: the sequence run backwards produces audience-flattery positioning that commits to nothing.

**Source.** April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*; "A Quickstart Guide to Positioning" (read in full).

---

### E2 · Message testing — the four dimensions
**What it is.** Validating a positioning hypothesis cheaply.

**Mechanics.** Test copy against the actual ICP on:
- **Clarity** — do they understand it?
- **Relevance** — does it connect to a real need?
- **Value** — does it feel worth something?
- **Differentiation** — does it say something competitors don't?

**Segment the test cohorts before running**, or you will generalise a one-segment win into a universal truth.

**Use when.** Two segments tie; or before committing budget to an angle.

**Output.** Per-segment reaction on all four dimensions, with verbatim confusion points.

**Watch out.** A/B tests tell you *which* won. Qualitative testing tells you *why* — and only the why transfers to the next asset.

**Source.** Wynter; CleverX.

---

### E3 · Smoke tests, fake doors and concierge MVPs
**What it is.** Manufacturing evidence when none exists.

**Mechanics — match the instrument to the unknown:**
| Unknown | Instrument |
|---|---|
| **Demand** — will anyone want this? | **Fake door** |
| **Market or message** — which segment, which angle? | **Smoke test** across segments/messages |
| **Delivery or value capture** — can we actually do it, and will they pay? | **Concierge MVP** |

Canonical examples: Buffer's "Plans and Pricing" button leading to an email signup; Tesla taking $5,000 deposits before the first car existed.

**Use when.** Choosing between segments, or validating one before investment.

**Output.** Per-segment response rate on **one costly action** — email, call booked, deposit — never a click, with traffic source held constant.

**Watch out.** Ethical floor: **never take money or personal data you cannot honour.** A fake door that collects emails must say what happens next. Also, traffic quality dominates results — a bad channel will fail a good segment.

**Source.** Learning Loop; Evelance; dowhatmatter guides.

---

### E4 · Review mining
**What it is.** The highest-yield zero-cost research method.

**Mechanics.**
1. Start with 10–15 recent reviews across the rating range.
2. **Concentrate on 3- and 4-star reviews** — they bought, they're honest, they're not venting.
3. Count theme frequency — turns subjective complaints into an ordered list.
4. Capture verbatim language (this is your copy).
5. **Flag reviews written by someone who is clearly not the intended customer** — that is an entire unaddressed segment describing itself.

**Surfaces.** G2 / Capterra / TrustRadius (B2B software) · Amazon and marketplaces (consumer) · app stores (mobile) · Otzovik, Yandex Maps, marketplace reviews (RU).

**Use when.** Always. First method when there is no access to buyers.

**Output.** Theme frequency table · verbatim quote bank · "wrong customer" list.

**Watch out.** Reviews over-represent extremes. Incentivised reviews distort B2B software sites. The goal is not to read every review — it is to find the handful of themes that repeat across hundreds.

**Source.** Why We Buy; Invesp; practitioner guides.

---

### E5 · Search intent classification
**What it is.** Reading demand and awareness from queries.

**Mechanics.** Separate queries into **problem-phrased · solution-phrased · brand-phrased**. The ratio between them is the market's awareness distribution.
**Intent tell:** the SERP itself carries ads and shopping features — advertisers have already validated that the query converts. Commercial intent beats volume.

**Use when.** Establishing that demand exists at all, and at what awareness stage.

**Output.** Query table with volume, intent class, awareness stage, seasonality, region.

**Watch out.** Blind to new markets (zero volume is expected, see A2) and to people who cannot name their problem. RU standard instruments are **Яндекс Wordstat** and **Google Trends** — free, and the evidence a RU client will accept.

**Source.** Ahrefs; Semrush; Backlinko; RU: Sberbank «Своё дело», Yandex Market partner blog.

---

### E6 · Comment mining — the stimulus map
**What it is.** Building a sourced avatar from comments rather than reviews.

**Why it is a separate instrument from review mining (E4).** Reviews are written by **buyers**, retrospectively, in response to a form, *about the product*. Comments are written by buyers, near-buyers, **refusers** and lurkers, in the moment, unprompted, and are effectively *about the commenter*. Comments are the only free source that contains **non-customers** — Blue Ocean Tiers 1 and 2 (A3) — which reviews structurally cannot reach.

**Mechanics — the stimulus map.** Every comment is a response to a known, timestamped stimulus, so the stimulus *selects* the commenter. Sample a portfolio of at least four types, never one:
your post · a competitor's post · a price post · a "how do I do X" thread · a contrarian take · **a 60+ day ad's live comments** · a churn/complaint post · a "what do you use for X" thread · an explainer · a case study · marketplace pre-purchase questions.

**Governing rule.** No avatar claim may rest on a single stimulus type. A trait found only under a price post is a trait of *people who comment on price posts*.

**Three distinctive moves:**
- **The awareness tell** — comment phrasing reveals the Hunt/Schwartz stage directly ("а что, так можно было?" → unaware; "чем лучше, чем X?" → solution-aware). Count the mix; it decides channel before anything else does.
- **The contradiction scan** — "too expensive" beside "cheap for what it does" under the same stimulus is **a segmentation boundary, not noise.** Split; never average. Averaging is the milkshake error (B1).
- **Meta-signals** — likes on a comment are people holding that view without writing it; silence under a high-view post is negative evidence.

**Use when.** Any avatar or persona request · no access to buyers · zero budget and days, not weeks · you specifically need refusers.

**Output.** Tagged verbatim rows → clusters → `../templates/avatar-card-template.md`, every field citation-backed.

**Watch out.** **Comment data can never support a prevalence claim** ("40% of our audience…"). Commenters are a vocal, self-selected minority skewed toward the emotionally activated. Read sorted by *new*, not only by *top* — top is ranked for entertainment. And comments are a **recruiting pool**: refusers and workaround-builders found here are the highest-priority interview candidates you will get for free.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/avatar-from-comments.md`.

**Source.** RU practice on hearing real words «без маркетингового фильтра»; TikTok Ads Manager *Comment Insights* (first-party) for own-ad comment analysis; social-persona practitioner guidance. See `source-map.md` §6.7.

---

### E7 · Voice of Customer — the seven language types
**What it is.** Capturing what buyers say in the words they actually use, and refusing to translate it into marketing language.

**The paraphrase decay chain** — each step feels like tidying and deletes something unrecoverable:

| Step | Text | Deleted |
|---|---|---|
| Raw | «Я заебался каждый вечер переносить заявки из Telegram в CRM» | — |
| Cleaned | «Устал каждый вечер переносить заявки…» | **Intensity** — and with it, half the urgency |
| Third person | «Тратит время на ручной перенос заявок» | First person, frequency, both named systems |
| Professionalised | «Неэффективный процесс обработки лидов» | The human being |
| Strategic | «Клиенты хотят автоматизировать бизнес» | **Everything.** Fits any company, describes nobody |

Most audience research is delivered at the last step.

**The seven types**, harvested separately because they power different assets:

| Type | Powers | Marker to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Hooks, headlines, cold-outreach openers | First person + time marker + named system |
| **Desired outcome** | Value props, promise lines | *Their* metric, not yours |
| **Objection** | FAQ, risk reversal, guarantees | Conditionals: "if only…", «а вдруг…» |
| **Comparison** | Comparison pages, battlecards | The dimension named, and the tiebreaker |
| **Trigger** | Timing, targeting, outbound hooks | "после того как…", "since we…" |
| **Emotion** | Tone, creative direction | Profanity, hyperbole, caps, self-deprecation |
| **Decision criteria** | Sales narrative, proof, roadmap | Hard constraints vs. preferences |

**The intensity rule.** Emotional intensity is a **proxy for urgency and therefore for willingness to pay.** Sanitise it and you systematically underestimate what a segment will pay.

**The four competitor patterns — free customer interviews someone else already ran:**
*"I bought X because…"* → trigger + winning criterion · ***"I switched from X to Y because…"*** → a completed four-forces case, the most valuable of the four · *"I like X, but…"* → unmet need inside a satisfied customer · *"I would use X if…"* → the exact barrier blocking a willing buyer.

**The reasoning chain, enforced in writing:**
```
DATA → OBSERVATION → INSIGHT → HYPOTHESIS
```
never `DATA → confident assumption`. Each stage gets its own column. If you cannot write the observation, you do not have the insight.

**The highest-yield B2B question:** *"What metric is this person measured on?"* — it predicts behaviour better than title, company size or stated preference, because it determines what they can defend internally.

**Watch out.** Never translate the swipe file; store every quote in its original language. Formulate copy natively, never by translating a message built in another language.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/voice-of-customer.md`.
**Source.** RU practitioner sources on hearing real words «без маркетингового фильтра»; Wynter buyer intelligence; CXL/ResearchXL multi-method discipline (the four-stage chain itself is `[S]` synthesis — see `source-map.md` §9.8).

## F. RU-canonical frameworks

### F1 · 5W (Марк Шеррингтон)
**Mechanics.** Five questions, one row per segment, held in a matrix: **What · Who · Why · When · Where.**

**Use when.** RU engagements — clients expect it by name.

**Watch out.** Only *Why* carries real information. Used alone, 5W produces demographic wallpaper. **Use it as an output-formatting convention, not as a discovery method.**

**Source.** Mark Sherrington (Added Value), via RU marketing media.

---

### F2 · Khramatrix
**Mechanics.** Extends 5W with four more columns: geography and demographics · behavioural specifics · **stage of purchase readiness** · **the target action** you want them to take.

**Use when.** RU engagements needing more than 5W.

**Why it's worth borrowing.** The last two columns force a segment definition to terminate in something *actionable* — which is exactly what 5W lacks.

**Source.** RU marketing education media.

---

### F3 · Карта эмпатии (empathy map)
**Mechanics.** Visualises what the customer thinks, feels, sees, hears, says and does — before, during and after the interaction.

**Use when.** Creative and content work, after the segment is defined by harder criteria.

**Watch out.** Entirely built on empathy, which means entirely built on inference. Never let it substitute for evidence of buying behaviour.

**Source.** RU marketing education media.

---

### F4 · The RU zero-budget research stack
**Mechanics.** Four substitute sources when interviews are impossible:
1. **Inbound client messages** — chats, comments, support tickets
2. **Forums and communities** — Reddit, Pikabu, topical Telegram groups
3. **Reviews** — of your own and competitors' products (marketplaces, Google, Otzovik)
4. **Competitors' content and its comments** — plus Google Trends and Wordstat

**Method.** Record client wording **verbatim and unedited** → find repeating problems → watch which posts collect most comments → log recurring objections → use their words in content and pre-empt the objections.

**Volume floor for a first pass:** ~20–30 reviews + ~10 call recordings + ~10 search queries + a set of forum and social comments.

**Watch out.** The one documented failure mode is substituting your own assumptions for "the client's actual words." That is the failure this whole skill guards against.

**Source.** vc.ru (read in full); Texterra; Деловая среда.

### F5 · RU habitat & free-acquisition tactics
**Mechanics.**
- **TGStat** — the working Telegram catalogue with per-channel statistics.
- **Взаимопиар** — cross-promotion between comparable channels.
- **«Перелив»** — pulling audience from vc.ru, Dzen, VK, YouTube Shorts and Reels into a Telegram channel.
- **VK people-search filters** — free audience-size estimation by parameter.
- **The transposition trick** — to profile a local audience you cannot yet reach, study the social-media community of a comparable business **in another district or city**.

**Watch out.** Paid channel placements are a grey market with inflated metrics. A big channel with dead comments is a broadcast, not a community — check comment activity, not subscriber count.

**Source.** Habr; SMMplanner; vc.ru; Unisender; inSales.

### E8 · Laddering — surface complaint to root pain
**What it is.** Getting from what they said to what actually drives the purchase.

```
COMPLAINT      "Your export is slow"
   ↓ why does that matter to you?
FRICTION       "I wait around instead of finishing"
   ↓
CONSEQUENCE    "I stay late on Thursdays"
   ↓
ROOT PAIN      "I promised my family I'd stop doing this"
```

**Only the bottom layer explains the purchase. The top explains a support ticket.**

**Method.** Means-End Chain theory: features → benefits → motivational and emotional criteria. Ask *"why does that matter to you?"*, not bare *"why?"* — the latter reads as a challenge.

**Two stopping rules, and both matter:** too shallow = the answer is still about the product · too deep = a life theme, true of everyone, triggers no purchase. **The right depth is one rung above the life theme** — the same altitude the abstraction test (B1) lands on.

**The branching rule.** The 5 Whys is a root-cause tool for *defects* and assumes one mechanical cause. **Motivation branches.** If ten people ladder from one complaint to three roots, **that is three segments**, not noise to average.

**Output.** Middle rungs → content. Root → positioning.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/pain-discovery.md` §3.

### E9 · EPPM — the ethics and the effectiveness of pain messaging
**What it is.** Kim Witte's Extended Parallel Process Model. The best-evidenced answer to *how hard can you press on a pain*.

```
THREAT   = severity × susceptibility
EFFICACY = response efficacy ("the fix works") × self-efficacy ("I can do it")
```

| Combination | Result |
|---|---|
| High threat + **high** efficacy | **Danger control** — acts on the problem. Message accepted |
| High threat + **low** efficacy | **Fear control** — acts on the fear. Defensive avoidance, denial, reactance. **Message rejected** |

> *"Most fear campaigns fail not because fear is the wrong lever but because they crank threat without lifting efficacy."*

**Why this is the strongest rule in the module.** It makes the ethical line and the effectiveness line **the same line**. Amplifying pain without offering credible, achievable relief is not just manipulative — it is **predicted to fail**. That removes the usual ethics-versus-conversion trade-off.

**The operating rule:** **never raise threat without raising efficacy in the same breath.**

**And it fails on layout, not only intent** `[S]` — a truthful page whose relief sits below the fold is fear control regardless of the writer's good faith.

**The neglected half:** teams supply *response* efficacy ("our product works") and skip *self* efficacy ("and **you** can do this"). EPPM weights them equally.

**Autonomy cross-check — three conditions:** manipulation *"pushes a customer toward a decision they might not choose if they had **full information, emotional space, and a clear exit**."* All three preserved → persuasion.

**Watch out.** Developed for health communication. Transfer to commercial marketing is an extension — mechanism sound, magnitudes untested in this domain.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/pain-discovery.md` §8. **Source.** `source-map.md` §12.1–12.2.

### E10 - Frequency is not importance
**What it is.** The correction that invalidates how most pain ranking actually works.

Griffin & Hauser tested the hypothesis everyone quietly assumes - that customers mention their most important needs most often, so counting mentions could substitute for measuring importance.

> **"The data do not support this hypothesis - high-priority needs do not seem to be mentioned more often than low-priority needs."** `[E - Marketing Science 12(1), 1993]`

```
100 people say:  "The interface is ugly."
 10 people say:  "This bug costs us $10k every month."
```
**Counting ranks the first higher. It is the less interesting problem by an order of magnitude.**

**The rule.** Measure and report **prevalence** and **severity** separately, always. Prevalence is countable and cheap. Importance must be **asked for** - ranked or traded off by the people who have the pain. It cannot be inferred from counting.

**Also from the same paper:** ~20-30 interviews surface ~90% of a category's needs, and **two analysts should code the transcripts - one finds only about half.**

**Watch out.** Every "top 10 pain points" list built by tallying reviews, comments or search volume is measuring prevalence and calling it importance. **This is the default behaviour of an LLM asked to find pains.**

**Full method:** `../knowledge/pain-opportunity.md` section 3.

### E11 - Two pain scores, and the behavioural rung
**What it is.** Pain is scored twice because *how much does this hurt* and *can a business be built on it* are different questions.

| | Weighted on |
|---|---|
| **Pain Strength (PSS)** | severity, urgency, frequency, **cost of inaction**, persistence, emotional intensity, evidence quality |
| **Commercial Opportunity (COS)** | WTP, ability to pay, economic value, **prevalence**, existing spend, dissatisfaction with alternatives, reachability, product fit, evidence quality |

**The 2x2 is the point.** The *high pain / low commercial* quadrant - **the Charity** - is real suffering with no budget, and it is the trap that most resembles a discovery. A blended score hides it completely.

**The behavioural rung - what they DID, not what they said:**

`B1 complain -> B2 search -> B3 workaround -> B4 time -> B5 switch -> B6 pay -> B7 hire -> B8 budget -> B9 market`

> **The gap between B1 and B3 is where fictional pains die.** People complain freely about things they have never once tried to fix. **If no workaround exists, ask why not** - usually because it isn't worth the effort, which means it isn't worth paying for.

**Cost of inaction is mandatory** and must show its arithmetic: `3 people x 4 hrs/wk x 52 = 624 person-hours/yr`. Every multiplier sourced. An unsourced multiplier makes an authoritative number out of a guess.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/pain-opportunity.md` sections 1, 2, 4.

### E12 - Concentration, threshold, evolution
**Three questions that turn pain research into segmentation.**

| Question | Yields |
|---|---|
| **Concentration** - where does this become 5-10x worse? | A segment defined by circumstance, with higher WTP, that competitors serving the flat market don't address |
| **Threshold** - where does the old solution stop scaling? | An ICP boundary **with a trigger built in** - crossing it *is* the buying event |
| **Evolution** - which maturity stage is this? | Five segments wearing one company name; the expansion path or the ceiling |

**Threshold confirmation test:** people **below** the line should report the same situation as tolerable. **If they also call it a problem, it's a different pain, not a threshold.**

**The feedback rule from the Pain x Segment matrix:** if one pain behaves completely differently across two segments, they were correctly separated. **If every pain scores identically, they are one segment.** A segmentation test derived from pain data.

**Full method:** `../knowledge/pain-opportunity.md` sections 5-8. Generators C18-C20 in `../knowledge/segment-generation-lenses.md`.

## G. The Evidence Ladder — the protocol under everything

**Not a framework — the rule that governs every claim this skill makes about a client's audience.**

**The generating principle:** evidence strength equals **what it cost the person to produce it.** An opinion costs nothing; a search costs intent; a self-built workaround costs hours; a ₽150k/month job posting costs an approved budget; a purchase costs money.

| Level | Meaning | Licenses |
|---|---|---|
| **L0** | Speculation — model or founder asserted it | Nothing. **A defect in a delivered report** |
| **L1** | Plausible — mechanism sound, unobserved | A hypothesis worth testing |
| **L2** | Observed — one real artefact | A research direction |
| **L3** | Repeated — 3+ instances, same source type | A working assumption |
| **L4** | **Triangulated — 3+ *independent* source types** | **A strategy decision** |
| **L5** | Behavioural — spending time or effort | Channel investment, building |
| **L6** | Transactional — money already moved | Full commitment |

**The counting rule:** 1 source = hypothesis · 2 = weak evidence · **3+ independent = validated.**

**Independence** requires differing on at least two of: **source type · population · mechanism** (stated / behavioural / transactional). Three review sites are one type. Five articles citing one study are one source. Survey + interview + poll are all stated preference — they agree with each other and are wrong together.

**The cardinal sin:** laundering `L0` into `L2` by rewriting it more confidently. Fluency is not evidence.

**Note the asymmetry:** `L3 → L4` is the highest-value single move available and usually costs a few hours — add one *different kind* of source, ideally behavioural. It is also the step most often skipped, because `L3` already feels convincing.

**Feeds the score.** The `E` variable in the TAS formula is the mean level across a segment's load-bearing claims. This is what stops the matrix from converting speculation into a confident number.

**Full protocol:** `../knowledge/evidence-ladder.md`.
