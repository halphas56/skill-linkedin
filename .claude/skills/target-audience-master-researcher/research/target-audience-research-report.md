# Target Audience Research Report

**How to find the audience that will actually pay, stay, and spread — including the ones nobody else is looking at.**

**Compiled:** 2026-08-25 · **Corpus:** `source-map.md` · **Condensed frameworks:** `frameworks.md` · **Worked cases:** `examples.md`

---

## Scope and method

This report exists to be the evidence layer under a reusable skill (`../SKILL.md`). It is written to be *used during a live engagement*, not admired.

**What was done:** roughly thirty targeted searches in English and Russian across market selection, JTBD, positioning, PMF measurement, channel frameworks, research methods, pricing research, and Russian-market practice; seven sources retrieved and read in full; four retrieval failures logged; the rest reviewed at search-extract level. Full accounting, including reliability scores and the four blocked URLs, is in `source-map.md`.

**What was not done:** no exhaustive literature survey, no proprietary databases, no primary research with real buyers, no verification of vendor-published conversion statistics. This is a representative corpus, deliberately diverse, with counter-evidence included.

### Evidence marking

Every substantive claim carries one of three markers. This convention is load-bearing — the skill inherits it, and it is the difference between a research asset and a confident-sounding blog post.

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[E]** | **Evidence.** Traceable to a named source in `source-map.md`. |
| **[S]** | **Synthesis.** A conclusion drawn across sources, or from accumulated practice, that no single source states. Defensible, but not citable. |
| **[A]** | **Assumption.** Reasoning by analogy where the corpus is thin. Flagged so it can be attacked. |

Nothing here carries invented statistics. Where the corpus offered a number, it appears with its source and its caveat. Where it offered none — most notably conversion benchmarks — none is supplied. **A fabricated benchmark is worse than no benchmark, because it terminates thinking.** [S]

## §0 — Four laws that generate most of the rest

Before the taxonomies, the four propositions everything else hangs from. If the rest of this report were lost, these would reconstruct most of it. [S]

### Law 1 — The market outranks everything you can control

Hormozi states the hierarchy plainly: **Market > Offer > Persuasion.** *Great* at a higher level overpowers everything below it; *bad* at a higher level stops the chain. [E — Hormozi, §1.5] Balfour arrives at the same conclusion from the growth side: there are terrible products above $1B and excellent products that never went anywhere. [E — Balfour, §4.3]

The practical consequence: **effort spent choosing the audience has a higher ceiling than effort spent improving copy, design, or features.** This is why an audience-research engagement is worth days rather than an afternoon.

### Law 2 — Demand is created by a struggling moment, not by a product

"It's a struggling moment that creates demand." [E — Moesta, §2.3] People do not buy because you exist. They buy because something in their situation became intolerable, and they went looking.

Therefore an audience is not a group of *people*. **An audience is a group of people in a circumstance.** [S] "Marketing directors at mid-market SaaS companies" is not an audience. "Marketing directors who just inherited a channel they cannot report on, three weeks before a board meeting" is. The first is a list. The second is demand.

This law is why every good audience definition contains a *when*.

### Law 3 — Value comes first, audience second

Dunford's sequence is: competitive alternatives → unique attributes → **differentiated value** → best-fit customers → category. Best-fit customers are defined as "customers that really care a lot about your unique value." [E — Dunford, §3.1]

The direction matters enormously and is almost always run backwards in practice. Teams pick an appealing audience and then try to manufacture relevance for it. The correct move is to establish what you are genuinely better at, then ask **who is structurally forced to care about exactly that.** [S]

Corollary: if you are not better at anything, no audience research will save you. It will only tell you which crowd will notice fastest.

### Law 4 — The channel is not yours, and it selects the audience for you

**"Products are built to fit with channels. Channels do not mold to products."** [E — Balfour, §4.3] You do not set Google's ranking rules, Meta's delivery rules, Gmail's spam rules, or Telegram's discovery mechanics.

Therefore an audience you cannot reach through a channel your business model can afford **is not an audience — it is a fantasy with demographics attached.** [S] This is why "reachability" and "audience-channel fit" carry heavy weight in the scoring matrix, and why a beautiful segment with no habitat loses to an adequate segment with a mailing list.

### The counterweight — when narrow targeting is the wrong advice

Honesty requires stating the boundary. Ehrenberg-Bass research (Sharp, *How Brands Grow*) finds that in established consumer categories, brands grow chiefly by **reaching more category buyers, especially light buyers** — and that rival brands' customer bases are statistically near-interchangeable. [E — Sharp, §3.4]

Both things are true, in different regimes: [S]

| Regime | Dominant logic | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Startup, new offer, service business, expert, challenger with no distribution | **Narrow ruthlessly.** Beachhead, best-fit, one segment. | You cannot afford breadth. Word of mouth needs density. Positioning needs contrast. |
| Established brand, mass consumer category, physical availability already solved | **Broaden reach; build mental availability.** | Growth comes from penetration among light buyers, not loyalty among heavy ones. |
| Anything in between | Narrow for *acquisition mechanics*, broad for *brand assets*. | Sharp targeting of the buying moment; consistent brand recognition across the category. |

**The skill must name its regime before it recommends.** Handing a mass-market brand a beachhead strategy — or handing a bootstrapped consultant a "reach all category buyers" strategy — are symmetrical failures.

## §1 — What makes a target audience "good"

Twenty-three factors. They are not equal, and they are not independent. The first eight are near-veto conditions; the rest modulate.

The compressed version, for when there is no time: **massive pain · purchasing power · easy to target · growing.** [E — Hormozi, §1.5] Everything below refines those four.

---

### 1.1 Clear painful problem

**Why it matters.** Money moves toward relief, not toward improvement. Without an articulated problem, you are selling an upgrade to a person who does not know they are broken — the most expensive sale in commerce. [S]

**How to detect it.** Look for the problem being *named by the audience in their own words*, unprompted, in public. Reviews, forum threads, support tickets, search queries. The test is whether they have vocabulary for it. [E — vc.ru §8.4; review mining §6.1]

**Good looks like.** Recurring threads with the same complaint in different words. People describing consequences, not preferences. 3- and 4-star reviews that praise the product and then describe exactly what still hurts. [E — §6.1]

**Bad looks like.** You have to explain the problem before you can sell the solution. Nobody searches for it. Competitors' marketing is all education, no offer.

**Questions to ask.** "When did this last happen to you? Walk me through it." "What did it cost you?" "What did you do instead?" Never "would you like a tool that…". [E — Mom Test, §2.4]

**Signals & evidence.** Existing spend on workarounds. Manual processes with a named owner. Spreadsheets with a person's name on them. A dedicated forum thread with hundreds of replies.

**Example.** A "team collaboration" pain is vague. "I lost the client file version my colleague edited on Friday and re-did four hours of work" is a problem with a bill attached.

---

### 1.2 High urgency

**Why it matters.** Urgency is the difference between a pipeline and a graveyard. Non-urgent pain produces enthusiastic conversations and no purchase orders. [S]

**How to detect it.** Four forces: **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit.** Urgency is the magnitude of Push. Look for deadlines, penalties, external pressure, and events with dates. [E — Moesta, §2.3]

**Good looks like.** A date exists. Something bad happens if nothing is done by then — an audit, a launch, a season, a contract renewal, a regulatory deadline, a board meeting, a court date, a wedding.

**Bad looks like.** "Someday." "When we have time." "In next year's budget." Interest without a clock.

**Questions to ask.** "What happens if you do nothing for six months?" If the honest answer is "nothing much," you have found an interested audience, not a buying one.

**Signals & evidence.** Search queries containing *fast*, *urgent*, *deadline*, *срочно*, *сегодня*, *за 1 день*. Competitors advertising speed. Premium pricing tolerated for turnaround.

**Example.** Accounting software sells year-round; it sells *hardest* in the weeks before a filing deadline. The deadline is the audience's real segmentation axis.

---

### 1.3 Ability to pay

**Why it matters.** Pain without budget produces the most demoralising funnel in business: high engagement, high gratitude, no revenue. [S] Hormozi lists purchasing power as one of four market criteria. [E — §1.5]

**How to detect it.** Look at money the segment *already moves* for adjacent things — not at income statistics. Aulet's version is blunter: "Is the target customer well-funded?" [E — §1.1]

**Good looks like.** Existing line items. Existing vendors being paid. Funding, revenue, or discretionary spend visible from outside. In B2B: they employ someone whose salary is larger than your price.

**Bad looks like.** Segments whose defining feature is that they cannot afford the incumbent. Students, pre-revenue founders, and hobbyists are the classic traps — real pain, real enthusiasm, no budget.

**Questions to ask.** "What do you currently spend on this, including people's time?" "Whose budget would this come from?" "What did you buy most recently in this area?"

**Signals & evidence.** Job postings (a hire is a budget). [E — §5.5] Funding announcements. Existing paid tools in the stack. Price points that competitors sustain.

**Example.** "Freelance designers" have pain around contracts and late payment. "Design agencies with 5–20 staff" have the same pain *and* a bookkeeper, a bank line, and a per-seat software budget.

---

### 1.4 Willingness to pay

**Why it matters.** Ability and willingness are different variables, and confusing them is one of the most expensive errors in segment selection. Enterprises with millions in budget refuse to pay $50 for a category they have decided should be free. [S]

**How to detect it.** Van Westendorp's four questions establish an acceptable price corridor per segment — too cheap / cheap / expensive / too expensive. [E — §7.1] But it is stated preference; triangulate against behaviour.

**Good looks like.** The segment already pays for something in this category, at some price, without drama. Norms of payment exist.

**Bad looks like.** A strong free-culture norm (developers paying for tooling personally; consumers paying for content). Procurement processes that cost more than your price. "We'd build that internally."

**Questions to ask.** "What have you paid for to solve this?" "What would make this an easy yes at 3× the price?" "Who has to approve a purchase this size?"

**Signals & evidence.** Competitors' public pricing that has survived years. Paid alternatives with visible customers. Marketplace transactions.

**Example.** Photographers will pay four figures for a lens and resist $9/month for backup software. Ability is identical; willingness is category-bound.

---

### 1.5 Frequency of need

**Why it matters.** Frequency drives everything downstream: retention, referral, content demand, and how long you can afford to wait for payback. [S]

**How to detect it.** Ask how many times the triggering circumstance occurred in the last twelve months.

**Good looks like.** Monthly or weekly recurrence, or a permanent condition that must be continuously managed (compliance, security, bookkeeping, hiring).

**Bad looks like.** Genuinely once-per-lifetime needs (visas, weddings, probate). These can be excellent businesses, but they can never build retention — they must build *referral and search* instead, and the strategy differs completely.

**Questions to ask.** "How often does this come up?" "Is this a project or a permanent job?"

**Signals & evidence.** Seasonality curves in Wordstat / Google Trends. Subscription vs. one-off pricing in the category. [E — §8.7]

**Example.** Wedding photography and payroll processing can both be great businesses. Only one can be sold on retention. Treating a once-in-a-lifetime audience as a subscription audience is a strategy error, not a tactic error.

---

### 1.6 Easy reachability

**Why it matters.** Hormozi's "easy to target" criterion. [E — §1.5] Aulet's "readily accessible to your sales force." [E — §1.1] Balfour's channel law makes it structural. [E — §4.3] It is the most under-weighted criterion in amateur audience work and the one that kills the most plans. [S]

**How to detect it.** Ask the list question: **"Where is the list?"** Can you name a place where 500 of them are already gathered, subscribed, indexed, or licensed?

**Good looks like.** A professional association. A licensing register. A conference attendee list. A subreddit. Three Telegram channels. A job board. A directory. A marketplace category. An ad platform with a targeting parameter that maps to this segment.

**Bad looks like.** A segment defined by an internal psychological state with no external marker. "Ambitious people who value quality" cannot be targeted, because no platform has a checkbox for it and no list exists.

**Questions to ask.** "If I had to reach 100 of these people by Friday, exactly where would I go?" If there is no answer, the segment fails regardless of its other scores.

**Signals & evidence.** Existence of a trade publication. Existence of a conference. Existence of a certification. Ad-platform targeting options. A searchable public register.

**Example.** "Small business owners" — unreachable as stated. "Owners of dental practices in Kazan" — a licensing register, an association, two Telegram chats, a supplier list, and a trade show.

---

### 1.7 Concentration in identifiable places

**Why it matters.** Distinct from reachability. Reachability is *can you get to them*; concentration is *are they gathered densely enough for word of mouth and content to compound*. Aulet requires "strong word of mouth between end users" as a defining property of a real beachhead. [E — §1.1]

**How to detect it.** Do they know each other? Do they read the same three things? Do they attend the same event?

**Good looks like.** A community with a name. A conference everyone attends. A person everyone follows. Referral loops that already exist without you.

**Bad looks like.** A statistically real but socially non-existent segment — people who share an attribute but never meet, never talk, and have no shared media.

**Questions to ask.** "Who else has this problem that you know personally?" "Where do people like you talk shop?"

**Signals & evidence.** Named communities. Recurring events. Micro-influencers whose whole audience is this segment.

**Example.** Veterinary clinic owners: dense, they all know each other, they all attend the same two conferences. Owners of small warehouses: real, numerous, and socially scattered — same reachability, far worse compounding.

---

### 1.8 Strong emotional or rational motivation

**Why it matters.** The motivation type determines the entire message architecture, the proof required, and the sales cycle. Getting it wrong produces technically accurate marketing that moves nobody. [S]

**How to detect it.** Listen for what they say when they are not being professional. Emotional drivers (fear, status, guilt, relief, belonging, being seen as competent) hide behind rational justifications (ROI, efficiency, compliance).

**Good looks like.** Both present, and you know which is the engine and which is the permission slip. In B2B the emotional driver is usually *career risk*; the rational driver is the business case that lets them defend the decision.

**Bad looks like.** Motivation that is purely abstract ("it's better practice") with no personal stake.

**Questions to ask.** "What happens to *you* personally if this goes wrong?" "How would you explain this purchase to your boss / your spouse?"

**Signals & evidence.** Verbatim language intensity in reviews. Anonymous forum posts (where the emotional layer surfaces). What people complain about at 11pm.

**Example.** Nobody buys penetration testing because they love security. They buy it because a client's procurement questionnaire asked for a report and their deal is frozen.

---

### 1.9 Clear trigger events

**Why it matters.** Triggers convert a static audience into a *timed* audience — the difference between broadcasting and arriving at the right moment. [S] The Push force in JTBD terms. [E — §2.3]

**How to detect it.** Reconstruct the timeline backwards from a purchase: what happened first? Switch interviews are built for exactly this. [E — §2.3]

**Good looks like.** Externally observable, timestamped events: a funding round, a new executive, a new office, a regulation, a failed audit, a bad review, a lost client, a move, a birth, a lease renewal, a hire.

**Bad looks like.** No identifiable entry point. Demand that appears to arise spontaneously (usually meaning you have not found the trigger yet, not that it does not exist).

**Questions to ask.** "What happened in the week before you started looking?"

**Signals & evidence.** Job postings for a related role. [E — §5.5] Public announcements. Regulatory calendars. Seasonal spikes.

**Example.** A company posting a "first Marketing Manager" vacancy has just admitted, publicly and with a date, that marketing is now a budgeted problem.

---

### 1.10 Existing search behaviour

**Why it matters.** Search is the cheapest proof that a problem-aware or solution-aware audience exists at scale, and it is the only demand signal that is both free and quantitative. [E — §6.4, §8.7]

**How to detect it.** Keyword tools in EN (Ahrefs, Semrush), Wordstat and Google Trends in RU. [E — §8.7] Read the *phrasing*, not just the volume — the phrasing is your copy.

**Good looks like.** Meaningful volume on problem-phrased and solution-phrased queries. Commercial-intent modifiers. **The SERP carries ads** — advertisers have already validated that the query converts. [E — §6.4]

**Bad looks like.** Zero volume. But interpret carefully: for a *new market* in Blank's sense, zero volume is expected and correct. [E — §1.2] Zero volume in an *existing* market is a death sentence; zero volume in a new market is just an inapplicable instrument.

**Questions to ask.** "What did you type into Google?" — always ask this verbatim in interviews.

**Signals & evidence.** Ad presence on the SERP. Autocomplete suggestions. "People also ask." Related searches. Query modifiers indicating urgency or budget.

**Example.** "How to fire someone legally in Russia" carries far higher commercial intent than "HR best practices," despite lower volume. Intent beats volume. [E — §6.4]

---

### 1.11 Existing spending behaviour

**Why it matters.** The single most reliable predictor of future spending is past spending in the same category. Everything else is inference. [S]

**How to detect it.** Find the budget line that already exists and ask what it is currently being spent on.

**Good looks like.** They already pay someone — an agency, a freelancer, an incumbent tool, an in-house hire. Your job is displacement, which is far easier than creation.

**Bad looks like.** The category is entirely new to them and the money would have to come from nowhere. This is not fatal, but it means you are selling a *budget*, not a product, and the sales cycle triples.

**Questions to ask.** "What's the last thing you bought to fix this?" "What's the annual number?"

**Signals & evidence.** Sustained competitor pricing. Long-running ads (60–90+ days implies profitable economics). [E — §5.4] Vendor lists in public procurement. Tool logos on their website.

**Example.** A firm paying an agency ₽150k/month for something has proven both ability and willingness. A firm doing it in-house on a spreadsheet has proven neither.

---

### 1.12 Existing alternatives

**Why it matters.** Dunford starts positioning here, not with competitors — because the real alternative is often "a spreadsheet," "an intern," or "nothing." [E — §3.1] The alternative defines the comparison the buyer will actually make.

**How to detect it.** Ask what they would do if you vanished tomorrow. The answer is your true competitive set.

**Good looks like.** A clearly named alternative that is visibly inadequate. Best of all: **they built their own workaround** — this is a lead-user signal and a purchase intent signal simultaneously. [E — §2.5]

**Bad looks like.** A phantom competitive set. Teams list rivals they never actually lose to, and miss the incumbent that actually beats them: inertia. [E — §3.1]

**Questions to ask.** "What are you doing about this right now?" "What did you consider and reject?" "Who else did you talk to?"

**Signals & evidence.** Screenshots of home-built spreadsheets. Zapier chains. Templates being sold on marketplaces. "How do you handle X?" threads.

**Example.** The competitor to a client-portal SaaS is almost never another client portal. It is a shared Google Drive folder and an unread email.

### 1.13 Dissatisfaction with current alternatives

**Why it matters.** Existing alternatives prove the market; *dissatisfaction* proves the opening. Together they define a switchable audience. [S] Ulwick formalises this as the importance-minus-satisfaction gap. [E — §2.2]

**How to detect it.** Review mining, aimed at 3- and 4-star reviews — buyers who are honest rather than venting. [E — §6.1] Also Blue Ocean's Tier 1 "soon-to-be" noncustomers: minimal buyers who are mentally already gone. [E — §1.3]

**Good looks like.** Consistent complaints about the same dimension across many providers — meaning the whole category is underserving something.

**Bad looks like.** Universal satisfaction (an overserved market — you should be attacking from below on price and simplicity instead). [E — §2.2] Or complaints that are purely about price, which usually means the category is commoditised.

**Questions to ask.** "What do you wish it did that it doesn't?" "What made you nearly cancel?"

**Signals & evidence.** Review frequency counts by theme. Churn reasons. Competitor support forums. G2 / Capterra comparison pages.

**Example.** If forty reviews across six products all complain about onboarding, onboarding is not a feature. It is the segment.

### 1.14 Low education cost

**Why it matters.** Education cost is the hidden CAC multiplier. Schwartz's awareness stages are effectively a cost ladder — unaware audiences are the most expensive to acquire and the least suited to free acquisition. [E — §3.2] Hunt's ladder makes the same point in RU terms: on the first four rungs the customer needs *information only*; the purchase happens at rung five. [E — §8.2]

**How to detect it.** Place the segment on the awareness ladder honestly. Then count how many rungs you must fund.

**Good looks like.** Solution-aware or product-aware: they already know solutions exist and are comparing. You need differentiation, not education.

**Bad looks like.** Unaware. You must first convince them a problem exists, then that solutions exist, then that yours is best. Three sales instead of one, at your expense.

**Questions to ask.** "Do you have a name for this problem?" "Have you looked for tools like this before?"

**Signals & evidence.** Search volume on solution-category terms (education already done by the market). Competitors doing heavy education marketing (a warning: it means the category pays for education). Presence of a category name.

**Example.** Selling CRM to a company already using a bad CRM: one rung. Selling CRM to a company keeping clients in a notebook: three rungs, and often a lost year.

---

### 1.15 Fast path to trust

**Why it matters.** Every audience has a *trust threshold* — the minimum credibility required before money moves. It varies by an order of magnitude across segments for the same price point. [S]

**How to detect it.** Ask what proof they would need. Then ask what proof they have actually accepted before.

**Good looks like.** Trust transfers through channels you can access: a peer's recommendation, a community reputation, a visible portfolio, a free artefact that demonstrates competence directly.

**Bad looks like.** Trust that requires certifications you do not have, procurement approval, insurance, references from their own industry, or a track record measured in years. Regulated and enterprise segments frequently fail here — not on pain or money, but on *time-to-credibility*.

**Questions to ask.** "What would you need to see before hiring someone for this?" "Who did you ask before you bought last time?"

**Signals & evidence.** Whether competitors lead with logos, certifications, or case studies. Whether the category has an accreditation. Length of the typical sales cycle.

**Example.** A designer can win a startup client with a portfolio link in a day, and needs eighteen months and a security review to win a bank. Same skill; different trust threshold; effectively different businesses.

---

### 1.16 Repeat purchase potential

**Why it matters.** Repeat purchase determines how much you can afford to spend to acquire one customer, which determines which channels are open to you. Channel-model fit runs on the ARPU↔CAC spectrum. [E — §4.3]

**How to detect it.** Is the need recurring (1.5), and does your solution stay in the workflow after the first success?

**Good looks like.** Subscription-natural. Consumable. Embedded in a recurring process. Expansion potential (more seats, more locations, more volume).

**Bad looks like.** One-and-done by nature, *and* no referral loop. That combination forces you into pure acquisition economics, which is the hardest game there is.

**Questions to ask.** "After this is solved, what's the next thing?" "Would you use it again next quarter?"

**Signals & evidence.** Category norms (does anyone sell this by subscription?). Contract lengths. Whether incumbents report net revenue retention.

**Example.** Tax filing repeats annually and forgives high CAC. Wedding planning does not repeat at all and must be funded by referral and search.

---

### 1.17 Referral potential

**Why it matters.** Referral is the only acquisition channel with negative marginal cost, and it is a *property of the audience*, not of your marketing. Aulet makes strong word of mouth a defining condition of a valid beachhead. [E — §1.1]

**How to detect it.** Does the segment talk to each other about this class of problem? Is there status in being the person who found the solution?

**Good looks like.** Professional communities where sharing tools is normal and reputationally rewarded. Segments where the problem is embarrassing to have but impressive to have solved.

**Bad looks like.** Competitive segments where clients hide their advantages from each other (trading, some agencies, some sales orgs). Also: problems too private to discuss.

**Questions to ask.** "Who did you tell after you found it?" "Would you introduce me to two people like you?" — the second question is a live test, not a hypothetical.

**Signals & evidence.** Existing "what tools do you use?" threads. Referral norms in the category. Whether the segment has a shared identity.

**Example.** Notion spread through communities that treat productivity systems as an identity and a shareable artefact. The referral loop was in the audience, not the feature set.

---

### 1.18 Community density

**Why it matters.** Density is what makes free acquisition arithmetically possible. In a dense segment, one good piece of content reaches a meaningful fraction of the entire market. In a sparse one, the same content reaches statistical noise. [S]

**How to detect it.** Count the places. If three venues cover most of the segment, it is dense.

**Good looks like.** A handful of named venues where most of the segment is present, plus a small set of people everyone follows. [E — SparkToro, §5.2] In RU/CIS this usually means Telegram channels and chats, discoverable via TGStat. [E — §8.6]

**Bad looks like.** Fragmentation across hundreds of tiny venues with no overlap, or a segment that consumes only mass media.

**Questions to ask.** "What do you read every week?" "Which chats are you in?" "Whose posts do you never skip?"

**Signals & evidence.** Subscriber counts and posting activity in the top venues. Overlap between them. Whether the same names recur.

**Example.** Indie hackers: extremely dense, half a dozen venues cover most of the population. Owners of small logistics firms: sparse, mostly reachable only through trade press and outbound.

---

### 1.19 Content consumption patterns

**Why it matters.** Format is not a stylistic preference — it determines whether you can produce for the channel at all, and it is set by the channel, not by you. [E — Balfour, §4.3]

**How to detect it.** Observe what the segment actually consumes, not what they say they prefer.

**Good looks like.** A format you can sustainably produce, at a cadence you can sustain, in a venue that distributes it. Text for a founder who writes well; video for one who does not freeze on camera.

**Bad looks like.** The audience lives on short-form video and the expert refuses to appear on camera. This is a real, common, and fatal mismatch that is rarely stated aloud.

**Questions to ask.** "How did you learn the last professional thing you learned?" "Podcast, video, article, or a person?"

**Signals & evidence.** Which formats get engagement in their venues. Whether the category's leading voices are writers, speakers, or builders.

**Example.** B2B procurement audiences read comparison pages and analyst reports. B2C wellness audiences watch. Building a blog for the second is diligent and useless.

### 1.20 Fit with product economics

**Why it matters.** Model-market fit: ARPU × reachable customers × capture % must clear your revenue requirement, and the ARPU must fund the CAC the channel demands. [E — §4.3]

**How to detect it.** Bottom-up arithmetic. Count the customers, multiply by realistic price, cross-check top-down. [E — §1.7]

**Good looks like.** Price high enough to fund the channel the audience actually lives in. Enough countable customers that a plausible capture rate produces a viable business.

**Bad looks like.** A high-touch sales motion required by a segment paying $20/month. Or a segment of 40 companies worldwide with a $500 price. Both are structural, and no amount of marketing skill repairs them.

**Questions to ask.** "How many of them exist?" "What can they pay?" "What will it cost to reach one?"

**Signals & evidence.** Countability of the segment. Competitor pricing. Whether incumbents use sales teams or self-serve.

**Example.** Fifteen thousand dental clinics × ₽5,000/month × 3% capture = a real business. Fifteen thousand × ₽500/month × 3% = a hobby with support tickets.

---

### 1.21 Fit with founder / brand / expert credibility

**Why it matters.** Aulet includes consistency with the team's values, passions and goals as a formal selection criterion — not sentiment, but a predictor of whether the team can sustain the work and be believed. [E — §1.1]

**How to detect it.** Ask whether the founder has standing in this segment: shared background, prior experience, an existing network, a war story.

**Good looks like.** "I was one of them" or "I've done this for them a hundred times." Instant trust, faster research, warmer outreach, credible content.

**Bad looks like.** A segment chosen purely from a spreadsheet where the founder has no network, no vocabulary, and no natural interest. These are the engagements that die in month four from boredom and low reply rates. [S]

**Questions to ask.** "How many people in this segment do you already know?" "Would you enjoy talking to these people every day for three years?"

**Signals & evidence.** Existing network overlap. Prior employment. Existing inbound from that segment.

**Example.** An ex-restaurant-manager selling to restaurants outperforms a better-funded generalist for two years, on access alone.

---

### 1.22 Fit with available acquisition channels

**Why it matters.** The direct consequence of Law 4. Channels select audiences. If none of the channels available to you reaches this segment affordably, the segment is closed to you specifically — even if it is objectively excellent. [E — §4.3]

**How to detect it.** Cross the segment against the channel list, and mark each cell reachable / expensive / impossible given the current budget and assets.

**Good looks like.** At least two viable channels, one of which is free or nearly free, and both testable within a month for under a small fixed budget. [E — Bullseye, §5.1]

**Bad looks like.** The only viable channel is one you cannot operate — outbound with no list, ads with no budget, SEO with no domain authority and an 18-month horizon.

**Questions to ask.** "Which three channels could we test in the next 30 days for under this budget?"

**Signals & evidence.** Where competitors actually spend. Ad-platform targeting parameters that map to the segment. Whether communities permit vendor participation.

**Example.** A segment perfectly targetable on LinkedIn is closed to a business with no LinkedIn presence, no budget, and no case studies — until it builds the asset. That is a sequencing conclusion, not a rejection.

---

### 1.23 Fit with positioning opportunity

**Why it matters.** The best audience is the one where you can be *the obvious choice*, not one of nine reasonable options. Positioning defines how you are a leader at something a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. [E — §3.1]

**How to detect it.** Apply the swap test: write the positioning statement, substitute the strongest competitor's name, and see whether it still reads true. If it does, there is no positioning opportunity in that segment. [S]

**Good looks like.** A segment where your specific strength maps to their specific top-priority need, and where incumbents are generalists who cannot follow you without breaking their own positioning.

**Bad looks like.** A crowded segment where you would be the eleventh entrant with the same claim. High market sophistication with no new mechanism to offer. [E — §3.2]

**Questions to ask.** "What can we say to this segment that no competitor can say honestly?" "What would we have to give up to say it?"

**Signals & evidence.** Homogeneity of competitor messaging (all saying the same thing = an opening). Whether a specialist already owns the segment.

**Example.** "Project management software" is unwinnable. "Project management for architectural practices, with RIBA stages built in" is a position an incumbent cannot copy without confusing everyone else.

---

### The near-veto set

If a segment fails any of these, no combination of strengths elsewhere rescues it: [S]

| Veto | Failure mode |
|---|---|
| **No painful problem (1.1)** | You are funding education forever. |
| **No ability to pay (1.3)** | Applause without revenue. |
| **No reachability (1.6)** | The plan requires a channel that does not exist. |
| **No urgency (1.2)** | An infinite pipeline that never closes. |
| **No trust path (1.15)** | You are structurally not credible; time cannot be bought. |

Everything else is a matter of degree. These five are gates.

## §2 — Types of target audiences

Twenty ways to define an audience. They are not competing theories; they are **lenses**, and the discipline is knowing which lens generates a *decision* for the case in front of you.

**The meta-rule:** a usable audience definition must contain, at minimum, a **who**, a **when** (circumstance or trigger), and a **where** (habitat). A definition missing the *when* is a list. A definition missing the *where* is a wish. [S]

---

### 2.1 Demographic audience
*Age, gender, income, location, education, family status.*

- **When to use.** Late, as a *filter* on an already-defined segment; or when the media channel only accepts demographic targeting; or where demography is genuinely causal (age-gated products, geography for local business).
- **Strengths.** Universally understood; directly maps to ad-platform targeting; countable.
- **Weaknesses.** Almost never explains buying. Christensen's milkshake is the canonical demolition: the same demographic bought the same product for two entirely different jobs. [E — §2.1]
- **Example.** *Weak:* "women 25–45." *Adequate:* "women 25–45 within 3 km of the studio" (for a local gym, where radius is genuinely causal). [E — §8.8]
- **How to research.** Ad-platform audience estimators, census data, VK people-search filters. [E — §8.7]
- **Where to find.** Every ad platform. It is the lingua franca of media buying, which is exactly why it survives despite explaining so little.

---

### 2.2 Psychographic audience
*Values, attitudes, identity, worldview.*

- **When to use.** Consumer brands where identity drives choice; communities organised around belief; premium and status categories.
- **Strengths.** Explains preference where function is identical across options. Powerful for creative and brand assets.
- **Weaknesses.** Frequently untargetable — there is no checkbox for "values craftsmanship." Must be converted into a behavioural or habitat proxy before it becomes actionable. [S]
- **Example.** "People who believe software should be private" → proxy: subscribers to specific privacy newsletters, users of specific tools.
- **How to research.** Community observation, language analysis, empathy mapping. [E — §8.3]
- **Where to find.** Interest communities, creator audiences, subcultural media.

---

### 2.3 Behavioural audience
*What they actually do — usage, purchase history, frequency, tools.*

- **When to use.** Whenever behavioural data exists. It is the most predictive of the classical three.
- **Strengths.** Based on facts, not statements. Directly supports RFM-style prioritisation and lookalike modelling.
- **Weaknesses.** Requires an existing customer base or observable behaviour. Blind to *why*, which means it cannot generate messaging on its own.
- **Example.** "Companies that added a second office location in the last 6 months" — behaviour that implies a specific new problem.
- **How to research.** CRM analysis, product analytics, technographics, purchase data.
- **Where to find.** Your own data first. Then technographic sources, marketplace data, app-store category rankings.

---

### 2.4 Problem-aware audience
*They know they have the problem; they do not yet know solutions exist.*

- **When to use.** When your category is young, or you are creating a new category, or the segment is early on Hunt's ladder. [E — §8.2]
- **Strengths.** Uncontested. No comparison shopping. You define the category and the evaluation criteria.
- **Weaknesses.** You pay for the education. Long cycle. Free acquisition works here (content answers their questions) but slowly; paid acquisition here is usually a money fire. [S]
- **Example.** People posting "my invoices are always late and I don't know why" who have never heard of AR automation.
- **How to research.** Forum questions, "how do I…" search queries, support tickets.
- **Where to find.** Q&A sites, Reddit, Telegram chats, YouTube comments, industry forums.

---

### 2.5 Solution-aware audience
*They know solutions exist; they are choosing between approaches.*

- **When to use.** Most commonly. This is the sweet spot for content-led acquisition and comparison content.
- **Strengths.** Education already paid for by someone else. Intent is real. Comparison keywords are cheap relative to their value.
- **Weaknesses.** Competitors are present and often better resourced. Requires genuine differentiation, not just presence.
- **Example.** Someone searching "Notion vs Obsidian for research notes."
- **How to research.** Comparison keyword research, G2/Capterra category browsing, "alternatives to X" queries. [E — §6.1, §6.4]
- **Where to find.** Comparison SERPs, review sites, category directories, "what do you use for X" threads.

---

### 2.6 High-intent audience
*Actively looking, with a timeline.*

- **When to use.** When you need revenue quickly, or to validate that a segment can convert at all.
- **Strengths.** Shortest path to cash. Best signal-to-noise for testing an offer. Intent-driven leads convert at multiples of general traffic. [E — §6.4, vendor-sourced — treat the multiple as directional]
- **Weaknesses.** Small, expensive, and contested. Never a growth strategy on its own — it is a harvest, not a farm.
- **Example.** "emergency plumber near me at 2am"; a tender published with a closing date.
- **How to research.** Commercial-intent keywords, ad-carrying SERPs, tender platforms, hiring signals. [E — §5.5, §6.4]
- **Where to find.** Search ads, marketplaces, directories, tender platforms, job boards, "recommend me a…" threads.

---

### 2.7 Community-based audience
*Defined by where they gather rather than by who they are.*

- **When to use.** Zero-budget acquisition; niche B2B; anything where trust must precede transaction.
- **Strengths.** Reachability and concentration solved by definition. The community itself validates the segment's existence.
- **Weaknesses.** Community norms punish selling. Requires patient, genuine participation. Rarely scales past the community's own ceiling. [E — §5.3]
- **Example.** "Members of three specific Telegram chats for RU e-commerce operations managers."
- **How to research.** TGStat for RU; subreddit and Discord discovery for EN; community rules and top threads. [E — §5.3, §8.6]
- **Where to find.** Telegram, Discord, Slack, Reddit, professional forums, closed paid communities.

---

### 2.8 Occasion-based audience
*Defined by a recurring situation.*

- **When to use.** Seasonal, event-driven, or ritual purchases.
- **Strengths.** Timing is knowable and repeatable. Content and campaigns can be scheduled a year out. Urgency is built in.
- **Weaknesses.** Dead time between occasions. Cash flow lumpiness. Competitors crowd the same window.
- **Example.** "Companies preparing for annual audit"; "parents in the two weeks before school starts."
- **How to research.** Seasonality curves in Wordstat / Google Trends; category calendars. [E — §8.7]
- **Where to find.** Search around the occasion; occasion-specific communities and media.

---

### 2.9 Trigger-event-based audience
*Defined by a discrete event that just happened.*

- **When to use.** Outbound, ABM, and any motion where timing beats volume. Usually the highest-yield B2B definition available. [S]
- **Strengths.** Turns a static list into a queue. The trigger is often public and timestamped, so the list rebuilds itself continuously.
- **Weaknesses.** Requires monitoring infrastructure. Windows are short. Everyone else can see public triggers too.
- **Example.** "Hired their first Head of Sales in the last 60 days." [E — §5.5]
- **How to research.** Job boards, funding databases, news alerts, registry changes, tech-stack change detection.
- **Where to find.** Job boards, press releases, corporate registries, LinkedIn role changes, tender platforms.

---

### 2.10 Role-based B2B audience
*Defined by job title and function.*

- **When to use.** As the *addressing* layer for B2B — the mechanism by which you reach a company-level segment.
- **Strengths.** Directly targetable on LinkedIn and in databases. Maps to responsibilities and therefore to problems.
- **Weaknesses.** A title is not a buying unit. A complex B2B purchase involves 6–10 people, each arriving with their own independently gathered information. [E — §6.5] Targeting one title and calling it your audience is a structural error.
- **Example.** "Head of Customer Support at 50–200-person SaaS companies" — but you must also map who signs, who blocks, and who will actually use it.
- **How to research.** Buying-committee mapping; interviews with each role separately; win/loss analysis.
- **Where to find.** LinkedIn, professional communities per role, role-specific newsletters and Slack groups.

---

### 2.11 Industry-based B2B audience
*Defined by vertical.*

- **When to use.** When the problem is genuinely industry-shaped (regulation, workflow, terminology, seasonality) — which makes the vertical causal rather than cosmetic.
- **Strengths.** Language, references and case studies compound. Word of mouth is dense within verticals. [E — §1.1] Registries and associations make the list findable.
- **Weaknesses.** Ceiling equals the vertical's size. Vertical downturns hit the whole book at once. Easy to confuse "we have three customers in logistics" with "we serve logistics."
- **Example.** "Dental clinics" — regulated, licensed, associated, conference-attending, tightly networked.
- **How to research.** Trade press, associations, conference agendas, licensing registers, industry classification codes. [E — ABCDX formalized attributes, §4.2]
- **Where to find.** Associations, trade shows, vertical media, public registers, vertical marketplaces.

---

### 2.12 Budget-based audience
*Defined by the size of the wallet and the buying process it implies.*

- **When to use.** When the same problem is bought completely differently at different price tiers — which is most of the time.
- **Strengths.** Directly determines sales motion, cycle length, and required proof. Prevents the classic error of selling enterprise-style to SMBs.
- **Weaknesses.** Budget is often invisible from outside; must be inferred from proxies. Can drift into snobbery and away from fit.
- **Example.** "Companies with a marketing budget over ₽500k/month" — a different creature from a ₽50k/month company with the identical problem.
- **How to research.** Van Westendorp per tier [E — §7.1]; competitor price ladders; headcount and funding proxies.
- **Where to find.** Proxied via headcount, funding, office locations, existing vendor logos, hiring patterns.

---

### 2.13 Job-to-be-done audience
*Defined by the progress they are trying to make in a circumstance.*

- **When to use.** As the **primary definition** in almost every case. The other lenses become descriptors of the people doing this job. [S]
- **Strengths.** Explains buying rather than describing buyers. Reveals non-obvious competitors (the spreadsheet, the intern, doing nothing). Generates messaging directly.
- **Weaknesses.** Not directly targetable — no ad platform sells "people trying to make progress." Must be bridged to an observable proxy.
- **Example.** "When I inherit a messy client account, I want to show the client something credible in week one, so I don't lose them before I fix anything."
- **How to research.** Switch interviews with recent buyers; four-forces analysis; ~10 interviews to surface the main struggling moments. [E — §2.3]
- **Where to find.** You do not find a job; you find people in the circumstance. Bridge via trigger events (2.9) and habitats (§5).

---

### 2.14 Lifestyle-based audience
*Defined by how their week is actually structured.*

- **When to use.** Consumer products where the constraint is time, place or routine rather than money.
- **Strengths.** Predicts context of use, which predicts channel and format. Generates highly specific creative.
- **Weaknesses.** Slides easily into stock-photo fiction. Requires observation, not imagination.
- **Example.** "Shift workers who shop between 22:00 and 01:00" — a scheduling fact with real commercial consequences.
- **How to research.** Diary studies, timestamp analysis of your own traffic, observation.
- **Where to find.** Behavioural data first; then the media that matches the schedule.

---

### 2.15 Pain-based audience
*Defined by the specific consequence they are suffering.*

- **When to use.** For messaging and for ranking segments by severity. The most direct route to urgency.
- **Strengths.** Maps one-to-one onto copy. Easy to validate through review mining and forum threads. Instantly comparable across segments — pain has magnitude.
- **Weaknesses.** Pain intensity is easy to overstate from the outside. Some pains are chronic and tolerated indefinitely — which looks like urgency and is not.
- **Example.** "Loses 6 hours a week reconciling two systems by hand."
- **How to research.** Review mining focused on 3–4 star reviews [E — §6.1]; support tickets; RU practice suggests ~20–30 reviews + ~10 calls + ~10 queries as a defensible first pass. [E — §8.5]
- **Where to find.** Reviews, support queues, forums, competitor complaint threads, sales-call recordings.

---

### 2.16 Aspirational audience
*Defined by who they want to become.*

- **When to use.** Education, coaching, career products, status goods, community products.
- **Strengths.** High emotional energy and willingness to pay. Strong content resonance. Identity-driven loyalty.
- **Weaknesses.** **Aspiration converts to intent unreliably.** People buy the identity cheaply (following, watching, saving) and the transformation rarely. High refund and churn risk. Easy to build an audience of admirers who never purchase. [S]
- **Example.** "People who want to become independent consultants" — enormous, engaged, and only partly monetisable.
- **How to research.** Content engagement patterns; but *always* validate against a payment event, never against likes.
- **Where to find.** YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, course platforms, aspirational communities.
- **Rule.** Never score an aspirational audience on engagement. Score it on completed transactions by comparable sellers.

---

### 2.17 Underserved audience
*Has the need; the category serves them badly or not at all.*

- **When to use.** When the category is mature and homogeneous — precisely where a specialist can beat generalists.
- **Strengths.** Low competition for a real, funded need. Positioning writes itself. Word of mouth is fast because the relief is novel.
- **Weaknesses.** Sometimes underserved for a good reason (unprofitable, unservable, regulated). You must find out which.
- **Example.** Ulwick's framing: outcomes scoring high on importance and low on satisfaction. [E — §2.2] Blue Ocean's Tier 2 refusers: those who evaluated the category and said no because it was unacceptable, unaffordable or too complicated. [E — §1.3]
- **How to research.** Importance-vs-satisfaction gap analysis; refuser interviews; complaint clustering across many providers.
- **Where to find.** Competitor complaint threads; churn cohorts; the "we looked at all of them and gave up" posts.

---

### 2.18 Overlooked / unexpected audience
*Nobody in the category is marketing to them, yet they buy.*

- **When to use.** When obvious segments are saturated, or when your existing data shows anomalies.
- **Strengths.** Cheap attention. No competitive claims to overcome. Often disproportionately loyal because nobody has ever spoken to them directly.
- **Weaknesses.** Hard to size. Sometimes small. Requires evidence, or it is just a hunch with a slide.
- **Example.** Documented segment pivots: consumer → enterprise once the revenue was examined; a product design that resonated with a customer type the company had never considered. [E — §6.6]
- **How to research.** §3 of this report is entirely about this.
- **Where to find.** Your own anomalies; adjacent communities; competitor reviews written by "the wrong" customer.

---

### 2.19 Adjacent audience
*Structurally similar to your proven audience, one step sideways.*

- **When to use.** After a beachhead is won — Aulet's "can you leverage this segment to enter others?" [E — §1.1]
- **Strengths.** Assets transfer: case studies, vocabulary, product, sometimes referrals. Lower research cost.
- **Weaknesses.** Adjacency is often illusory. The workflow may be identical and the *buying process* completely different, which is the part that matters.
- **Example.** From dental clinics → veterinary clinics: same shape (licensed, appointment-driven, owner-operated), different regulation and referral networks.
- **How to research.** Test whether the three homogeneity conditions still hold — same product, same sales process, word of mouth between them. [E — §1.1]
- **Where to find.** Adjacent associations, neighbouring categories in directories, "people also considered" data.

---

### 2.20 Anti-audience / audience to avoid
*Explicitly excluded. The most under-used definition in marketing.*

- **When to use.** Always. Every engagement should name one.
- **Strengths.** Protects margin, sanity, and positioning. Sharpens the message — messages that exclude are the only ones that attract. Speeds qualification.
- **Weaknesses.** Emotionally hard when revenue is scarce; needs explicit permission to say no.
- **Example.** ABCDX's **D-segment**: no real need; even if they buy they extract no value and generate negative experience. [E — §4.2] Also the **X-segment**: would be excellent but requires product work you have not done — a roadmap signal, not current demand. [E — §4.2]
- **How to research.** Analyse your worst customers with the same rigour as your best: highest support load, lowest margin, longest cycles, most refunds. Study anti-patterns and disqualifiers deliberately. [E — ICP practice, §1.4 cluster]
- **Where to find.** Your own support queue, refund log, and the deals you were relieved to lose.

---

### Choosing a lens: the routing rule

For any engagement, use this order. [S]

1. **JTBD (2.13)** — always. Establish the job and the circumstance first. This is the definition.
2. **Trigger events (2.9)** — to make it timed.
3. **Habitat / community (2.7)** — to make it reachable.
4. **Role + industry + budget (2.10–2.12)** for B2B, or **behaviour + demography (2.3, 2.1)** for B2C — to make it targetable.
5. **Underserved / overlooked (2.17, 2.18)** — as a parallel generator, always run at least once.
6. **Anti-audience (2.20)** — before delivering. Name who you are refusing.

Steps 1–3 produce the *definition*. Step 4 produces the *targeting parameters*. Confusing the two is why so many audience documents describe someone precisely and cannot reach them at all.

## §3 — How to find unexpected target audiences

An unexpected audience is not a creative flourish. It is a **structural arbitrage**: a group with real pain and real money that nobody in the category is addressing, because the category defined itself around a stereotype of its customer. [S]

The economics are asymmetric and worth stating plainly. In a crowded segment you compete on execution against funded incumbents. In an overlooked segment you compete against *nobody* — which means cheaper attention, warmer replies, easier positioning, and disproportionate loyalty from people who have never been spoken to directly. [S]

Two rules govern this whole section:

1. **An unexpected audience must survive the same scoring as an obvious one.** Novelty is not a score. Unexpected + unable to pay = a nice observation. [S]
2. **Every candidate needs at least one piece of external evidence** — a thread, a review, a job posting, a workaround someone built. A segment that exists only in the analysis is a hypothesis, and must be labelled as one. [S]

---

### 3.1 Fourteen generators

Run these as a checklist. Each is a question you point at the market; each reliably produces candidates that segment-brainstorming does not.

---

#### G1 — Who is already hacking together a solution?

**The logic.** von Hippel's lead users experience mainstream needs early and in extreme form, and build their own workarounds. In studies of extreme sports clubs, over a third of members had built or modified their own equipment. [E — §2.5]

**Where to look.** Spreadsheet templates being shared or sold. Zapier/Make chains. Custom scripts on GitHub. "I built a thing to solve X" posts. Notion templates. Google Sheets with a person's name on them.

**Why it works.** Someone who built a workaround has *already paid* — in hours. They have proven willingness to pay in the only currency that never lies. And their workaround is a free specification for your product.

**Example.** Recruiters maintaining elaborate Airtable bases to track candidates are a validated ATS market segment that no ATS vendor is speaking to, because they self-identify as "people who don't need an ATS."

---

#### G2 — Extreme users at both ends

**The logic.** Extreme users push past incremental improvement. [E — §2.5] The heaviest user and the most reluctant user both reveal what the mainstream will need later.

**Where to look.** The top 1% by usage volume in any product's data. The people asking questions that are "too advanced" in community forums. Conversely: the people who tried the category and bounced in week one.

**Why it works.** The heavy end shows where the ceiling is and who will pay a premium. The reluctant end shows the barrier that a whole underserved tier is stuck behind.

**Example.** Whoever processes ten times the normal document volume is either your best future enterprise account or the person who will build a competitor out of frustration.

---

#### G3 — Complaints about competitors

**The logic.** Review mining exists to find themes repeating across hundreds of reviews; 3- and 4-star reviews carry the most actionable insight because those people bought, stayed honest, and are not venting. [E — §6.1]

**Where to look.** G2, Capterra, TrustRadius for B2B software; Amazon and marketplaces for consumer; app stores for mobile; Otzovik and marketplace reviews for RU. [E — §6.1, §8.4]

**Why it works.** But look for a *specific* thing: complaints written by someone who is clearly **not the intended customer**. "Great for agencies, useless for in-house teams like mine" is not a feature request. It is an entire unaddressed segment describing itself.

**Example.** Repeated complaints of "too complex for what I need" across every product in a category define an overserved tier — Ulwick's disruptable-from-below condition. [E — §2.2]

---

#### G4 — Adjacent communities

**The logic.** The same job appears in industries that never talk to each other. Lead-user theory explicitly includes users in *other markets* who face the same problem in more extreme form. [E — §2.5]

**Where to look.** Take the underlying job — scheduling irregular shifts, proving compliance, collecting deposits — and search for it in three unrelated industries.

**Why it works.** You arrive with a solution the whole industry has never seen, in a market with no incumbent competing on that dimension.

**Example.** Appointment-and-deposit software built for beauty salons transfers almost unchanged to tattoo studios, dog groomers, private tutors and physiotherapists — and each of those markets is served by generalists who do not understand no-shows.

---

#### G5 — People paying for imperfect alternatives

**The logic.** Blue Ocean Tier 1: buyers who purchase minimally, out of necessity, while being mentally noncustomers — "waiting to jump ship." [E — §1.3]

**Where to look.** Customers of the category's worst-reviewed but widely-used product. People on the cheapest plan of an expensive tool. Anyone whose renewal is described as reluctant.

**Why it works.** Spending is already proven. Only the destination is in question. Displacement is far cheaper than creation.

**Example.** Firms paying for enterprise software and using 5% of it are simultaneously the easiest sale for a focused alternative and the hardest for a broader one.

---

#### G6 — People with the problem but not the vocabulary

**The logic.** The unaware and problem-aware stages. [E — §3.2, §8.2] They describe symptoms, not categories, so they are invisible to keyword research and to every competitor relying on it.

**Where to look.** Forum posts phrased entirely as symptoms: "why does this always happen to me", "am I the only one who…", "is there a normal way to do this". Search queries with no category term in them.

**Why it works.** Zero competition, because competitors optimise for category keywords these people never type. Free acquisition is unusually effective here, because answering the question *is* the marketing.

**Caveat.** Long education cycle (1.14). Excellent for content-led acquisition; poor for paid.

**Example.** Someone posting "my bookkeeper keeps asking for receipts I already sent her" has an expense-management problem and has never heard the phrase "expense management."

---

#### G7 — Trigger events nobody is monitoring

**The logic.** Public, timestamped admissions of a problem. [E — §5.5, §2.3]

**Where to look.** Job postings; funding; new executives; office moves; regulatory deadlines; licence grants; lawsuits; new locations; tender publications; certification expiries.

**Why it works.** The audience is not defined by identity but by *the last thirty days*, which most competitors do not monitor at all.

**Example.** A company posting a vacancy for a role that does work your product automates has published its budget, its pain, its timeline, and a contact — voluntarily.

---

#### G8 — Hidden B2B buyers

**The logic.** 6–10 people in a complex B2B decision, each arriving with independently gathered information. [E — §6.5] The category markets to one of them, usually the most senior. The other five to nine are unaddressed.

**Where to look.** The person who will actually use it daily. The one who gets blamed if it fails. The blocker — security, legal, finance, procurement. The internal champion who has to sell it upward with no materials.

**Why it works.** Arming the champion is a different, cheaper, and less contested motion than persuading the executive. Nobody is producing "the internal deck you can forward to your CFO," and it is often the highest-leverage asset in the funnel.

**Example.** In compliance software, the buyer is a director and the *blocker* is IT security. Content aimed at the blocker removes the real obstacle; content aimed at the buyer competes with everyone.

---

#### G9 — Underserved geographies and subcultures

**The logic.** Blank's clone market. [E — §1.2] A model proven in one geography, language or subculture, absent from another.

**Where to look.** Categories mature in EN markets and thin in RU/CIS, or vice versa. Regional cities where national players do not localise. Language communities served only in translation. Professional subcultures with their own norms.

**Why it works.** Demand is pre-validated elsewhere; you import certainty. Local language and local proof beat superior foreign products routinely.

**Caveat.** Verify the *reason* for the absence. Sometimes the market is genuinely different (regulation, payment habits, purchasing power) and the gap is not an opening but a wall. [A]

---

#### G10 — High-value niches inside broad markets

**The logic.** ABCDX: rank paying customers by value received and speed of payment, and it turns out the A-segment shares attributes nobody had noticed. [E — §4.2] Superhuman's version: filtering to the persona dominating the "very disappointed" group lifted the PMF score from 22% to 32% **without touching the product**. [E — §4.1]

**Where to look.** Inside your own customer base. Sort by margin, cycle length, support load, retention, referrals. The averages are hiding the segments. [E — §6.6]

**Why it works.** It is not a hypothesis. It is measurement of something already true and unexploited.

**Example.** An agency serving "SMBs" discovers that four of its five profitable clients are franchise operators with multi-location reporting needs — a segment it never named, never marketed to, and could own.

---

#### G11 — High urgency, low marketing saturation

**The logic.** Urgency (1.2) is what makes people buy; saturation is what makes them expensive. The intersection of high urgency and low saturation is the most valuable coordinate in the whole space. [S]

**Where to look.** Problems with legal or financial deadlines that are too unglamorous for venture-funded competitors. Categories where all the marketing is done by lead-gen aggregators rather than by operators.

**Why it works.** You get intent-level demand at awareness-level prices.

**Example.** Almost any deadline-driven, boring, regulated back-office problem in a mid-sized industry. Nobody wants to make content about it, so nobody has.

---

#### G12 — People already gathered in communities

**The logic.** Concentration (1.7) and density (1.18) solved by definition, and a self-selecting membership list. [E — §5.3, §8.6]

**Where to look.** TGStat for RU Telegram; subreddit and Discord discovery for EN; professional Slack groups; paid mastermind communities; association member directories. [E — §8.6, §5.3]

**Why it works.** The community's existence is proof the segment is real, coherent, and socially connected — which is exactly what makes word of mouth work.

**The move.** Look for communities organised around a *problem* rather than around a *tool*. A community named after a competitor is a customer list. A community named after a struggle is a market.

---

#### G13 — People asking the same question repeatedly

**The logic.** Community research surfaces conversation types worth isolating: **Pain Points · Solution Requests · Money Talk · Hot Discussions.** [E — §5.3] The questions people repeatedly ask *are* the content plan and the segment map simultaneously.

**Where to look.** The question that gets asked every month in the same forum. The thread that always gets bumped. The FAQ nobody wrote.

**Why it works.** Repetition is frequency evidence. A question asked once is curiosity; asked monthly for two years, it is a market with no supplier.

**Example.** "How do I price this?" asked endlessly in a freelance community is a product, not a thread.

---

#### G14 — "Expensive pain" and the five pressures

**The logic.** Pain is not equally monetisable. The pains that convert most reliably carry a **quantified external cost**. [S] Five pressure types dominate:

| Pressure | What it looks like | Why it converts |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliance** | Fines, licences, audits, certifications | Non-negotiable, dated, with a named penalty |
| **Time** | Billable hours lost, hiring another person | Directly convertible to money |
| **Risk** | Data loss, downtime, lawsuits, safety | Insurance logic; buyers pay a premium for reduced variance |
| **Status** | Being seen as competent by peers, clients or a boss | Emotional engine, rational justification |
| **Money** | Cash flow, leakage, unbilled work | Self-funding purchase |

**Where to look.** Segments under two or more pressures simultaneously. Compliance + time is the classic combination — it forces action on a schedule with a budget.

**Why it works.** The buyer does not need to be convinced the problem is worth solving. They need to be convinced you can solve it.

---

### 3.2 Two more generators worth running by default

**G15 — Ask your existing users who else should use it.** The Superhuman PMF survey's second question — *"What type of people do you think would most benefit from this?"* — is a free unexpected-audience generator, answered by people who understand the product's value and have no reason to flatter you. [E — §4.1]

**G16 — Interview the refusers.** Blue Ocean Tier 2: people who evaluated the category and consciously said no, because it was unacceptable, unaffordable or too complicated. [E — §1.3] They are the most under-interviewed group in commerce, and they explain the category's ceiling better than any customer can.

### 3.4 How to *disqualify* an unexpected audience fast

Novelty is seductive. Run these four tests before anything reaches a recommendation. [S]

1. **The list test.** Name a place where 200 of them are already gathered. No place, no segment. (1.6)
2. **The money test.** Name something comparable they already pay for. No precedent, treat willingness as unproven. (1.4, 1.11)
3. **The absence test.** Ask *why* no competitor serves them. If the honest answer is "it's unprofitable" or "it's unservable," that is the finding. If it is "nobody thought to look," that is the opportunity.
4. **The reachability-cost test.** Estimate what it costs to have twenty conversations with them this month. If the answer is "we can't," the segment is closed to you today, whatever its score. (1.22)

A candidate that passes all four is a recommendation. A candidate that fails one is a hypothesis with a named experiment attached. **Both are legitimate outputs; conflating them is not.**

## §4 — Audience research methods

Twenty-three methods, ordered from most evidential to most inferential. The ordering matters: **a method's position in this list is inversely related to how much you should trust a conclusion drawn only from it.** [S]

Each entry carries: what it reveals · process · tools · limitations · output format · example finding.

**The universal rule, from Fitzpatrick:** ask about what has already happened, never about opinions or predictions. [E — §2.4] Every method below is a variation on finding out what people actually did.

---

### Tier A — Direct evidence from people who have bought or nearly bought

---

#### 4.1 Switch interviews (JTBD interview analysis)

- **Reveals.** The actual causal chain: trigger → search → shortlist → decision → the four forces. The single richest source of audience truth. [E — §2.3]
- **Process.** (1) Recruit people who bought *recently* — within 90 days, so memory is intact. (2) Start at the purchase and walk *backwards* to the first thought. (3) Find the exact moment the passive thought became an active search. (4) Map Push, Pull, Anxiety, Habit. (5) Repeat until themes recur — roughly 10 interviews of 30–60 minutes typically surfaces the 3–5 struggling moments covering most of a market. [E — §2.3]
- **Tools.** Recording plus transcript; any note tool; a shared timeline template.
- **Limitations.** Requires access to recent buyers. Memory reconstruction is imperfect. Cannot be run pre-launch — use 4.2 instead.
- **Output.** One timeline per interview; a consolidated four-forces table per segment; a verbatim quote bank.
- **Example finding.** "Every buyer started looking within a week of a specific failure, and all of them tried two free tools first" — which tells you both the trigger to target and the competitive set to displace.

---

#### 4.2 Customer development / problem interviews

- **Reveals.** Whether the problem exists, how it is currently handled, what it costs, who else is involved. Pre-launch equivalent of 4.1. [E — §2.4]
- **Process.** (1) Define the hypothesis you could be wrong about. (2) Recruit from the habitat, not from friends. (3) Ask only about the past: what happened, when, what it cost, what you tried, what you paid. (4) Record verbatim language. (5) End with "who else should I talk to?" — the referral test doubles as a density measurement.
- **Tools.** A written script; recording; a coding sheet for themes.
- **Limitations.** People are polite. The whole Mom Test exists because of this. Any question containing "would you" produces fiction.
- **Output.** Problem-frequency table; verbatim quote bank; a list of current alternatives with the price of each.
- **Example finding.** "Nine of twelve described the same workaround, and four had built it themselves" — a lead-user signal (G1) and a specification simultaneously.

---

#### 4.3 Sales call analysis

- **Reveals.** Real objections in the buyer's own words, real competitors, real budget conversations, real deal-killers. The highest-fidelity source most businesses already own and never mine.
- **Process.** (1) Take the last 20 calls, split won / lost / stalled. (2) Tag by objection, by named alternative, by trigger mentioned. (3) Compare won vs. lost on segment attributes — this is ABCDX applied to the pipeline. [E — §4.2] (4) Extract verbatim phrasing for copy.
- **Tools.** Call recording; transcription; a tagging sheet. Do not skip step 4 — the copy is in the transcripts.
- **Limitations.** Biased toward people who agreed to a call. Blind to those who never entered the pipeline.
- **Output.** Objection frequency table; won/lost attribute comparison; competitor mention counts.
- **Example finding.** "Every lost deal mentioned a security review; every won deal had fewer than 50 employees" — a segment boundary discovered from the pipeline rather than invented.

---

#### 4.4 Support ticket analysis

- **Reveals.** Where the product breaks the promise, which segment is being served badly, and which use cases you never designed for but people are attempting anyway.
- **Process.** (1) Pull 3–6 months of tickets. (2) Categorise by root cause, not by symptom. (3) Cross-tabulate by customer attribute. (4) Flag tickets describing a use case you never intended — these are unexpected-audience candidates (G1).
- **Tools.** Helpdesk export; a spreadsheet; clustering.
- **Limitations.** Only reflects existing customers, and only vocal ones.
- **Output.** Root-cause frequency table by segment; a list of unintended use cases.
- **Example finding.** "Forty percent of tickets come from one industry that represents eight percent of revenue" — a D-segment identified with numbers. [E — §4.2]

---

#### 4.5 CRM / sales data analysis

- **Reveals.** Which segments actually pay, pay fast, stay, and refer — the empirical ICP as opposed to the aspirational one.
- **Process.** (1) Export all closed-won and closed-lost. (2) Score each account on value delivered and speed of payment — ABCDX. [E — §4.2] (3) Extract the shared attributes of the A group, splitting **formalized** (role, industry, headcount, vacancies, licences) from **non-formalized** (problem circumstance, monetary damage, deadline, budget). [E — §4.2] (4) Do the same for the worst decile: that is your anti-audience.
- **Tools.** CRM export; a spreadsheet; cohort analysis.
- **Limitations.** Requires history. Reflects who you *sold to*, which is partly an artefact of past marketing — beware confirming your own bias.
- **Output.** ABCDX table; the attribute profile of A and of D; a disqualification list.
- **Example finding.** ICP practice reports that firms focused on well-defined best-fit accounts see materially higher win rates than broad-targeting peers. [E — §1 cluster, vendor-sourced — treat the magnitude as directional, the direction as sound]

---

#### 4.6 PMF survey with segmentation

- **Reveals.** Which segment already has product-market fit with you — and the profile of the high-expectation customer. [E — §4.1]
- **Process.** (1) Survey users who engaged at least twice in two weeks. (2) Four questions: how would you feel if you could no longer use this (very / somewhat / not disappointed); what type of people would most benefit; what is the main benefit you get; how can we improve it. (3) Benchmark against ~40% "very disappointed." (4) **Segment the responses by persona and re-score** — this is the actual insight. (5) Build the HXC profile from the dominant persona in the "very disappointed" group. (6) Ignore "not disappointed" entirely; mine "somewhat disappointed who already value the core benefit" for the roadmap. [E — §4.1]
- **Tools.** Any survey tool; a spreadsheet for segmentation.
- **Limitations.** Needs an engaged user base. Useless pre-launch.
- **Output.** Segmented PMF scores; HXC profile; benefit language in users' own words; unexpected-audience candidates from question 2.
- **Example finding.** Superhuman: 22% overall → 32% after filtering to the right persona, with no product change; 58% within three quarters. [E — §4.1]

---

#### 4.7 Survey analysis (general)

- **Reveals.** Distribution and prevalence. Good for confirming a pattern found qualitatively; poor for discovering one.
- **Process.** (1) Never survey before you have interviewed — you will not know what to ask. (2) Ask about past behaviour and observable facts. (3) Keep hypotheticals out, or quarantine them clearly. (4) Segment before analysing, not after. [E — §6.3]
- **Tools.** Any survey platform; RU: Yandex Forms, Marquiz-style quizzes.
- **Limitations.** Stated preference. Sampling bias. Surveys tell you *how many*, never *why*.
- **Output.** Prevalence tables cut by segment.
- **Example finding.** "Sixty percent of respondents in one segment have a deadline in Q4" — a scheduling fact you can build a campaign around.

---

#### 4.8 Expert interviews

- **Reveals.** Market structure, buying processes, who really decides, and the segments an outsider cannot see.
- **Process.** (1) Identify people who sell *to* your target audience already but do not compete with you — adjacent vendors, consultants, association staff, trade journalists. (2) Ask about patterns across many buyers, not about their opinion of your idea. (3) Ask specifically: "who buys fastest, and who wastes everyone's time?"
- **Tools.** LinkedIn; associations; conference speaker lists.
- **Limitations.** Second-hand. Experts generalise, and their generalisations are a decade old more often than they admit.
- **Output.** Market structure map; buying-process map; a shortlist of segments to investigate.
- **Example finding.** "Nobody buys in this vertical between May and August" — a fact that reorders a whole go-to-market plan.

---

### Tier B — Public traces of real behaviour

---

#### 4.9 Review mining (your own category)

- **Reveals.** Pain themes, the audience's vocabulary, switching triggers, and unaddressed segments.
- **Process.** (1) Start with 10–15 recent reviews across the rating range. (2) **Concentrate on 3- and 4-star reviews** — they bought, they are honest, they are not venting. (3) Count theme frequency to convert subjective complaints into an ordered list. (4) Capture verbatim phrasing. (5) Flag any review that reveals a customer type the vendor clearly did not design for. [E — §6.1]
- **Tools.** G2, Capterra, TrustRadius (B2B software); Amazon and marketplaces (consumer); app stores (mobile); Otzovik, Yandex Maps, marketplace reviews (RU). [E — §6.1, §8.4]
- **Limitations.** Reviews over-represent extremes. Incentivised reviews distort B2B software sites.
- **Output.** Theme frequency table; verbatim quote bank; a list of "wrong customer" reviews.
- **Example finding.** Forty reviews across six competing products all complaining about onboarding = onboarding is not a feature, it is a segment. [E — §6.1]

---

#### 4.10 Competitor review analysis

- **Reveals.** Who your competitors actually serve, who they fail, and where the positioning gap is.
- **Process.** (1) Pick the three most-reviewed competitors. (2) Segment their reviewers by apparent company type or use case. (3) Cluster complaints per competitor. (4) Find the group that appears in the reviews but not in the marketing. That group is unclaimed.
- **Tools.** As 4.9, plus competitor case-study pages for the *claimed* audience — the gap between claimed and actual is the finding.
- **Limitations.** Reviewer self-description is unreliable.
- **Output.** Competitor-audience map; complaint clusters; a list of unclaimed groups.
- **Example finding.** "Their case studies are all enterprise; their reviews are all sole traders" — the market has quietly repositioned the product and the vendor has not noticed.

---

#### 4.11 Marketplace review analysis

- **Reveals.** Consumer purchase reasoning, price sensitivity, gifting vs. self-purchase, use contexts you never imagined.
- **Process.** (1) Find the category's bestsellers. (2) Read reviews for *purchase reason* rather than product opinion. (3) Tag by use context. (4) Note explicitly which reviews describe a use the manufacturer never intended.
- **Tools.** Wildberries, Ozon, Yandex Market (RU); Amazon, Etsy (EN); category analytics tools. [E — §8.7]
- **Limitations.** Marketplace review culture is noisy and partly incentivised.
- **Output.** Use-context table with frequencies; price-reaction notes.
- **Example finding.** A product bought overwhelmingly as a gift has a completely different buyer from its user — which changes the entire message.

---

#### 4.12 App store review and ranking analysis

- **Reveals.** Mobile audience expectations, category maturity, and the specific moment users churn.
- **Process.** (1) Read the 1-star reviews for churn triggers and the 3-star for unmet needs. (2) Track ranking movement in the category to gauge saturation. (3) Read the *replies* competitors give — they reveal what the vendor considers acceptable.
- **Tools.** App Store, Google Play, RuStore; ASO tools.
- **Limitations.** Skews toward extremes and toward consumer.
- **Output.** Churn-trigger list; unmet-need list.

---

#### 4.13 Reddit / forum research

- **Reveals.** Unfiltered problem statements, real vocabulary, and the repeat questions that constitute a market. [E — §5.3]
- **Process.** (1) Build a keyword list including synonyms, abbreviations and competitor names. (2) Find communities via community search plus plain Google. (3) Read for the four conversation types: **Pain Points · Solution Requests · Money Talk · Hot Discussions.** [E — §5.3] (4) Log repeat questions with dates to establish frequency. (5) Read the *comments*, not the posts — the posts are performances, the comments are opinions.
- **Tools.** Reddit search and community discovery; site: search on Google; Pikabu and topical forums (RU). [E — §8.4] **Note:** GummySearch, long the standard tool here, is reported to be shutting down — verify before recommending it or any successor. [E — §5.3]
- **Limitations.** Self-selected, vocal, skewed young and technical in EN. Community norms punish extraction.
- **Output.** Community inventory with size and activity; repeat-question log; verbatim quote bank.
- **Example finding.** The same pricing question asked monthly for two years in one community = a validated product with no supplier.

---

#### 4.14 Telegram / Discord / Slack community research

- **Reveals.** The RU/CIS habitat layer in particular, plus real-time professional conversation that never reaches the open web. [E — §8.6]
- **Process.** (1) Find channels and chats via TGStat (RU) or community directories (EN). (2) Record subscriber counts, posting activity and, critically, *comment* activity — a large channel with dead comments is a broadcast, not a community. (3) Read the last 200 messages of the top chats. (4) Note who answers questions — those people are the segment's influencers. (5) Observe the rules on vendor participation before doing anything.
- **Tools.** TGStat (RU catalogue with per-channel statistics); Discord and Slack community directories. [E — §8.6]
- **Limitations.** Closed communities are invisible from outside. Telegram search is poor. Scraping violates most communities' norms and often their rules.
- **Output.** Habitat inventory: name, size, activity, rules, key voices, admission route.
- **Example finding.** Three chats totalling 12,000 members contain most of a national professional segment — meaning free acquisition is arithmetically viable.

---

#### 4.15 YouTube comment mining

- **Reveals.** Objections and confusion at scale, and the exact questions the audience asks after being told something.
- **Process.** (1) Find the three most-viewed videos explaining the problem. (2) Read comments for "but what about…" and "this doesn't work if…" — these are objections, pre-sorted by popularity. (3) Sort by top-voted: the crowd has ranked the objections for you.
- **Tools.** YouTube search and comment sort; transcripts for topic extraction.
- **Limitations.** Comment culture varies wildly by niche.
- **Output.** Objection list ranked by votes; confusion points; content-gap list.
- **Example finding.** The top comment on every explainer video asks the same follow-up question — that question is your lead magnet.

---

#### 4.16 Social listening

- **Reveals.** Brand and category mentions, sentiment, and emerging vocabulary.
- **Process.** (1) Track category terms, competitor names and problem phrases. (2) Separate complaint mentions from recommendation-request mentions — the latter are live leads. (3) Note who is asked for recommendations; those are the segment's trusted nodes.
- **Tools.** Brand-monitoring platforms; RU: Brand Analytics, YouScan, Google Alerts as a free floor.
- **Limitations.** Paid tools for real coverage. Noisy. Poor coverage of closed channels — which is where RU professional conversation actually lives.
- **Output.** Mention volume by theme; recommendation-request log; influencer node list.

---

#### 4.17 Content performance analysis

- **Reveals.** Which topics your audience actually cares about — measured, not guessed — and which segment is arriving.
- **Process.** (1) Rank your own content by qualified conversions, never by traffic or likes. (2) Do the same for competitors' most-shared content. (3) Identify topic clusters that attract the *right* people. (4) Note the mismatch: high-traffic content bringing the wrong audience is a positioning warning, not a success.
- **Tools.** Analytics; RU: Yandex Metrica; social platform insights.
- **Limitations.** Reflects your current distribution, not the market. Vanity metrics are actively misleading here.
- **Output.** Topic → segment → conversion table.
- **Example finding.** The post with the least traffic produced the most sales calls — and it was about a segment you had deprioritised.

---

### Tier C — Market-structure and intent signals

---

#### 4.18 Search query analysis

- **Reveals.** Demand volume, phrasing, seasonality, geography, and awareness stage.
- **Process.** (1) Collect problem-phrased, solution-phrased and brand-phrased queries separately — the ratio between them tells you the market's awareness distribution. (2) Check seasonality and region. (3) Read the phrasing as copy source. (4) Check whether the SERP carries ads — advertiser presence is validation that the query converts. [E — §6.4]
- **Tools.** Яндекс Wordstat, Google Trends (free, RU-standard) [E — §8.7]; Ahrefs, Semrush (EN).
- **Limitations.** Blind to new markets — zero volume there is expected, not damning. [E — §1.2] Blind to problems people cannot name (G6).
- **Output.** Query table with volume, intent class, awareness stage, seasonality.

---

#### 4.19 SEO / competitive keyword analysis

- **Reveals.** Which audiences competitors are actually pursuing, and which have been abandoned.
- **Process.** (1) Pull competitors' top organic pages. (2) Classify each by the segment it targets. (3) Find the segment-shaped gaps. (4) Assess difficulty honestly against your domain's realistic capability.
- **Tools.** Ahrefs, Semrush (EN); Keys.so, Bukvarix (RU).
- **Limitations.** Tool data is estimated. Ranking ≠ converting. SEO horizons are long, which makes this a strategy input, not a validation method.
- **Output.** Competitor-segment map; gap list with difficulty scores.

---

#### 4.20 Competitor ad-library analysis

- **Reveals.** Competitors' audience hypotheses, message angles, and — through ad longevity — which ones are profitable.
- **Process.** (1) Open the ad library for each competitor. (2) Sort by start date; **ads running 60–90+ days are almost certainly profitable**, because nobody keeps paying for losers. [E — §5.4] (3) Extract the angle, the promise and the implied audience from each long-runner. (4) Note which segments they have *stopped* advertising to.
- **Tools.** Meta Ad Library (free, public); Google Ads Transparency Center, TikTok Creative Center [A — assumed by analogy, not verified in this corpus]; RU: ad-spy services for VK and Yandex [A].
- **Limitations.** Longevity is a proxy, not proof. No spend or performance data. Coverage varies by platform and jurisdiction.
- **Output.** Angle inventory per competitor with run-length; implied-segment list.
- **Example finding.** A competitor has run the same ad for eleven months aimed at a segment you dismissed — that is a well-funded vote against your judgement.

---

#### 4.21 Trigger-event and intent-signal monitoring

- **Reveals.** Who has a problem *right now*, with a date attached. [E — §5.5]
- **Process.** (1) Define the events that imply your problem: hiring for a related role, funding, new executive, new location, licence, regulation, tender, tech-stack change. (2) Set up monitoring. (3) Score each event for recency — the window is usually weeks. (4) Route to outbound while it is warm.
- **Tools.** Job boards (hh.ru, HeadHunter, LinkedIn, Indeed); funding databases; corporate registries; tender platforms; news alerts.
- **Limitations.** Vendor-published conversion claims in this area are unverified [E — §5.5, reliability 2]. Public triggers are visible to competitors too. Monitoring has an operational cost.
- **Output.** A trigger list with source, detection method, freshness window and outreach angle.
- **Example finding.** A job posting quoting the salary for a role your product replaces gives you the exact budget, the exact pain and a named contact.

---

#### 4.22 Willingness-to-pay research

- **Reveals.** The acceptable price corridor *per segment* — which is often the deciding difference between two otherwise equal candidates.
- **Process.** (1) Run Van Westendorp's four questions per segment: too cheap (you would doubt quality) / cheap / expensive / too expensive to consider. [E — §7.1] (2) Compare corridors across segments. (3) **Triangulate against behaviour** — what they already pay, competitor prices that have survived years, ad longevity in the segment. [E — §7.1]
- **Tools.** Any survey tool; pricing-analysis calculators.
- **Limitations.** Stated preference. Does not optimise revenue on its own. The corpus is explicit that it must be triangulated — never use it as sole evidence. [E — §7.1]
- **Output.** Price corridor per segment plus the behavioural cross-check.

---

### Tier D — Experiments that manufacture evidence

---

#### 4.23 Landing-page smoke tests, fake doors and lead-magnet validation

- **Reveals.** Whether a segment responds to a *specific message* with a *specific action* — the closest thing to demand you can get before building anything.
- **Process.** (1) Pick which unknown you are testing, and choose the instrument accordingly: **demand unknown → fake door; market or message unknown → smoke test across several segments and messages; delivery or value capture unknown → concierge MVP.** [E — §6.2] (2) Build one page per segment with identical structure and different audience-specific promise. (3) Drive equal, comparable traffic to each. (4) Measure a costly action — email, call booked, deposit — never a click. (5) Decide in advance what result changes your mind.
- **Tools.** Any landing builder; a small ad budget; RU: Tilda, VK Ads, Yandex Direct.
- **Limitations.** Measures message response, not product love. Traffic quality dominates results — a bad channel will fail a good segment. Never take money or personal data you cannot honour; a fake door that collects emails must say what happens next. [S, ethical constraint]
- **Output.** Per-segment response rate on one costly action, with traffic source held constant.
- **Example finding.** Buffer validated with a "Plans and Pricing" button that led to an email signup rather than a product. Tesla validated its first car with $5,000 deposits. [E — §6.2]

---

#### 4.24 Manual outreach validation

- **Reveals.** Reachability, reply rate, and whether the pain statement lands — all at once, for free.
- **Process.** (1) Build a list of 30 named individuals in the segment — if you cannot, the segment has already failed the list test. (2) Write one message referencing a specific, observable fact about them. (3) Ask for a conversation, not a sale. (4) Measure reply rate and, more importantly, *what they say*. (5) Recruit users manually and one by one — Airbnb, Stripe and Pinterest all did; the point is not the users but what manual recruiting teaches you that a funnel never will. [E — §5.6]
- **Tools.** Email, LinkedIn, Telegram; whatever the segment actually uses.
- **Limitations.** Slow, unscalable by design, and subject to anti-spam law and platform rules. Comply with them.
- **Output.** Reply rate; a log of reactions; a verbatim objection list.
- **Example finding.** Zero replies from 30 well-researched messages is not a message problem. It is a reachability verdict.

---

#### 4.25 Paid ad tests for segment discrimination

- **Reveals.** Relative cost to reach each segment, and which angle earns attention — quickly, if you can afford it.
- **Process.** (1) One campaign per segment, identical creative structure, different audience-specific angle. (2) Hold budget and placement constant. (3) Measure cost per costly action, not per click. (4) Run long enough to leave the learning phase. (5) Follow the Bullseye discipline — 3–4 parallel cheap tests, then concentrate on the winner; the corpus repeatedly cites roughly a month and under ~$1,000 to make a channel decision. [E — §5.1]
- **Tools.** Meta, Google, LinkedIn (EN); VK Ads, Yandex Direct, Telegram Ads (RU).
- **Limitations.** Costs money. Platform targeting cannot express most good segment definitions, so you end up testing a proxy. Algorithmic delivery optimises toward whoever converts cheapest, which quietly re-segments your test for you. Never conclude "the segment is bad" from a failed ad test alone.
- **Output.** Cost per costly action by segment and angle; the winning angle's verbatim wording.

---

#### 4.26 Community observation (participant, not extractive)

- **Reveals.** Norms, vocabulary, status hierarchy, and whether this community can ever be an acquisition channel.
- **Process.** (1) Join and read for a fortnight before posting. (2) Answer questions with no link and no pitch. (3) Note which answers get thanked — that is proof of what the community values. (4) Only then consider whether a mention is welcome. [E — §5.3]
- **Tools.** The community itself.
- **Limitations.** Slow. Cannot be delegated credibly. Communities detect and punish extraction, and the punishment is permanent.
- **Output.** Norms note; vocabulary list; a viability verdict on the community as a channel.

---

### 4.27 Choosing methods: the minimum defensible pass

Nobody runs twenty-six methods. The minimum that produces a defensible answer, by situation: [S]

| Situation | Minimum method set |
|---|---|
| **Has paying customers** | 4.5 CRM/ABCDX → 4.1 switch interviews (5–10) → 4.6 PMF survey → 4.9 review mining |
| **Has users, no revenue** | 4.6 PMF survey → 4.4 support tickets → 4.2 interviews → 4.23 smoke test on price |
| **Pre-launch, has access to the market** | 4.2 interviews (10) → 4.13/4.14 community research → 4.10 competitor reviews → 4.24 manual outreach |
| **Pre-launch, no access** | 4.9/4.10 review mining → 4.18 search analysis → 4.20 ad-library → 4.13/4.14 habitat mapping → then buy access via 4.25 |
| **RU market, zero budget** | Inbound messages → forums and Telegram chats → reviews of own and competitors' products → competitors' content and comments + Wordstat/Google Trends [E — §8.4] |
| **Choosing between two segments** | 4.23 parallel smoke tests + 4.22 WTP per segment. Everything else is argument; these two are evidence. |

**Volume floor for a first pass** (RU practice, and a reasonable default anywhere): roughly 20–30 reviews, 10 call recordings, 10 search queries, plus a set of forum and social comments. [E — §8.5] Enough to be defensible; small enough to finish this week.

## §5 — Where to find target audiences: the habitat atlas

A **habitat** is a specific place where a defined audience is already gathered, indexed, or reachable. The concept comes from audience-intelligence practice: find the exact publications, podcasts, channels and accounts your audience consumes, then go there instead of buying broad reach. [E — §5.2]

**The standard this atlas enforces:** a habitat is only named when you can state *the specific instance*. "LinkedIn" is a platform. "These six LinkedIn creators, this Slack group, and the alumni list of this certification" is a habitat map. Platform names in an audience report are a sign the research stopped early. [S]

Each entry: **who lives there · signals to look for · how to research without spamming · free attraction possible? · best formats · risks.**

---

### Family 1 — Intent surfaces (people actively looking)

#### 5.1 Search engines (Google, Yandex)
- **Who lives there.** Problem-aware through most-aware audiences, self-selected by intent. The single largest source of high-intent B2C and B2B demand.
- **Signals.** Query phrasing (problem vs. solution vs. brand), commercial modifiers, seasonality, region. **Ads on the SERP = advertisers have validated conversion.** [E — §6.4]
- **Research without spamming.** Pure observation. Wordstat and Google Trends are free and RU-standard. [E — §8.7]
- **Free attraction?** Yes, but slowly. SEO is a 6–18 month asset, not a validation channel.
- **Formats.** Comparison pages, "alternatives to X," how-to content, calculators, local landing pages.
- **Risks.** Blind to new markets (zero volume is expected there) [E — §1.2] and to people who cannot name their problem (G6). Algorithm dependency — you do not set the rules. [E — §4.3]

#### 5.2 Marketplaces (Amazon, Wildberries, Ozon, Etsy, Yandex Market)
- **Who lives there.** Buyers in purchase mode, with a card open. Consumer-dominant, increasingly B2B for supplies.
- **Signals.** Category bestsellers, review volume and velocity, price bands, seasonality, "frequently bought together."
- **Research.** Read reviews for *purchase reason* and use context, not product opinion (4.11). Category analytics tools give demand by SKU. [E — §8.7]
- **Free attraction?** Partially — organic marketplace ranking is real but governed by the platform's rules and increasingly pay-to-play.
- **Formats.** Listing optimisation, bundles, review generation, off-platform content driving on-platform sales.
- **Risks.** The marketplace owns the customer relationship and the data. Margin compression. Sudden rule changes.

#### 5.3 Review sites and directories (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Otzovik, Yandex Maps, 2GIS)
- **Who lives there.** Solution-aware buyers comparing options — the most valuable and most contested audience state.
- **Signals.** Category leaders, review recency, reviewer job titles and company sizes, complaint clusters.
- **Research.** Review mining, focused on 3–4 star reviews. [E — §6.1] Read who the reviewers *are*, not just what they say.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — a free listing plus systematically requesting reviews from happy customers. For local business, Yandex Maps and 2GIS presence is close to mandatory.
- **Formats.** Profile completeness, review velocity, comparison pages, responding publicly to negative reviews.
- **Risks.** B2B review sites run on paid placement; organic visibility is limited. Incentivised reviews distort the data you are mining.

#### 5.4 App stores (App Store, Google Play, RuStore)
- **Who lives there.** Consumers and prosumers browsing by category and searching by problem.
- **Signals.** Category ranking movement, review themes, update cadence of competitors, keyword fields.
- **Research.** 1-star reviews for churn triggers; 3-star for unmet needs; competitor reply tone for what they consider acceptable (4.12).
- **Free attraction?** Yes — ASO is genuine organic acquisition, and one of the few places a small player can still out-rank a large one on a specific query.
- **Formats.** Store listing copy, screenshots, review responses, seasonal updates.
- **Risks.** Discovery is winner-take-most. Platform policy risk. Ratings are a hostage.

#### 5.5 Job boards (hh.ru, LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed)
- **Who lives there.** Companies publicly announcing a funded problem, with a date and a named contact.
- **Signals.** A role whose duties describe work your product or service replaces or supports. Salary figures give you the budget. Repeated reposting means they cannot fill it — an even stronger signal. [E — §5.5]
- **Research.** Systematic search on duty keywords, not job titles. Titles vary; duties do not.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, entirely — this is free research and free outbound targeting.
- **Formats.** Direct outreach referencing the vacancy; content addressed to the problem the vacancy admits.
- **Risks.** Approach with care: implying "don't hire, buy us" reads as insulting unless framed around helping the new hire succeed. The signal is visible to competitors too.

#### 5.6 Tender and public procurement platforms
- **Who lives there.** Organisations with approved budget, published requirements and a legal deadline. The highest-intent audience that exists, and the most bureaucratic.
- **Signals.** Recurring buyers in your category; requirement language; incumbent suppliers; contract values.
- **Research.** Read *past* awards to learn who buys, at what price, and from whom. That is a free competitive and pricing intelligence archive.
- **Free attraction?** The information is free. Participation carries real compliance cost.
- **Formats.** Bidding; pre-tender relationship building; subcontracting to existing winners.
- **Risks.** Long cycles, heavy documentation, price-dominant selection, payment terms that strain small suppliers. [A — this habitat is included on reasoning; the corpus contains no dedicated source on it]

### Family 2 — Conversation surfaces (people talking to each other)

#### 5.7 Reddit
- **Who lives there.** EN-dominant, skewing technical, consumer-hobbyist and professional-anonymous. Over 100,000 active communities.
- **Signals.** Four conversation types worth isolating: **Pain Points · Solution Requests · Money Talk · Hot Discussions.** [E — §5.3] Repeat questions are the strongest signal of all.
- **Research.** Build a keyword list with synonyms, abbreviations and competitor names; run each through community search and Google. [E — §5.3] Read comments over posts.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — but only through genuine contribution before any promotion. [E — §5.3]
- **Formats.** Answering questions with no link; AMAs where permitted; a free tool that solves the recurring question.
- **Risks.** Self-promotion is detected and punished, often permanently. Moderator rules vary per community and must be read.

#### 5.8 Telegram channels and chats
- **Who lives there.** The primary RU/CIS professional and interest habitat. People self-select by subscribing, which makes interest legible. [E — §8.6]
- **Signals.** Subscriber count, post reach, and above all **comment activity** — a big channel with dead comments is a broadcast, not a community.
- **Research.** TGStat as the working catalogue with per-channel statistics. [E — §8.6] Read the last 200 messages; note who answers questions.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, and this is the RU zero-budget backbone: contributing in topical chats, **взаимопиар** (cross-promotion between comparable channels), and «перелив» — pulling audience from vc.ru, Dzen, VK and Shorts/Reels into a channel. [E — §8.6]
- **Formats.** Own channel with a narrow topic; expert answers in chats; guest posts; joint AMAs; folder-based cross-promotion.
- **Risks.** Closed chats are invisible from outside. Native search is weak. Paid channel placements are a grey market with inflated metrics.

#### 5.9 Discord servers
- **Who lives there.** Gaming, crypto, creator, developer and increasingly SaaS-power-user communities. Younger, real-time, high-context.
- **Signals.** Member count vs. daily active; channel structure; whether the server is vendor-run or independent.
- **Research.** Join, read history, identify which channels carry problem talk rather than chatter.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, with high time cost — Discord rewards presence, not campaigns.
- **Formats.** Answering in help channels; hosting an event; a bot that provides genuine utility.
- **Risks.** Ephemeral, unsearchable, extremely norm-sensitive. Impossible to delegate credibly.

#### 5.10 Slack communities (professional)
- **Who lives there.** B2B professionals by role — often the highest-value, hardest-to-reach audience available.
- **Signals.** Whether joining requires vetting (vetted = higher value); channel activity by topic; who answers.
- **Research.** Apply, read, contribute. Many require a referral, which is itself a density signal.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, and disproportionately effective — but "no selling" is usually an explicit rule.
- **Formats.** Expert answers; sharing a resource with no gate; DM only after public value.
- **Risks.** Rules are strictly enforced. One bad pitch ends access to a community you cannot re-enter.

#### 5.11 Industry forums and niche communities
- **Who lives there.** Practitioners in mature or technical fields — often older, often the actual decision-makers, and often ignored by modern marketing.
- **Signals.** Thread longevity, archive depth, the presence of a recognised expert class.
- **Research.** Search the archive. A ten-year-old thread still receiving replies is a permanent unmet need.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, and competition is unusually low because forums are unfashionable.
- **Formats.** Long technical answers; signature links where permitted; sponsoring the forum itself.
- **Risks.** Small absolute numbers. Insular cultures. Some are dying and their traffic is nostalgia.

#### 5.12 vc.ru, Habr, Dzen (RU media-communities)
- **Who lives there.** RU founders, marketers, product people, developers. vc.ru comments are frequently more informative than the articles. [E — §8.4]
- **Signals.** Which posts attract argument rather than applause. Who argues competently in comments — those are your experts and sometimes your buyers.
- **Research.** Read comment sections on competitor and category posts. Habr for technical audiences.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — publishing is free and distribution is algorithmic, plus «перелив» into Telegram. [E — §8.6]
- **Formats.** Case studies with real numbers, teardown posts, methodology posts, failure post-mortems (which outperform success stories).
- **Risks.** Harsh audiences that punish thin content publicly. Reputational risk is real and permanent.

#### 5.13 Stack Overflow and technical Q&A
- **Who lives there.** Developers with a specific problem, right now, at the moment of maximum need.
- **Signals.** Question frequency by tag; unanswered questions; workaround answers with many upvotes (a lead-user signal, G1).
- **Research.** Tag-based frequency analysis; read the accepted answers for what people build instead of buying.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, but strictly through genuinely useful answers.
- **Formats.** High-quality answers; open-source tooling; documentation that ranks.
- **Risks.** Overt promotion is removed. Developers are structurally resistant to marketing and often not the budget holder — map the buying group. [E — §6.5]

#### 5.14 GitHub
- **Who lives there.** Developers, technical founders, and the people building the workarounds that define your market (G1).
- **Signals.** Repos that solve your problem badly; issue threads asking for features; stars on "awesome-X" lists; forks of internal tooling.
- **Research.** Search for the problem, not the product. Read issues, not READMEs.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — open source is the developer world's content marketing.
- **Formats.** A genuinely useful open tool; contributing to adjacent projects; technical writing.
- **Risks.** Long horizon. Attracts users, not necessarily buyers. Maintenance obligations are permanent.

### Family 3 — Broadcast surfaces (people consuming)

#### 5.15 YouTube
- **Who lives there.** Almost everyone, segmented by search and by channel subscription. Strong for both problem-aware and solution-aware states.
- **Signals.** Search volume on problem phrases; comment objections; which competitor channels exist and how they monetise.
- **Research.** Comment mining on the top explainer videos (4.15). Sort comments by top-voted — the crowd ranks the objections for you.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, and it compounds: YouTube is a search engine with a recommendation engine attached, so old videos keep working.
- **Formats.** Problem-first tutorials, teardowns, comparisons, "how we did X" case videos.
- **Risks.** High production cost per unit. Requires a person willing to be on camera — a constraint that must be checked *before* recommending it (1.19).

#### 5.16 Podcasts
- **Who lives there.** Professional and interest audiences with high trust in the host — trust that transfers unusually well.
- **Signals.** Which shows your audience names unprompted in interviews. Guest lists reveal the segment's expert class.
- **Research.** Ask in interviews: "what do you listen to?" Then read the show's guest history.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — guesting is free, and a niche show with 2,000 highly-targeted listeners outperforms a general show with 200,000.
- **Formats.** Guesting; sponsoring a niche show; starting one only if the audience is dense enough to justify it.
- **Risks.** Hard to attribute. Slow. Reach is small in absolute terms — the value is trust density, not volume.

#### 5.17 Newsletters
- **Who lives there.** Self-selected professional audiences with genuine attention, and an owner who can be partnered with directly.
- **Signals.** Which newsletters the audience names. Whether the newsletter sells sponsorship (and to whom — that reveals who else targets this audience).
- **Research.** Subscribe. Read the sponsors: they are your competitors' validated audience bets.
- **Free attraction?** Partially — guest contributions, cross-promotions, being featured. Sponsorship is paid but often cheap relative to precision.
- **Formats.** Sponsorship, guest issues, swaps, being cited.
- **Risks.** Small lists. Owner-dependent. Hard to verify claimed open rates.

#### 5.18 LinkedIn
- **Who lives there.** B2B professionals, targetable by role, company, size and seniority. The most precisely targetable B2B habitat that exists.
- **Signals.** Job changes (a trigger event) [E — §5.5]; which posts in your category get engagement from *buyers* rather than from peers — a distinction most people never check.
- **Research.** Search by role and company attributes; read comment sections on category posts.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — organic posting plus conversation. Currently one of the better organic reach opportunities in B2B.
- **Formats.** Founder-led content; case studies; commenting substantively on prospects' posts; light, personalised outreach.
- **Risks.** Peer-applause trap: content that performs among competitors and never reaches buyers. Automated outreach damages the account and the brand.

#### 5.19 Instagram
- **Who lives there.** Consumer, local, aesthetic, lifestyle and personal-brand audiences. Strong for local services.
- **Signals.** Geotag activity for local; hashtag communities; which competitor accounts have engaged rather than large followings.
- **Research.** Read comments and saved-post patterns; look at who local competitors' engaged followers are.
- **Free attraction?** Yes for local and visual categories; hard for abstract B2B.
- **Formats.** Before/after, process, Reels, local geotagged content, DM conversations.
- **Risks.** Reach volatility. Follower count correlates weakly with revenue. Content treadmill with no archive value.

#### 5.20 TikTok
- **Who lives there.** Broad and rapidly broadening consumer audiences; increasingly used as a search engine by younger users.
- **Signals.** Whether category content exists and gets views; comment sections asking "where do I get this."
- **Research.** Search your problem phrase as a query; read the comments on the top results.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — the highest organic-reach ceiling of any current platform for a standing start.
- **Formats.** Problem-first hooks, demonstrations, myth-busting, founder-face content.
- **Risks.** Very short attention half-life. Poor fit for considered high-ticket purchases. Regulatory and availability risk varies by market.

#### 5.21 VK
- **Who lives there.** RU mass consumer, regional, and interest-group audiences. Groups remain a genuine community layer.
- **Signals.** Group size and post engagement; VK people-search filters give a free audience-size estimate by parameter. [E — §8.7]
- **Research.** Study the community of a comparable business *in another district or city* and transpose — a documented RU technique for profiling a local audience you cannot yet reach. [E — §8.8]
- **Free attraction?** Yes — groups, content, and participation in existing communities.
- **Formats.** Group content, community participation, targeted posts, VK Ads for cheap testing.
- **Risks.** Demographic skew varies sharply by region and category. Declining relevance for some professional audiences.

#### 5.22 Facebook groups
- **Who lives there.** Interest, expat, professional and local communities; still dominant in some geographies and diasporas.
- **Signals.** Group activity vs. size; admin strictness; recurring question threads.
- **Research.** Join, search the group's history for your problem phrase.
- **Free attraction?** Yes, subject to admin rules, which are usually stricter than they look.
- **Formats.** Answering questions; admin partnership; group-exclusive resources.
- **Risks.** Declining organic reach; admin dependency; regional relevance varies enormously.

#### 5.23 Medium and EN publishing platforms
- **Who lives there.** EN professional readers arriving mostly via search and topic feeds; strong for methodology and framework content.
- **Signals.** Which publications cover your topic; response counts (more informative than claps).
- **Research.** Read the responses on the top-ranked articles for your problem — objections, gaps, and requests.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — publishing is free; distribution is partly algorithmic and partly borrowed from publications.
- **Formats.** Framework posts, teardowns, contrarian analyses, republished founder content.
- **Risks.** Paywall and distribution changes. Weak commercial intent. Attribution is poor.

### Family 4 — Institutional and offline surfaces

#### 5.24 Professional associations and licensing bodies
- **Who lives there.** Regulated professions, verticals, and trades — pre-qualified, pre-listed, and pre-networked.
- **Signals.** Member directories (often public), certification registers, event calendars, member magazines.
- **Research.** These are the **formalized attributes** of ABCDX made public: role, licence, certification, industry code. [E — §4.2]
- **Free attraction?** Partially — speaking, contributing to the member publication, and committee participation are free; membership and sponsorship are not.
- **Formats.** Speaking slots, member-magazine articles, association partnerships, member discounts.
- **Risks.** Slow, relationship-driven, gatekept by long-standing incumbents.

#### 5.25 Conferences and events
- **Who lives there.** The segment's decision-makers and its expert class, physically concentrated for two days.
- **Signals.** **The speaker list is a free map of the segment's influencers. The sponsor list is a free map of who else is selling to them and at what level of commitment.** [S]
- **Research.** You do not need to attend to learn from the agenda, the speaker list and the sponsor tiers. That research is free.
- **Free attraction?** Attending is not free; speaking, side events, and hallway conversations are cheap relative to booths.
- **Formats.** Speaking, workshops, side dinners, "we're here, coffee?" outreach in the week before.
- **Risks.** High cost and poor attribution. Booths mostly generate lists, not deals.

#### 5.26 Offline communities and local networks
- **Who lives there.** Local business owners, trades, hobbyists, parents, and the segments least well-served by digital marketing.
- **Signals.** Business breakfasts, chambers of commerce, coworking spaces, parent groups, sports clubs.
- **Research.** Ask a customer where they go. Then go.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — this is the original free channel, and it still works for high-trust local services.
- **Formats.** Referral relationships, talks, partnerships with complementary local businesses.
- **Risks.** Does not scale, cannot be delegated, and returns compound only over years.

#### 5.27 Local business directories and maps
- **Who lives there.** Local high-intent demand, at the moment of need.
- **Signals.** Review counts and recency of local competitors; category saturation within the radius; the radius itself is the segmentation axis. [E — §8.8]
- **Research.** Map competitors by radius; read their reviews for local-specific complaints.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — a complete Yandex Maps / 2GIS / Google Business profile with active review management is free and non-optional for local business.
- **Formats.** Profile completeness, photos, review velocity, replies, local posts.
- **Risks.** Review manipulation by competitors; ranking opacity.

#### 5.28 Courses, bootcamps and educational communities
- **Who lives there.** People who have just paid money to solve a problem — proving willingness to pay *and* problem awareness simultaneously.
- **Signals.** Which courses exist for your problem, what they cost, how full their cohorts are, what their alumni communities discuss.
- **Research.** Course curricula are a free map of what the segment knows it does not know. Alumni chats are a free habitat.
- **Free attraction?** Partially — guest lectures, alumni-community participation, and partnerships with course authors.
- **Formats.** Guest teaching, being the recommended tool, alumni offers, co-created materials.
- **Risks.** Course audiences skew toward learning rather than doing; buying intent for *tools* can be much lower than it appears (see 2.16, aspirational audiences).

### Family 5 — Borrowed-audience surfaces

#### 5.29 Competitor audiences
- **Who lives there.** People already qualified by someone else's marketing.
- **Signals.** Who comments, reviews, follows, and complains. Complaint threads are Blue Ocean Tier 1 in public. [E — §1.3]
- **Research.** Read their reviews (4.10), their comment sections, their community. Note who they *stopped* marketing to (4.20).
- **Free attraction?** Yes — comparison content, alternatives pages, and answering their unanswered questions.
- **Formats.** "Alternatives to X" pages, migration guides, comparison content, being present where they are absent.
- **Risks.** Reactive positioning. Repositioning by direct comparison invites discounting and legal attention; say something that changes the buyer's mind about the category, not something that merely attacks a rival.

#### 5.30 Influencer and creator audiences
- **Who lives there.** Pre-aggregated, trust-transferring audiences, segmented by the creator's topic.
- **Signals.** Whether the creator's audience is *buyers* or *peers* — the single most important check, and the one most often skipped. Comment quality beats follower count.
- **Research.** Ask your interviewees whose content they never skip. [E — §5.2] That answer beats any influencer-discovery tool.
- **Free attraction?** Sometimes — genuine relationships, co-created content, being a useful source for their content.
- **Formats.** Collaborations, expert commentary, tool gifting, affiliate arrangements.
- **Risks.** Inflated metrics. Audience mismatch. One-off spikes with no compounding.

#### 5.31 Paid ad libraries (as research, not as media)
- **Who lives there.** Nobody — this is an intelligence surface, listed here because it maps everyone else's audience bets.
- **Signals.** **Ads running 60–90+ days are almost certainly profitable.** [E — §5.4] The angles in long-runners are validated angles.
- **Research.** Sort by start date; extract angle, promise and implied audience; note abandoned segments.
- **Free attraction?** The research is free; the channel is not.
- **Formats.** N/A — this feeds strategy.
- **Risks.** Proxy, not proof. Coverage limited to platforms with public libraries.

#### 5.32 Freelance platforms (Upwork, Kwork, FL.ru)
- **Who lives there.** Buyers publishing their problem, budget and deadline in writing, voluntarily.
- **Signals.** Repeated job types = an unserved productisable need. Budget ranges reveal willingness to pay by segment. Client history reveals repeat buyers.
- **Research.** Read job posts as a demand feed, whether or not you bid. This is one of the cleanest free demand datasets available in any market.
- **Free attraction?** Browsing is free; participation costs fees or connects.
- **Formats.** Bidding; productising the most repeated request; content aimed at people about to post such a job.
- **Risks.** Price-competitive, race-to-the-bottom dynamics. Platform-owned client relationships.

#### 5.33 Product Hunt and launch platforms
- **Who lives there.** Early adopters, builders, and other founders — rarely the mainstream buyer.
- **Signals.** Which categories get traction; comment questions revealing objections.
- **Research.** Read comments on competitor launches for objections, pre-sorted.
- **Free attraction?** Yes for a launch spike; almost never for sustained acquisition.
- **Formats.** Launch, maker comments, follow-up content.
- **Risks.** **The classic trap:** a Product Hunt audience is not your market unless your market is founders. A successful launch that produces no retained users is a very convincing false positive.

#### 5.34 SaaS directories and app marketplaces (integration ecosystems)
- **Who lives there.** Solution-aware buyers already using an adjacent product — the highest-intent B2B software audience there is.
- **Signals.** Which platform's marketplace your buyers already live in; which integrations competitors list.
- **Research.** Read the reviews on the marketplace listing, not the vendor site.
- **Free attraction?** Yes — listings are usually free and the traffic is pre-qualified by the host platform's own audience.
- **Formats.** Integration listing, co-marketing with the platform, docs that rank inside the ecosystem.
- **Risks.** Platform dependency in its purest form. The host can compete with you at any time.

### 5.35 Building a habitat map: the procedure

Three passes, and a rule. [S]

**Pass 1 — Ask.** In every interview: "What do you read every week? Which chats are you in? Whose posts do you never skip? Where did you look last time you had this problem?" This outperforms every tool.

**Pass 2 — Trace.** Follow the answers. Every named venue has a members list, a sponsor list, a comment section, and neighbours.

**Pass 3 — Verify density.** For each candidate habitat record: **name · size · activity level · rules on vendor participation · who the recognised voices are · route to entry · free or paid.**

**The rule:** a habitat map is not finished until you can answer *"if I had to reach 100 of these people by Friday, exactly where would I go?"* with a list of named places and a first action for each. Anything less is a platform inventory. [S]

## §6 — Free vs paid acquisition

### 6.1 What "free" actually costs

There is no free acquisition. There is acquisition paid for in **time, credibility, and optionality** instead of cash. [S] The honest question is never "is it free?" but **"which currency can this business actually spend, and how long can it wait for a signal?"**

Three properties decide everything:

| Property | Question | Why it decides |
|---|---|---|
| **Time-to-signal** | How long before you know it is working? | A business with three months of runway cannot use a channel with a nine-month signal, regardless of its eventual ROI. |
| **Asset requirement** | What must exist first? | Free channels are usually free *given an asset* — an audience, a portfolio, a network, a domain, a founder who will publish. |
| **Compounding** | Does the work keep paying? | Content and SEO compound. Outreach and ads do not. This is the real trade against paid. |

**The founder's-time trap:** free channels are routinely recommended to businesses whose founder has no spare hours, and they fail — not because the channel was wrong but because the currency was unavailable. Always price the founder's time before calling a channel free. [S]

**The regime check:** early customers overwhelmingly come from founder-led sales and founder-led content, not from ads or SEO authority. [E — §5.7] Manual, unscalable recruitment is the documented default for the first users — Airbnb, Stripe and Pinterest all did it. [E — §5.6] Do not recommend a scalable channel to a business that does not yet know who it is selling to.

### 6.2 The nineteen methods

Each: **when it works · when it does not · required assets · time-to-signal · audience fit · best use · common mistakes.**

---

#### M1 — Organic content (blog, articles, posts)
- **Works when.** The audience is solution-aware and searches or reads; the topic has depth; you can publish consistently for months.
- **Fails when.** The audience is unaware and does not read; the founder cannot sustain cadence; the topic is thin.
- **Assets.** Subject expertise, a writer, a place to publish, patience.
- **Time-to-signal.** 2–6 months for direction; 6–18 for compounding.
- **Fit.** Problem-aware and solution-aware; B2B strongly; considered B2C.
- **Best use.** Answering the repeat question a community asks every month (G13).
- **Mistakes.** Writing for peers instead of buyers. Measuring traffic instead of qualified conversations. Stopping at month three, which is exactly when it starts working.

#### M2 — SEO
- **Works when.** Real search volume exists; you can build or already have domain credibility; the horizon is long.
- **Fails when.** New market with no volume [E — §1.2]; brand-new domain against entrenched incumbents; you need revenue this quarter.
- **Assets.** A site, content capacity, technical basics, 6–18 months.
- **Time-to-signal.** 4–12 months.
- **Fit.** Solution-aware and most-aware; high-intent commercial queries. [E — §6.4]
- **Best use.** Comparison and "alternatives to X" pages, which capture buyers already in motion.
- **Mistakes.** Chasing volume over intent. [E — §6.4] Treating SEO as validation — it is far too slow to test a segment hypothesis.

#### M3 — Community participation
- **Works when.** Dense communities exist (1.18); the founder can genuinely contribute; the norms permit eventual mention.
- **Fails when.** The founder cannot answer questions credibly; the community bans vendors; the segment is not gathered anywhere.
- **Assets.** Genuine expertise, time, patience, a real account with a history.
- **Time-to-signal.** 2–8 weeks for the first conversations.
- **Fit.** Niche B2B, technical audiences, RU/CIS Telegram, EN Reddit/Slack/Discord.
- **Best use.** Give value first, build trust, mention only when genuinely relevant. [E — §5.3]
- **Mistakes.** Extraction. Posting a link in week one. Delegating to someone with no standing — the community can tell within two messages.

#### M4 — Partnerships (non-affiliate)
- **Works when.** Someone else already serves your audience without competing; the value exchange is genuinely two-way.
- **Fails when.** You have nothing to offer yet; the partner's audience is not actually yours; the arrangement is one-sided.
- **Assets.** Something the partner wants — audience, product, expertise, or their client's problem solved.
- **Time-to-signal.** 3–8 weeks per partnership.
- **Fit.** B2B services, complementary tools, local business.
- **Best use.** Adjacent service providers selling to the identical buyer at a different moment.
- **Mistakes.** Asking before giving. Partnering by audience *size* rather than audience *overlap*.

#### M5 — Referral mechanics
- **Works when.** The audience talks to each other (1.17); the result is visible; the referral is socially safe to make.
- **Fails when.** The category is embarrassing or competitively sensitive; the product is not yet good enough that a referral is safe.
- **Assets.** Satisfied customers, a moment of visible success, an easy ask.
- **Time-to-signal.** Immediate once there are happy customers.
- **Fit.** Dense professional communities; local services; anything Aulet-shaped (strong word of mouth between end users). [E — §1.1]
- **Best use.** Ask at the moment of proven value, and ask for a *specific* introduction, not a general one.
- **Mistakes.** Building an incentive scheme before the product earns referrals. Asking too early — a referral before value destroys the relationship you are borrowing.

#### M6 — Cold outreach
- **Works when.** The segment is listable (1.6); there is a real trigger to reference (G7); the message is specific.
- **Fails when.** You cannot build the list; the message is generic; the segment is drowning in outreach.
- **Assets.** A list, a reason to write, a tolerance for silence, legal compliance.
- **Time-to-signal.** 1–3 weeks.
- **Fit.** B2B; high-ticket; trigger-event audiences.
- **Best use.** Manual recruitment of the first users, one by one — the point being what it teaches you. [E — §5.6]
- **Mistakes.** Automating before a message works manually. Ignoring anti-spam law and platform rules. Treating a 0% reply rate as a copy problem when it is a reachability verdict.

#### M7 — Lead magnets
- **Works when.** The audience has a task they need done now and your artefact does part of it.
- **Fails when.** The magnet is generic (a "guide"); it attracts the wrong segment; there is nothing behind it.
- **Assets.** Something genuinely useful, a delivery mechanism, a follow-up.
- **Time-to-signal.** 1–4 weeks.
- **Fit.** Problem-aware and solution-aware; strongest where a template, calculator or checklist replaces real work.
- **Best use.** Make it *the thing they were about to build themselves* (G1).
- **Mistakes.** Optimising for volume of emails. A magnet so broad it recruits an audience you cannot sell to — a list of the wrong people is a liability, not an asset.

#### M8 — Public teardowns and audits
- **Works when.** The audience's work is public; the critique is generous and specific; you can demonstrate rather than assert.
- **Fails when.** The critique is cruel, or the subject is private, or you lack standing.
- **Assets.** Real expertise, judgement about tone, willingness to be judged in return.
- **Time-to-signal.** Days to weeks — one of the fastest.
- **Fit.** Design, marketing, UX, code, copy, positioning — anything visible.
- **Best use.** Teardown of a category, not of a person; or of a volunteer.
- **Mistakes.** Punching down. Teardowns that showcase cleverness rather than a repeatable method — the audience must be able to imagine you doing it for them.

#### M9 — Expert commentary
- **Works when.** Questions are being asked publicly and you can answer better than anyone (5.7, 5.13, 5.15).
- **Fails when.** The answer requires context you cannot fit in the format, or the venue punishes vendors.
- **Assets.** Expertise, presence, speed.
- **Time-to-signal.** Days.
- **Fit.** Every habitat with a comment section.
- **Best use.** The top-voted objection under a competitor's content (4.15).
- **Mistakes.** Answering with a link. Answering questions your buyer does not have, in venues your buyer does not read.

#### M10 — Webinars and live sessions
- **Works when.** The topic needs demonstration; the audience will book time; you can fill the room from an existing habitat.
- **Fails when.** You have no audience to invite and no partner to borrow one from — the classic empty-webinar failure.
- **Assets.** A list or a partner, a topic worth an hour, presentation ability.
- **Time-to-signal.** 2–6 weeks per session.
- **Fit.** B2B considered purchases; expert products; complex offers.
- **Best use.** Co-hosted with a partner who supplies the audience while you supply the content.
- **Mistakes.** Running a webinar with no distribution plan. Pitching for fifty minutes. Choosing a topic that interests you rather than one that solves their Friday problem.

#### M11 — Telegram content (RU/CIS)
- **Works when.** The audience lives on Telegram (most RU professional segments do) [E — §8.6]; you can post consistently; the topic is narrow enough to be worth subscribing to.
- **Fails when.** The channel is broad and undifferentiated; there is no cross-promotion route.
- **Assets.** A narrow topic, cadence, and a starting audience from somewhere else.
- **Time-to-signal.** 4–12 weeks.
- **Fit.** RU B2B, RU expert products, RU services.
- **Best use.** Combine own-channel content with **взаимопиар** and «перелив» from vc.ru, Dzen, VK and Shorts/Reels. [E — §8.6]
- **Mistakes.** Buying placements in channels with inflated metrics. Launching a channel with no plan for the first 100 subscribers.

#### M12 — YouTube content
- **Works when.** The problem benefits from demonstration; someone will appear on camera; the topic has search demand.
- **Fails when.** Nobody will be on camera — check this before recommending it (1.19).
- **Assets.** A face, a topic, minimum production capability, persistence past the first ten videos.
- **Time-to-signal.** 3–9 months.
- **Fit.** Both B2C and B2B, especially where a process must be seen.
- **Best use.** Problem-phrase-titled tutorials that keep ranking for years.
- **Mistakes.** Optimising for subscribers instead of for the searcher with the problem. Quitting at video eight.

#### M13 — Founder-led content
- **Works when.** The founder has a real point of view, standing in the segment, and will sustain it.
- **Fails when.** It is delegated to a ghostwriter with no access to the founder's actual thinking — the audience detects the hollowness.
- **Assets.** The founder's time and willingness to be visible.
- **Time-to-signal.** 4–12 weeks for conversations.
- **Fit.** B2B, expert products, agencies, early-stage anything.
- **Best use.** The documented flywheel — sales calls generate the content topics, content generates the inbound, the loop compounds. [E — §5.7]
- **Mistakes.** Talking about the product instead of the customer's problem. Peer-applause content that never reaches a buyer.

#### M14 — Case studies
- **Works when.** You have results and permission; the reader can see themselves in the subject.
- **Fails when.** The case subject is nothing like the target segment, or the numbers are absent.
- **Assets.** A real result, a willing client, specifics.
- **Time-to-signal.** Immediate — case studies convert existing traffic rather than creating it.
- **Fit.** B2B, services, high-ticket. Frequently the deciding asset for trust (1.15).
- **Best use.** One case study per target segment, in that segment's own vocabulary.
- **Mistakes.** Anonymising until the case is unrecognisable. Leading with your process instead of their situation. Never fabricate one — a fabricated case is a business-ending risk, not a marketing shortcut.

#### M15 — Guest posts and cross-publishing
- **Works when.** Publications your audience actually reads accept contributions; you have something non-generic to say.
- **Fails when.** The publication's audience is not yours; the piece is a thinly veiled ad.
- **Assets.** A distinctive angle, writing capability, a relationship.
- **Time-to-signal.** 2–8 weeks.
- **Fit.** B2B, RU media-communities (vc.ru, Habr), EN industry publications.
- **Best use.** The specific publication named by interviewees, not the biggest one.
- **Mistakes.** Choosing outlets by traffic instead of by audience overlap. Writing the same piece everywhere.

#### M16 — Marketplace and directory listings
- **Works when.** Your category has marketplaces; buyers search there natively.
- **Fails when.** The category has no marketplace, or the platform's economics destroy your margin.
- **Assets.** A listing, reviews, competitive pricing, operational capacity.
- **Time-to-signal.** 1–6 weeks.
- **Fit.** Products, local services, SaaS with an ecosystem (5.34).
- **Best use.** Integration marketplaces of platforms your buyers already use — pre-qualified traffic at zero cost.
- **Mistakes.** Treating a listing as complete when it is created. Ignoring review velocity, which is the actual ranking mechanic.

#### M17 — Product-led growth
- **Works when.** The product delivers value fast, alone, without setup or a salesperson; the market is broad; time-to-value is short. [E — §4.3]
- **Fails when.** Value requires configuration, data, or a team; the buyer is not the user; the segment is small and high-ticket.
- **Assets.** A product that works unaided, onboarding, self-serve billing.
- **Time-to-signal.** 4–12 weeks.
- **Fit.** Broad-market software with short time-to-value.
- **Best use.** Where the free tier does a genuinely complete small job.
- **Mistakes.** Bolting a free tier onto a product needing a two-week implementation. Ignoring the product-channel fit constraint: the product must be *built* for the channel, not adapted to it afterwards. [E — §4.3]

#### M18 — Viral loops
- **Works when.** Use of the product naturally exposes non-users; the market is broad; the cycle is short. [E — §4.3]
- **Fails when.** Use is private; the market is narrow; the loop is bolted on rather than intrinsic.
- **Assets.** A collaboration or output-sharing mechanic that exists for the user's benefit, not yours.
- **Time-to-signal.** 4–12 weeks once instrumented.
- **Fit.** Collaboration tools, content creation, anything with a shareable artefact.
- **Best use.** Where sharing helps the sharer — the only loops that survive.
- **Mistakes.** Expecting virality in a niche B2B market. Incentivised sharing that produces junk signups.

#### M19 — Affiliate and partner programmes
- **Works when.** Margins support commission; the sales cycle is short enough for partners to see money; partners with your audience exist.
- **Fails when.** Payouts are too slow or too small relative to the partner's effort; the product is hard to explain.
- **Assets.** Tracking, margin, materials, and someone to actually manage partners.
- **Time-to-signal.** 2–6 months.
- **Fit.** Info products, SaaS with clear value, e-commerce.
- **Best use.** Partners who already sell something adjacent to the same buyer.
- **Mistakes.** Recruiting many partners and activating none. Setting commission from your own accounting rather than from the partner's earnings-per-effort.

### 6.3 Choosing: the decision rule

Run this in order. Stop at the first honest answer. [S]

1. **Is the segment gathered somewhere dense?** (1.18) → **M3 community participation** plus **M11/M1 content in that venue**. This is the cheapest real acquisition there is.
2. **Is the segment listable with observable triggers?** (1.6, 2.9) → **M6 cold outreach** on trigger events. Free, fast signal, immediate learning.
3. **Does the segment search for the problem?** (1.10) → **M1 + M2 content and SEO** — but only if you can wait 4–12 months.
4. **Does the founder have standing in the segment?** (1.21) → **M13 founder-led content** and the sales-call-to-content flywheel. [E — §5.7]
5. **Does someone else already own this audience?** → **M4 partnerships**, **M15 guest publishing**, **M10 co-hosted webinars**.
6. **None of the above, and there is budget?** → **paid tests** to buy the access you cannot earn (4.25), then re-run this list once you know who converts.
7. **None of the above, and there is no budget?** → **the segment is closed to you today.** Say so plainly, and either change segment or name the asset that must be built first. This is a legitimate and frequently correct answer.

### 6.4 When free acquisition is *not* realistic — say it plainly

Free acquisition fails predictably in these situations, and predicting it is more valuable than a channel list: [S]

| Situation | Why free fails | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Audience is unaware of the problem (1.14) | You must fund three rungs of education [E — §8.2] | Narrow to a problem-aware sub-segment, or budget for the education |
| Audience is scattered with no dense venue (1.18) | Content reaches statistical noise | Paid targeting, or change segment |
| Founder has no time and will not appear publicly | The currency free channels require is unavailable | Paid, or outsourced outbound with a bought list |
| Purchase is rare and impulse-driven | No compounding surface to build on | Search and marketplace presence only |
| Segment is saturated with high-authority incumbents | Organic ceiling is set by their domain and their years | Attack a sub-segment they ignore, or buy the traffic |
| Runway is under three months | Every free channel's time-to-signal exceeds the runway | Paid or outbound, immediately |
| The community explicitly bans vendors | The only compliant move is not to be there | Partner with a member, or sponsor the venue officially |

**The rule for the skill:** never answer "can this be done for free?" with encouragement. Answer with the channel, the required asset, the time-to-signal, and the condition under which the answer becomes no. [S]

## §7 — The audience scoring matrix

### 7.1 Why score at all

Scoring does not make the decision. It does three things that argument cannot: [S]

1. **It forces every segment through the same questions**, which surfaces the criterion nobody thought to check — usually reachability or trust.
2. **It makes disagreement specific.** "I prefer segment B" is unresolvable; "I score B a 4 on willingness to pay and you score it a 2" is a research task.
3. **It exposes missing evidence.** A cell you cannot score is a cell you have not researched.

The failure mode is scoring theatre — a table of confident numbers derived from nothing. The corpus's own guardrail is the fix: **define explicitly what a "1" and a "5" mean for each criterion, in writing, before scoring anything.** [E — §1.4] The skill goes one step further and requires a source per score.

### 7.2 The nineteen criteria

All on a 1–5 scale. Criteria marked **(inverse)** are scored so that 5 is always good — a short sales cycle scores 5, a long one scores 1.

| # | Criterion | 1 = | 5 = | Evidence that settles it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pain intensity** | Mild annoyance, tolerated indefinitely | Quantified loss they can state in money or hours | Verbatim complaints; review theme counts (4.9) |
| 2 | **Urgency** | No consequence to waiting a year | Dated deadline with a penalty | Trigger events (4.21); seasonality (4.18) |
| 3 | **Ability to pay** | No budget exists anywhere | Existing line item larger than your price | Job postings, funding, vendor logos (4.5, 4.21) |
| 4 | **Willingness to pay** | Category norm is "should be free" | Already pays comparable amounts without friction | Van Westendorp per segment + behavioural cross-check (4.22) |
| 5 | **Reachability** | No list, no channel, no targeting parameter | A named list or channel you can access this week | The list test (§3.4) |
| 6 | **Concentration** | Scattered, never meet | 3 venues cover most of the segment | Habitat map with sizes (5.35) |
| 7 | **Trust accessibility** | Needs certifications/references you lack | A portfolio link or a peer intro is enough | What proof buyers actually accepted (4.1, 4.3) |
| 8 | **Existing demand** | Nobody searches, nobody buys | Active search volume and paying incumbents | Wordstat/keyword data + competitor pricing (4.18) |
| 9 | **Competition level (inverse)** | Many funded specialists own it | Only generalists, serving it badly | Ad libraries, review sites, SERP (4.20, 4.10) |
| 10 | **Differentiation potential** | You would be the eleventh identical claim | You can say something true nobody else can | Swap test (1.23) |
| 11 | **Free acquisition potential** | No dense venue, no founder standing, no search | Dense communities plus a founder with standing | §6.3 decision rule |
| 12 | **Paid acquisition potential** | No targeting parameter matches the segment | Precise targeting plus affordable CPMs plus ARPU that funds it | Ad platform parameters + ARPU↔CAC check [E — §4.3] |
| 13 | **Content potential** | No questions being asked; nothing to say | Repeat questions asked monthly for years | Community repeat-question log (4.13) |
| 14 | **Sales cycle length (inverse)** | 9+ months, committee, procurement | Days, single decision-maker | Observed cycles (4.3); buying-group size [E — §6.5] |
| 15 | **Retention potential** | One-off, no reason to return | Recurring need embedded in a workflow | Frequency of need (1.5) |
| 16 | **Referral potential** | Private or competitively sensitive | Peers routinely share tools and are rewarded for it | "Who did you tell?" (4.1); word-of-mouth condition [E — §1.1] |
| 17 | **Strategic fit** | Off-mission; team has no interest or network | Founder's own network and expertise; on-mission | Aulet criterion 7 [E — §1.1] |
| 18 | **Positioning strength** | Message survives the swap test intact | Incumbents cannot copy it without breaking themselves | Competitor message homogeneity (1.23) |
| 19 | **Risk level (inverse)** | Regulatory, platform, concentration or reputational exposure | No structural exposure | Named risks with likelihood |

### 7.3 Weighting rules

Four rules, in priority order. [S]

**Rule 1 — Gates before weights.** Criteria 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 are the near-veto set (§1). **A score of 1 on any gate disqualifies the segment regardless of total**, and the report must say so explicitly rather than letting a high total hide it. A weighted average that averages away a fatal flaw is worse than no matrix.

**Rule 2 — Weights sum to 100.** Distributed across the nineteen criteria, per business model. No criterion above 15; no criterion below 2. Concentration above 15 means you have a checklist with extra steps; below 2 means you should have deleted the criterion.

**Rule 3 — Every score cites a source.** Format: `4 [4.9 review mining: 18/30 reviews cite this]` or `2 [assumption — no evidence found]`. **Assumption-scored cells must be visually distinct**, and the count of them is itself a finding: a matrix that is 70% assumptions is a research plan, not a recommendation.

**Rule 4 — Score relatively, not absolutely.** The question is never "is this a 4 in the abstract" but "is this better or worse than the other segments on the table." Score all segments on one criterion before moving to the next — column-wise, not row-wise. Row-wise scoring produces halo effects, where a segment you like gets 4s across the board.

### 7.4 Presets

Ten weighting profiles. Each reflects what actually kills businesses of that type. Full tables live in `../templates/scoring-matrix-template.md`; the reasoning is here.

| Preset | Heaviest weights | Why | Deliberately light |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B2B SaaS** | Ability to pay, retention, reachability, sales cycle | Retention is the model; CAC must be recovered over months, so cycle length and reachability decide viability | Content potential, referral |
| **Expert course / info product** | Willingness to pay, urgency, concentration, content potential | Aspiration converts unreliably (2.16); urgency and dense communities are what turn interest into purchase | Retention, sales cycle |
| **Agency / service** | Ability to pay, trust accessibility, referral, positioning strength | Services are sold on trust and referral; the ceiling is set by credibility, not by demand | Paid acquisition, virality |
| **Local business** | Concentration (geographic), existing demand, urgency, reachability | Radius caps everything [E — §8.8]; demand is local and mostly search- or map-driven | Differentiation, content |
| **Consumer product** | Existing demand, paid acquisition, competition, retention | Margin per unit is small; only channels that scale cheaply matter | Sales cycle, strategic fit |
| **High-ticket consulting** | Ability to pay, trust accessibility, positioning strength, strategic fit | One client changes the year; access and credibility dominate everything else | Free acquisition volume, retention |
| **Marketplace** | Concentration (both sides), existing demand, referral, retention | Liquidity is the product; density on both sides decides survival | Positioning, differentiation |
| **Mobile app** | Existing demand, paid acquisition, retention, competition | Store discovery is winner-take-most; retention determines whether paid installs are affordable | Trust accessibility, sales cycle |
| **Subscription (non-SaaS)** | Retention, frequency-driven pain, willingness to pay, referral | Churn is the only number that matters; the pain must recur | Differentiation, competition |
| **B2B services / outbound-led** | Reachability, urgency, trigger events, ability to pay | The whole motion depends on a list and a reason to write today | Content potential, virality |

**Preset selection rule:** if the business does not match a preset, pick the nearest and adjust no more than three weights, documenting each change and why. Inventing a bespoke weighting per engagement destroys comparability across engagements, which is most of the value of having a matrix at all. [S]

### 7.5 Reading the result

- **Do not rank by total alone.** Report the total, the gate check, and the assumption count together. A 3.9 with two assumptions beats a 4.3 with eleven.
- **A tie is a signal, not a problem.** Two segments within ~0.3 of each other are not distinguishable by analysis. Break the tie with an experiment — parallel smoke tests or WTP research (§4.27), not more argument.
- **A segment scoring high everywhere deserves suspicion.** Real segments have trade-offs. Uniform excellence usually means uniform assumption.
- **Report the best segment you are rejecting, and why.** The rejected runner-up is often the more useful finding, because it names the condition under which the strategy should change.

## §8 — Positioning around the audience

Positioning and audience selection are the same decision viewed from two sides. Dunford: positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something **a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about**. [E — §3.1] Choose the audience and you have constrained the positioning; choose the positioning and you have named the audience.

**The order is non-negotiable** [E — §3.1]: competitive alternatives → unique attributes → differentiated value → **best-fit customers** → category. Running it backwards — picking an attractive audience and reverse-engineering why you are right for them — produces claims that fail the swap test.

### 8.1 The seventeen components

#### 1. Audience-specific promise
The result *this* segment wants, in their words. Not "grow your business" but "get the client's monthly report out on the 3rd without staying late on the 2nd." **Test:** could a different segment read this and feel it was written for them? If yes, it is not audience-specific.

#### 2. Pain-specific message
Names the struggling moment, not the category. [E — §2.3] Sourced verbatim from research, never invented — this is precisely what the RU practice literature warns against: substituting your assumptions for the client's actual words. [E — §8.4]

#### 3. Outcome-specific message
The measurable after-state. Where possible with a number, a timeframe, or an observable event. Where no honest number exists, use an observable event rather than a fabricated metric: "the report goes out before the client asks for it."

#### 4. Enemy / alternative
What they will do instead of buying you. Usually a spreadsheet, an intern, an agency, or nothing. [E — §3.1] **The phantom-competitor warning applies:** name the alternative you actually lose to, not the rival you find most interesting. Beating "doing nothing" requires a different message from beating a named competitor.

#### 5. Category framing
The context that makes your value obvious. [E — §3.1] Default to an existing category the buyer already understands. A new category means you fund the education (1.14) — sometimes correct, never cheap.

#### 6. Proof requirements
What *this* segment accepts as evidence. Varies enormously: a portfolio link, a peer's word, a certification, a case study in their exact vertical, a security questionnaire. Ask what proof they have actually accepted before (1.15). **Never invent proof.** Mark gaps as `[PROOF NEEDED: how to obtain it]`.

#### 7. Trust-building assets
The concrete artefacts that carry the proof: case study in-vertical, a named reference, a free diagnostic, a public teardown, a certification, a guarantee. Each has a build cost and a lead time; list both.

#### 8. Language and vocabulary
The segment's own terms, drawn verbatim from reviews, calls and community threads. [E — §8.4] Includes the words to *avoid* — every segment has vocabulary that marks you as an outsider. In RU engagements, formulate natively; a translated positioning statement reads as foreign and undermines the trust it is meant to build. [S]

#### 9. Objections
Pre-known from the four forces: **Anxiety** (what if it doesn't work, what if it's hard, what if I look stupid) and **Habit** (our mess is at least our mess). [E — §2.3] These are not surprises to be handled in the sales call; they are content topics to be handled before it.

#### 10. Buying triggers
The events that start the search (2.9, G7). Positioning should explicitly reference the trigger: "just inherited an account you can't report on?" outperforms any feature claim.

#### 11. Status signals
What buying this says about them to peers, boss, or clients. In B2B the dominant unstated question is "will this make me look competent or reckless?" Status is why some objectively better products lose.

#### 12. Before / after state
Two concrete scenes, both specific to the segment. Not adjectives — scenes. "Sunday evening reconciling two exports by hand" → "Monday morning, the numbers already agree."

#### 13. Differentiation from competitors
What you are a leader at *for this segment*. The swap test governs: substitute the strongest competitor's name; if the statement still reads true, it is not differentiation. Bear in mind the sophistication level — in a market that has heard every claim, only a new mechanism or a narrower audience creates contrast. [E — §3.2]

#### 14. Offer packaging
How the offer is shaped for this segment: scope, format, delivery, guarantee, onboarding. The same capability sold to two segments should usually be packaged differently — and if it cannot be, the two "segments" may be one.

#### 15. Pricing implications
Which price corridor this segment accepts (4.22) and what pricing *model* their budget process tolerates. Ability to pay and process fit are different constraints: a segment may afford ₽200k and be unable to approve anything without a tender.

#### 16. Content pillars
Three to five recurring themes derived from the segment's repeat questions (G13) and objections (#9). Pillars are derived, never brainstormed — if you cannot trace a pillar to a real question someone asked, delete it.

#### 17. Sales narrative
The order in which the story is told: trigger → cost of the status quo → why alternatives fail *for them specifically* → the mechanism → proof → offer → next step. Same components as the positioning, sequenced for a conversation rather than a page.

### 8.2 Two failure modes worth naming

**Feature-led positioning.** Describing what the product does rather than what a defined set of customers gets. Detectable in a sentence: if the positioning statement contains no customer and no circumstance, it is a spec sheet. [S]

**Audience-flattery positioning.** Describing the audience admiringly ("for ambitious founders who refuse to settle") without naming a problem, an alternative, or a mechanism. It feels like positioning and commits to nothing. The swap test kills it instantly. [S]

The full working template is in `../templates/positioning-template.md`.

## §9 — Rules: how to do it, and how not to

### 9.1 The good approach

**1. Start from evidence, not fantasy.** Every claim about the audience traces to a source: an interview, a review, a query, a thread, a transaction. Assumptions are permitted and marked as such. [E — §8.4]

**2. Separate users, buyers, decision-makers, influencers and blockers.** A complex B2B purchase involves 6–10 people. [E — §6.5] "Our audience is marketing directors" describes one seat at a table of eight.

**3. Look for painful and expensive problems.** Prefer segments under compliance, time, risk, status or money pressure (G14). Cheap pain produces cheap conversations.

**4. Check ability and willingness separately.** They are different variables and they fail differently (1.3, 1.4).

**5. Map where the audience already gathers.** Named venues, with sizes and entry routes (§5.35). Platform names are not a habitat map.

**6. Test channels before scaling.** 3–4 cheap parallel tests, then concentrate — roughly a month and a small fixed budget to make the call. [E — §5.1]

**7. Use the audience's real language.** Verbatim from research, in content, ads, and the sales script. [E — §8.4]

**8. Build positioning from urgent pain and desired outcome**, in that order, after establishing differentiated value. [E — §3.1]

**9. Actively hunt overlooked segments.** Run the generators (§3.1) every engagement, not just when the obvious answers disappoint.

**10. Compare several audiences before choosing.** Ten to twenty candidates, scored on identical criteria, column-wise (§7.3).

**11. Validate with the cheapest instrument that could change your mind.** Interviews, manual outreach, smoke tests, landing pages — matched to which unknown you are actually testing. [E — §6.2]

**12. Name what you are giving up.** Every audience choice is an exclusion. A recommendation with no trade-off has not been made yet.

**13. Say when the segment is closed to you.** "This is an excellent audience that you cannot currently reach" is a complete, useful, professional answer.

### 9.2 The bad approach

**1. "Everyone is our audience."**
*Why it fails:* no message can be written, no channel chosen, no proof assembled. **Detect it by:** a promise that no segment would find surprising.
*RU form:* «наша ЦА — все, у кого есть эта проблема».
*Fix:* pick the beachhead — same product, same sales process, word of mouth between them. [E — §1.1]

**2. Demographic description only.**
*Why it fails:* Christensen's milkshake — the same demographic bought the same product for two different jobs. [E — §2.1]
*RU form:* «женщины 25–45, доход средний, интересуются саморазвитием» — a passport, not a segment.
*Fix:* define by job and circumstance (2.13), then use demographics only as a targeting parameter.

**3. No evidence of buying behaviour.**
*Why it fails:* enthusiasm is free; purchase is not. **Detect it by:** a segment where nobody can name the last comparable thing they paid for.
*Fix:* find the existing budget line (1.11), or reclassify the segment as unvalidated.

**4. Confusing interest with intent.**
*Why it fails:* saves, follows and "this is so useful!" comments are not demand. Aspirational audiences are the worst offenders (2.16).
*RU form:* «у нас 40 тысяч подписчиков, но никто не покупает» — the two facts are consistent, not contradictory.
*Fix:* score against transactions, never against engagement. Run a costly-action test (4.23).

**5. Choosing an audience you cannot reach.**
*Why it fails:* Balfour's law — you do not set the channel's rules. [E — §4.3] **Detect it by:** the list test returning no answer (§3.4).
*Fix:* pick a proxy segment that *is* reachable, or name the asset that must be built first.

**6. Choosing an audience that cannot pay.**
*Why it fails:* the most emotionally rewarding way to go out of business. Students, pre-revenue founders and hobbyists are the recurring traps.
*Fix:* purchasing power is a market criterion, not a nice-to-have. [E — §1.5]

**7. Positioning around features.**
*Why it fails:* features are alternatives-relative; buyers compare against doing nothing. [E — §3.1]
*Fix:* run the five components in order, starting from competitive alternatives.

**8. Ignoring competitors and alternatives.**
*Why it fails:* you end up competing with a spreadsheet you never mentioned, and losing.
*RU form:* «прямых конкурентов у нас нет» — almost always means the alternative is inertia, and inertia is undefeated by default.
*Fix:* ask "what would you do if we didn't exist?" and treat the answer as the competitor.

**9. Ignoring channel fit.**
*Why it fails:* a perfect segment reachable only through a channel your model cannot fund is not reachable. [E — §4.3]
*Fix:* score audience-channel fit explicitly (criteria 11 and 12).

**10. Over-indexing on founder assumptions.**
*Why it fails:* the founder's confidence is correlated with their proximity to the problem, not with the market's size.
*Fix:* every founder claim about the audience becomes a hypothesis with a named source or a named experiment.

**11. Copying competitors blindly.**
*Why it fails:* you inherit their segment, their claims, and their weaknesses, while giving up the only thing you had — difference. Also, they may be wrong; their long-running ads tell you what works for *them*. [E — §5.4]
*Fix:* use competitor research to find who they *ignore* (G3, 5.29), not who they target.

**12. Mistaking likes and comments for demand.**
*Why it fails:* engagement is produced by peers and by people enjoying the content, neither of whom buy. In B2B, applause usually comes from competitors.
*Fix:* measure a costly action only — email, call booked, deposit, purchase.

**13. Ignoring sales cycle and trust requirements.**
*Why it fails:* a segment that needs eighteen months of credibility is not accessible to a business with four months of runway, whatever its score.
*Fix:* score trust accessibility as a gate (criterion 7), and treat cycle length as a cash-flow constraint rather than an inconvenience.

**14. Persona theatre.**
*Why it fails:* a fictional biography with a stock photo generates no decisions. Personas are only useful when they capture the buying decision. [E — §3.3]
*RU form:* «Марина, 34 года, любит йогу и путешествия» — attached to a B2B software brief.
*Fix:* build the Five Rings — priority initiative, success factors, perceived barriers, buyer's journey, decision criteria. [E — §3.3]

**15. Narrow targeting where breadth is correct.**
*Why it fails:* the counterweight (§0). In established mass consumer categories, growth comes from reaching more category buyers, especially light ones. [E — §3.4]
*Fix:* name the regime before recommending. Narrow for a startup; broaden for an established brand with distribution already solved.

**16. Research that never terminates.**
*Why it fails:* Aulet warns explicitly against analysis paralysis. [E — §1.1] Audience research has decreasing returns and a real opportunity cost.
*Fix:* the minimum defensible pass (§4.27), then decide, then test. A decision with 70% confidence made this week beats 90% confidence in the second quarter.

## §10 — The master framework

Fourteen steps. This is the logic the skill executes. Steps marked **[internal]** happen in reasoning and surface only as conclusions.

---

**Step 1 — Understand the offer.**
What is sold, to whom currently, at what price, by what mechanism, with what proof, in what geography and language. What the business is genuinely better at — because best-fit customers are defined by who cares about *that*. [E — §3.1] Identify the business model (this selects the scoring preset) and the regime: challenger needing focus, or established brand needing reach (§0).

**Step 2 — Decompose the problem.**
State the job to be done in circumstance form: *when [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]*. Then decompose: which sub-problems, which are acute, which are chronic, which have deadlines. Identify the five pressures present (G14). **[partly internal]**

**Step 3 — Map the audience universe.**
Everyone who touches the problem, before any filtering: users, buyers, economic decision-makers, influencers, blockers, beneficiaries, gatekeepers. In B2B, assume 6–10 people in the decision. [E — §6.5] In B2C, identify whether buyer and user differ (gifts, family, professional purchases).

**Step 4 — Generate segments.**
10–20 candidates. Run every lens in §2 and every generator in §3.1. **A minimum of three candidates must come from the unexpected generators** — this is a hard requirement, because obvious segments crowd out non-obvious ones by default. Include at least one anti-audience.

**Step 5 — Collect evidence.**
Run the minimum defensible pass for the situation (§4.27). Record verbatim language. Tag every finding by source and method. **[partly internal]**

**Step 6 — Score.**
Nineteen criteria, preset weights, column-wise, source per cell, gate check separate from total (§7). Count the assumptions.

**Step 7 — Map habitats and channels.**
For the top segments: named venues with size, activity, rules, key voices, entry route (§5.35). Then cross against channels: reachable / expensive / impossible, given the actual budget and assets.

**Step 8 — Assess free-acquisition feasibility.**
Run the §6.3 decision rule. State the channel, the required asset, the time-to-signal, and the condition under which the answer becomes no. If free is not realistic, say so plainly (§6.4).

**Step 9 — Assess paid-acquisition feasibility.**
Targeting parameters that actually exist on the platform; the ARPU↔CAC check [E — §4.3]; the angle to test; the budget floor for a decision-grade test; what would falsify the segment.

**Step 10 — Build positioning hypotheses.**
For each of the top 3–5 segments, in Dunford order [E — §3.1], with the seventeen components (§8.1) and the swap test applied. Formulate natively in the client's language.

**Step 11 — Design message tests.**
Which message, to which segment, in which venue, measured by which costly action. Segment the cohorts before running. [E — §6.3]

**Step 12 — Design validation experiments.**
5–10 experiments matched to the actual unknown: demand → fake door; message → smoke test; delivery → concierge. [E — §6.2] Each with hypothesis, method, channel, success metric, minimum evidence, cost, speed, risk. **State in advance what result would change the recommendation.**

**Step 13 — Recommend.**
Best audience to start with; backup; audience to avoid; first experiment; first message angle; first channel. With the trade-off named, and the runner-up named with the condition that would promote it.

**Step 14 — Risks and next steps.**
What could make this wrong; which assumptions are load-bearing; what to check first; what to do in week one, month one, quarter one.

### 10.1 What the framework refuses to do

Three refusals, and each is a feature. [S]

- **It will not produce a demographic persona and call it an audience.** If the input demands one, it produces a job-and-circumstance definition with demographics attached as targeting parameters.
- **It will not recommend an unreachable segment.** However good the pain and the money, an unreachable segment is returned as "excellent, currently closed to you, here is the asset that would open it."
- **It will not invent evidence.** Missing data is marked, not filled. A matrix that is mostly assumptions is reported as a research plan, and that is a legitimate deliverable.

### 10.2 Where this framework is weakest

Stated plainly so it can be improved: [S]

- **Established mass-consumer brands.** The whole apparatus is tuned for focus. Sharp's evidence says focus is the wrong instrument there. [E — §3.4] The regime check in Step 1 mitigates this but does not solve it.
- **Genuinely new markets.** Search data, review mining and competitor analysis all fail when the category does not exist. [E — §1.2] Falls back to interviews and analogous markets, which are slower and less certain.
- **Very large enterprises.** Buying-group complexity beyond 6–10 people, procurement, and multi-year cycles exceed what a segment matrix models well.
- **Markets where the corpus is thin.** Tender platforms, non-Meta ad libraries, and podcast/newsletter research are reasoned rather than sourced (`source-map.md`, coverage self-assessment).
- **Tool recommendations.** They decay fastest. GummySearch, long the standard Reddit research tool, is reported shutting down. [E — §5.3] Treat every named tool as a category example, and verify before recommending.
