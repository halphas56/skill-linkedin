# Examples — Good and Bad Target Audience Choices

**Purpose.** Comparables. When a segment definition feels vague and you cannot say why, find its shape here.

---

## How to read this file

Examples are marked by their evidential status. This distinction is not pedantry — it decides whether you may present something to a client as a precedent or must present it as reasoning.

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[DOCUMENTED]** | Reported in a source in `source-map.md`. Cite it. |
| **[CONSTRUCTED]** | Built to illustrate a mechanism. The reasoning is sound; the company is not real, or the details are not verified. **Never present as a case study.** |
| **[PATTERN]** | A recurring failure or success shape observed across the corpus and practice. Present as a pattern, not as an incident. |

# Part 1 — Documented cases

---

## 1.1 Superhuman: the same product, a different audience, 10 points of PMF **[DOCUMENTED]**

**Situation.** Superhuman surveyed engaged users with the Sean Ellis question — *"How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman?"* Result: **22% "very disappointed."** Well below the ~40% benchmark.

**The move.** They did not change the product. They **segmented the responses by persona** and filtered to the persona that dominated the "very disappointed" group — founders, managers, executives, business development.

**Result.** The score went to **32% with no product change at all.** Then, by building the roadmap around that group (50% doubling down on what they loved, 50% fixing what blocked the "somewhat disappointed who already valued speed"), to **58% within three quarters.**

**The high-expectation customer they defined:** "Nicole" — a busy executive processing 100–200 emails a day, valuing responsiveness, sceptical about email innovation but open to genuine efficiency gains.

**Why this is the single most instructive case in the corpus.** It is direct evidence that **who you count as your audience is itself a growth lever**, not a preliminary to growth work. Ten points of measured product-market fit came from an act of definition.

**What to take.** Before improving anything, check whether you are measuring the wrong people. And note what they did with the "not disappointed" group: **ignored them entirely.**

*Source: Rahul Vohra / Superhuman, First Round Review.*

---

## 1.2 The milkshake: one demographic, two markets **[DOCUMENTED]**

**Situation.** A fast-food chain segmented milkshake buyers by product and by demographic profile. Improvements based on that segmentation did nothing.

**The finding.** Asking *"what job are they hiring the milkshake for?"* revealed two entirely separate markets:
- **Morning commuters** — a long, boring drive, one hand free, needing something that lasts. They wanted it **thicker**, with fruit chunks to make it interesting.
- **Parents buying a treat for a child** — needing it fast, so the child does not spend half an hour on a straw. They wanted it **thinner**.

Same demographic. Same product. Opposite requirements.

**What to take.** Demographic segmentation does not just under-perform — it **averages two real audiences into one fictional one**, and the fiction guides the product wrongly.

**Caveat to carry.** Strategyn (Ulwick) argues publicly that the milkshake example itself violates rigorous JTBD segmentation. Present it as an illustration of the *principle*, not as a demonstration of correct method.

*Source: Christensen, Cook & Hall, "Marketing Malpractice," HBR 2005.*

---

## 1.3 Buffer and Tesla: buying evidence before building **[DOCUMENTED]**

**Buffer.** Before the product existed, a landing page described the concept with a "Plans and Pricing" button. Clicking it did not show pricing — it explained the product was in development and offered email signup. **Signup rates validated demand before a line of product code was written.**

**Tesla.** For its first car, validated demand by asking customers to put down **$5,000 deposits** to secure a build date.

**The escalation that matters.** Buffer measured an email. Tesla measured five thousand dollars. Both were fake doors; the second measured a far more costly action and therefore produced far stronger evidence.

**What to take.** The strength of a validation signal is proportional to what the action **cost the person taking it**. A click costs nothing and proves nothing. An email costs a little. A calendar booking costs more. A deposit costs enough to be believed.

**The ethical floor.** Never take money or personal data you cannot honour. A fake door that collects emails must say what happens next.

*Source: Learning Loop; Evelance; dowhatmatter guides.*

---

## 1.4 Segment pivots: the product was fine, the audience was wrong **[DOCUMENTED]**

Several documented pivots are **customer-segment pivots**, not product pivots:

- A company discovered its revenue was actually coming from **business customers rather than consumers**, and repositioned to enterprise.
- **Ignighter** built a general-audience dating site and found unexpected traction in **Asian markets, particularly India** — a geography it had not targeted.
- **Segment** began as a classroom lecture tool. After lukewarm adoption, a small internal library built to manage event data across tools caught fire on Hacker News. The side artefact was the product; its users were a different audience entirely.
- A face-mask company monitoring reviews discovered its design was rating highly **with a customer type it had never considered**.
- Companies over-relying on averages found that "there weren't that many real customers who looked like this 'average client' at all" — segmentation then revealed high-value versus low-value groups that the average had concealed.

**What to take.** Two patterns worth naming:
1. **Averages hide audiences.** Any analysis that reports a mean customer is concealing the segments that matter.
2. **The market frequently knows better than the plan.** Reviews, unexpected traction, and unintended use cases are the market telling you who your audience is. The failure is not hearing it.

*Source: MarketingSherpa case studies; pivot compilations; gtmdialogues.*

---

## 1.5 Lead users build what you should have sold them **[DOCUMENTED]**

In studies of "extreme" sports clubs, **more than a third of members had developed or modified sports products for their own use.** Lead-user-derived concepts have been reported at multiple times the sales potential of internally generated ones.

**What to take.** In any category, a measurable fraction of the most demanding users have already built a workaround. Those people are:
- proof the problem is worth solving,
- proof of willingness to pay (in hours, the currency that never lies),
- and a free product specification.

**Where to find them.** Shared spreadsheet templates · Zapier and Make chains · scripts on GitHub · "I built a thing to solve X" posts · Notion templates sold on marketplaces.

*Source: von Hippel, lead-user theory; HBR "Let the Users Take the Lead"; European Business Review.*

# Part 2 — Weak → strong: fifteen rewrites

Each pair: the weak definition, the exact reason it fails, and a version that produces decisions. The test of a strong definition is that it contains a **who**, a **when**, and a **where**.

---

### 2.1 B2B SaaS **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Marketing directors at mid-market B2B companies."**

**Why it fails.** A job title is a seat at a table of six to ten people. No circumstance, so no urgency. No habitat, so no route to them. It describes who might benefit, not who is currently in motion.

> ✅ **"Marketing leads at 50–300-person B2B companies who have just taken over a channel they inherited from someone who left — and have a board or exec review within 6 weeks that they cannot currently report on. Found via: 'Head of Marketing' job changes on LinkedIn, plus three RevOps Slack communities."**

**What changed.** A trigger (inherited handover), a deadline (6 weeks), an emotional stake (being seen as competent in a first review), and a named route. Every one of those is a message and a targeting parameter.

---

### 2.2 Expert course **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"People who want to become successful freelancers."**

**Why it fails.** Aspirational. Enormous, engaged, and largely unmonetisable — people buy the identity cheaply (following, watching, saving) and the transformation rarely. Zero urgency.

> ✅ **"Employed specialists who have just been made redundant or have had a pay cut, already have 1–2 informal side clients, and need a repeatable way to get the next three — within the runway their severance buys. Found via: layoff announcement threads, professional Telegram chats, alumni communities of their profession."**

**What changed.** The trigger is dated and financial. The audience has proven capability (existing informal clients — they are not starting from zero) and a runway clock. Willingness to pay is real because the alternative is unemployment, not aspiration.

---

### 2.3 Agency service **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Small and medium businesses that need better marketing."**

**Why it fails.** "Need better marketing" is a judgement you hold about them, not a problem they experience. No budget signal, no vertical, no trigger.

> ✅ **"Manufacturers of technical products, 50–500 staff, whose distributors have started demanding better product presentation materials — where the budget sits with the sales director, not with marketing, and the pain is distributor shelf space rather than brand."**

**What changed.** The buyer moved (sales, not marketing — a different budget, a different fear). The pain became commercial and external. The vertical is listable via trade associations and distributor networks.

---

### 2.4 Local business **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Residents of the district who care about their health."**

**Why it fails.** No radius denominator, and "care about health" has no external marker — you cannot target a stated value.

> ✅ **"Shift workers within 2 km — hospital staff, logistics, retail management — whose schedules exclude them from every gym in the radius, because all of them close at 22:00. Found via: employer staff channels, night-shift community chats, and the employers themselves."**

**What changed.** A structural exclusion rather than a preference. Radius stated. The audience is reachable through employers, which is a channel no competitor is using because no competitor has noticed the segment.

---

### 2.5 Consumer product **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Women 25–45 with middle income who value quality."**

**Why it fails.** The canonical RU-market failure — «женщины 25–45, доход средний». A passport, not a segment. It would describe the buyers of a car, a yoga mat and a mortgage equally well.

> ✅ **"People buying this as a gift for someone whose taste they are unsure of, within 3 days of an occasion they forgot about — a distinct buyer from the self-purchaser, needing reassurance and speed rather than specification."**

**What changed.** Buyer ≠ user, which changes the entire message. The trigger (a forgotten occasion) supplies urgency. Gift-buyers need different proof (will this be well received?) than self-buyers (is this right for me?).

---

### 2.6 High-ticket consulting **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"CEOs of companies who want to scale."**

**Why it fails.** "Want to scale" is a permanent background wish, not a moment. No trigger, no admission of a problem, and an unreachable seniority without a warm route.

> ✅ **"Founders who have just raised their first institutional round and have to build a function they have never run — where the board expects a plan within the first quarter and the founder cannot admit uncertainty publicly."**

**What changed.** A dated, public trigger (funding announcement). A specific and *private* fear — which is why the outreach must be discreet and the proof must be peer-referred, not advertised.

---

### 2.7 Marketplace **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Buyers and sellers in the X category."**

**Why it fails.** Marketplaces fail on liquidity, and liquidity is local — geographically, categorically, or temporally. A national definition conceals that there is no density anywhere.

> ✅ **"Sellers of [narrow category] in [one city], where 40+ of them already trade informally in two Telegram chats and complain weekly about payment disputes — plus the specific repeat buyers those chats already serve."**

**What changed.** The definition names an existing, dense, informal market with an existing pain (payment disputes) — which is the only kind of marketplace that can be bootstrapped.

---

### 2.8 Mobile app **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Anyone who wants to be more productive."**

**Why it fails.** Store discovery is winner-take-most, and a broad definition means competing against every funded incumbent on the head terms.

> ✅ **"People managing a chronic condition that requires logging on an irregular schedule, who currently use a notes app and lose the history — reachable via condition-specific communities and the comment sections of three YouTube channels they all watch."**

**What changed.** A specific job with a failing current alternative (notes app), a named habitat, and long-tail store queries no incumbent optimises for.

---

### 2.9 Info product **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Beginners in [topic]."**

**Why it fails.** Beginners are the largest, poorest, least urgent, and most refund-prone slice of any education market — and free content already serves them well.

> ✅ **"Practitioners with 1–3 years' experience who have just been given responsibility for something adjacent to their skill and have a deliverable due — who will pay for a shortcut because the alternative is looking incompetent in a specific meeting."**

**What changed.** Status pressure with a date. They already have income and professional identity to protect. Completion and refund rates behave completely differently from beginner cohorts.

---

### 2.10 Subscription product **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"People who need this regularly."**

**Why it fails.** "Regularly" is asserted, not evidenced. Subscription businesses die of churn, and churn is decided by whether the need actually recurs on a schedule.

> ✅ **"Households where the consumption rate is predictable and running out has a same-day consequence — identified by reorder intervals in marketplace data, not by stated preference."**

**What changed.** Recurrence is measured from behaviour, and the consequence of running out supplies the reason not to cancel.

---

### 2.11 B2B services / outbound-led **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Companies that could benefit from automation."**

**Why it fails.** "Could benefit" is your opinion. There is no list and no moment.

> ✅ **"Companies that have posted a vacancy in the last 30 days for a role whose duties are 60%+ manual data transfer between two named systems — where the posted salary exceeds our annual price."**

**What changed.** The list rebuilds itself daily from job boards. The salary is the budget, stated publicly by the buyer. The 30-day window creates the queue.

**Handle with care.** Never frame this as "don't hire, buy us." Frame it around helping the new hire succeed, or the message reads as insulting.

---

### 2.12 SaaS in a crowded category **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **"Teams that need better project management."**

**Why it fails.** Maximum market sophistication. Every claim has been made. You would be the eleventh identical entrant.

> ✅ **"Architectural practices running RIBA-staged projects, currently using a generic tool plus a wall of printouts because no tool speaks in stages — where the pain is proving stage completion to a client, not managing tasks."**

**What changed.** A vertical where the workflow has a regulated vocabulary that generalists structurally cannot adopt without confusing their other customers. This is a positioning moat, not a feature.

---

### 2.13 RU market — services **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **«Малый и средний бизнес в России, которому нужна юридическая поддержка».**

**Почему не работает.** Нет триггера, нет отрасли, нет срочности. «Нужна поддержка» — это ваша оценка, а не их переживание.

> ✅ **«Селлеры на маркетплейсах, у которых заблокировали карточку товара или аккаунт по жалобе — выручка остановилась сегодня, решение нужно на этой неделе. Где искать: чаты селлеров в Telegram, разделы жалоб на профильных форумах, комментарии под постами о блокировках».**

**Что изменилось.** Предельная срочность (деньги не идут прямо сейчас), почти нулевая маркетинговая насыщенность в нише, и аудитория плотно сидит в опознаваемых чатах.

---

### 2.14 RU market — expert product **[CONSTRUCTED]**

> ❌ **«Предприниматели, которые хотят вырасти».**

**Почему не работает.** Это не сегмент, а настроение. Ни повода, ни дедлайна, ни площадки.

> ✅ **«Основатели, которые только что потеряли ключевого сотрудника — того, кто держал на себе процесс целиком, — и у которых нет ни регламентов, ни замены. Повод датирован, боль острая, платить готовы за скорость».**

**Что изменилось.** Событие с датой, измеримый ущерб, и готовность переплатить за срочность — три признака сегмента, который действительно покупает.

---

### 2.15 The universal repair **[PATTERN]**

Any weak definition can be repaired with three additions. Apply in this order:

1. **Add the circumstance.** *When* does this become urgent? (Converts a list into demand.)
2. **Add the habitat.** *Where* are 200 of them already gathered? (Converts a wish into a plan.)
3. **Add the exclusion.** *Who inside this group should we refuse?* (Converts a segment into a position.)

If a definition cannot take all three, it is not a segment — it is a market.

# Part 3 — Anatomy of good choices

---

### 3.1 The vertical nobody wants to market to **[PATTERN]**

**Shape.** A regulated, unglamorous, mid-sized industry with a compliance deadline and no venture-funded competitor.

**Why it works.** High urgency (compliance is dated and penalised) and low marketing saturation, because nobody wants to make content about it. You buy intent-level demand at awareness-level prices. Associations and licensing registers make the list free. Word of mouth is dense because everyone attends the same two conferences.

**Where it breaks.** Slow sales cycles, procurement, and a trust threshold that may take longer to clear than your runway (criterion 7).

---

### 3.2 The segment your own data already proves **[PATTERN]**

**Shape.** Sort existing customers by margin, cycle length, support load, retention and referrals. The top decile shares an attribute nobody named.

**Why it works.** It is not a hypothesis — it is measurement of something already true and unexploited. This is ABCDX applied to the base, and it is the fastest legitimate win in audience work.

**Where it breaks.** It reflects who you *sold* to, which is partly an artefact of past marketing. Confirm the pattern is causal, not a shadow of last year's campaign.

---

### 3.3 The refuser segment **[PATTERN]**

**Shape.** People who evaluated the category and consciously said no — too complicated, too expensive, unacceptable (Blue Ocean Tier 2).

**Why it works.** They are problem-aware and solution-aware (cheap to educate), they have already done the work of comparing, and they are almost never interviewed. Their reason for refusing is a product and positioning specification.

**Where it breaks.** Sometimes they refused for a reason you cannot fix. Establish which before committing.

---

### 3.4 The buying-group seat nobody addresses **[PATTERN]**

**Shape.** In a 6–10 person B2B decision, the category markets to the most senior seat. The champion, the user and the blocker are unaddressed.

**Why it works.** Arming the champion — with the internal deck, the objection handling, the security answers — is cheaper and far less contested than persuading the executive. Content aimed at the *blocker* removes the actual obstacle.

**Where it breaks.** Requires knowing the buying group, which requires interviews with each seat separately. Most teams interview one seat and generalise.

---

### 3.5 The adjacent market with the same job **[PATTERN]**

**Shape.** Take the job — collecting deposits, proving compliance, scheduling irregular shifts — and find it in three unrelated industries.

**Why it works.** You arrive with a solution the whole industry has never seen, competing against generalists who do not understand the specific pain. Assets transfer: product, vocabulary, sometimes case studies.

**Where it breaks.** Adjacency is often illusory. The workflow may be identical while the **buying process** is completely different — and the buying process is the part that decides. Test all three homogeneity conditions (same product, same sales process, word of mouth between them) before committing.

# Part 4 — Anatomy of bad choices

Each with its failure mechanism, its detection test, and its repair.

---

### 4.1 "Everyone with this problem" **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** No message can be written, because a message that fits everyone excludes nobody and therefore attracts nobody. No channel can be chosen. No proof can be assembled.

**Detection.** Write the promise. If no segment would find it surprising, the segment is not a segment.

**Repair.** Beachhead conditions — same product, same sales process, word of mouth between them.

---

### 4.2 The engaged audience that does not buy **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** Interest is not intent. Aspirational audiences are the worst offenders: people buy the *identity* cheaply — following, watching, saving — and the transformation rarely.

**Detection.** *«У нас 40 тысяч подписчиков, но никто не покупает».* These two facts are consistent, not contradictory. Also: high engagement in B2B that comes overwhelmingly from competitors and peers.

**Repair.** Score against completed transactions only. Run a costly-action test — email, booking, deposit — never a click. If the segment cannot produce a costly action, it is an audience for content, not for revenue.

---

### 4.3 The perfect segment you cannot reach **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** Balfour's law: you do not set the channel's rules. A segment with no habitat, no list, and no targeting parameter is unreachable regardless of its pain and money.

**Detection.** The list test. *"If I had to reach 100 of them by Friday, exactly where would I go?"* No answer = failed.

**Repair.** Either find a **reachable proxy** — an observable attribute that correlates with the segment — or name the asset that must be built first and treat the segment as a future target. **"Excellent audience, currently closed to you" is a complete and professional answer.**

---

### 4.4 The audience that cannot pay **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** The most emotionally rewarding way to go out of business. Enormous gratitude, real pain, no budget. Students, pre-revenue founders and hobbyists are the recurring traps.

**Detection.** Ask what they last paid for in this category. If nobody can name anything, willingness is unproven — and note that ability and willingness fail *separately*: enterprises with millions refuse to pay $50 for a category they have decided should be free.

**Repair.** Find the adjacent segment with the same pain **and** a budget line. Freelance designers → design agencies with a bookkeeper. Individual practitioners → the clinic that employs them.

---

### 4.5 Persona theatre **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** A fictional biography generates no decisions. *«Марина, 34 года, любит йогу и путешествия»* attached to a B2B software brief is the canonical form.

**Detection.** Does the persona contain a hobby and no decision criterion? Does any line in it change what you would build, write, or where you would spend?

**Repair.** Five Rings — priority initiative, success factors, perceived barriers, buyer's journey, decision criteria. If a persona attribute does not change a decision, delete it.

---

### 4.6 The Product Hunt false positive **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** A launch platform's audience is early adopters, builders and other founders. A successful launch produces a spike of exactly the wrong users, and the spike is very convincing.

**Detection.** Sign-ups spike, retention is near zero, and the feedback is all about the product rather than about the problem.

**Repair.** Treat launch audiences as a distribution event, never as a segment — unless your market genuinely is founders.

---

### 4.7 Copying the competitor's audience **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** You inherit their segment, their claims and their weaknesses, and give up the only asset you had — difference. Compounding the error: they may be wrong, and their long-running ads only prove what works *for them*, with their brand and their price.

**Detection.** Your positioning survives the swap test — substitute their name and it still reads true.

**Repair.** Use competitor research to find who they **ignore**, not who they target. Read their reviews for customers they clearly did not design for.

---

### 4.8 Research that never terminates **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** Audience research has decreasing returns and a real opportunity cost. Aulet warns explicitly against analysis paralysis.

**Detection.** Three weeks in, no decision, and the new findings are confirming rather than changing anything.

**Repair.** The minimum defensible pass, then decide, then test. **A decision at 70% confidence this week beats 90% confidence next quarter** — because the test produces better evidence than any further desk research would.

---

### 4.9 Narrow targeting in the wrong regime **[PATTERN]**

**Mechanism.** For an established brand in a mass consumer category with distribution solved, growth comes from reaching more category buyers — especially light ones — not from deepening loyalty in a narrow segment.

**Detection.** The client is an established brand, the category is a repertoire category, and the strategy proposes abandoning most of the market.

**Repair.** Name the regime before recommending. Narrow the *acquisition mechanics*; keep the *brand assets* broad and consistent.

# Part 5 — Anti-audience examples

The audience you refuse is part of the strategy. Five recurring shapes worth naming explicitly. **[PATTERN]**

| Anti-audience | The tell | Why refuse |
|---|---|---|
| **The D-segment** | Buys, extracts no value, generates support load and negative word of mouth | Every hour spent here is taken from an A-segment; ABCDX exists to make this visible |
| **The tyre-kicker vertical** | Long cycles, heavy customisation demands, price pressure, no reference value | Consumes the capacity that would have won three good clients |
| **The X-segment mistaken for demand** | Large, prestigious, would be excellent — but needs product work you have not done | A roadmap signal, not current demand. Chasing it now distorts the product for a customer you cannot yet serve |
| **The free-culture segment** | Real pain, real ability to pay, and a category norm that this should be free | Willingness fails independently of ability; the education cost is the whole business |
| **The wrong-trust segment** | Requires certifications, references or procurement you cannot clear for 18 months | Not a marketing problem. A time problem. Name the asset and revisit |

**How to present an anti-audience to a client.** Not as a judgement about those people, but as a resource decision with a number attached: *"This group is 30% of inbound and 8% of margin, and it consumes 40% of support. Declining it funds two more A-segment clients."* Framed that way it is accepted. Framed as "these customers are bad" it is not.

# Part 6 — The three tests, applied

Any candidate segment, good or bad, resolves under three questions. Run them in this order; stop at the first failure.

**1. The list test.** *Name a place where 200 of them are already gathered.*
No place → not a segment. (§4.3 above.)

**2. The money test.** *Name something comparable they already pay for.*
No precedent → willingness unproven. (§4.4 above.)

**3. The absence test.** *Why is no competitor serving them?*
- "It's unprofitable" or "it's unservable" → **that is the finding.** Move on.
- "Nobody thought to look" → **that is the opportunity.** Proceed.

A candidate passing all three is a recommendation. A candidate failing one is a hypothesis with a named experiment attached. Both are legitimate deliverables. **Presenting the second as the first is the only unforgivable error in this discipline.**
