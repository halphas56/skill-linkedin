# The JTBD Engine

**The primary definition mechanism of this skill.** Every segment starts here. Demographics, firmographics and psychographics are *targeting parameters* attached afterwards — they are never the definition.

**Christensen's definition, and the one this skill uses:**

> A job is **"the progress that a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance."**
> — Clayton Christensen, *Competing Against Luck* (2016) `[E — read in full]`

Two words carry the framework. **Progress** — movement toward a goal, a process rather than a discrete event. **Circumstance** — not a person. Christensen's own demonstration: he is 64, six foot eight, size sixteen shoes, drives a Honda minivan in suburban Boston. *"But these characteristics have not yet caused me to go out and buy the New York Times today."* Correlation is not causation, and customer attributes are correlation.

## §1 — Why this replaces the demographic definition

### The failure, stated concretely

**Bad — a passport, not a segment:**
> ЦА: предприниматели 25–45 лет, интересуются маркетингом.

**Good — a circumstance with a decision inside it:**
> Владелец небольшого интернет-магазина, у которого уже идут рекламные расходы, но CPA вырос, он не понимает причину и боится увеличивать бюджет до выяснения проблемы.

### Why the second one works

| Element | Extracted from the definition | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Already running paid ads → has budget, history, data | Qualification: they already pay for something |
| **Trigger** | CPA rose | *When* to reach them; the ad hook |
| **Struggle** | Doesn't understand the cause | The promise: diagnosis, not more traffic |
| **Anxiety** | Afraid to scale spend | The objection to pre-empt |
| **Desired progress** | Regain control over spend | The outcome message |
| **Implied alternatives** | Dig through the account alone · ask in a chat · hire a contractor · freeze the budget | The real competitive set |

The first definition hands you nothing. **A good audience definition writes the marketing brief by itself.**

### The four wrong organizing principles

Christensen names the four things managers organize innovation around instead of circumstance. None is *wrong*; all are **insufficient and non-predictive**: `[E]`

**Product attributes · Customer characteristics · Trends · Competitive response**

If your audience document is organised around any of these, you have described the market without explaining it.

### The authority on demographics

Adele Revella: **"Demographics are a Dangerous Distraction."** Starting from *the buying decision you want to influence* yields **about half as many personas** and tells you exactly what you can and cannot do to affect the decision. Her worked warning: the CFO signs off but rarely chooses between vendor A and vendor B — **so interviewing CFOs and building that persona yields nothing relevant.** `[E]`

### The average fallacy

In the 1950s the US Air Force, finding pilots could not control their planes, remeasured thousands of pilots on ten dimensions to redesign cockpits around the *average* pilot. How many pilots actually fit the average? **None.** Every pilot had a "jagged profile" — long legs with a short torso, and so on. The cockpit designed for everyone fit no one. The fix was the adjustable seat. `[E — Todd Rose, *The End of Average*, cited in Competing Against Luck]`

> **"There's no such thing as 'average' in the real world. And innovating toward 'average' is doomed to fail."**

This is the milkshake error wearing different clothes, and it is why this skill **never averages a contradiction** (see `avatar-from-comments.md` §7, `willingness-to-pay.md` §6).

## §2 — What is *not* a job: the three boundaries

Most bad JTBD work fails here, and the failure is invisible without the tests. Three things get mistaken for jobs.

### Boundary 1 — A need is too generic

*"I need to eat." "I need to feel healthy." "I need to save for retirement."*

Needs are almost always true, and their generality gives innovators "only the vaguest of direction." `[E]` Needing to eat will not cause you to pick one solution over another — you might skip the meal entirely.

**Test:** if the statement is true of the person on most days regardless of circumstance, it is a need, not a job.

### Boundary 2 — A life theme is too abstract

*"I want to be a good dad." "I want to be a valued member of my church."*

These are **guiding principles** — ever-present, important, and incapable of triggering a purchase. Christensen is explicit: **"Helping me feel like a good dad is not a Job to Be Done."** `[E]` A company cannot build a product for it without knowing the specific circumstance in which you are trying to achieve it.

The jobs you hire for are the ones that **overcome the obstacles in the way of your life themes, in specific circumstances.** Jobs roll up into life themes; they are not the same thing.

**Test:** if you cannot name the circumstance and the moment, you have a life theme.

### Boundary 3 — A spec or preference is too concrete

Covered by the abstraction test in §3, which is the single most useful precision instrument in this file.

## §3 — The two precision tests

These are mechanical. Run them on every job statement before it enters a segment definition.

### Test 1 — The abstraction test (the category test)

> **"If the architecture of the system or product can only be met by products within the same product class, the concept of the Job to Be Done does not apply."** `[E]`

**Procedure.** List everything the person could plausibly hire to make this progress. Look at the list.

- **All candidates from one product category** → you have a *need, a preference, or a technical spec*. Go up one level of abstraction.
- **Candidates from several different categories** → you are at the right altitude. This is a job.

**Worked, from the book:**

| Statement | Candidates | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| *"I need a chocolate milkshake in a 12-oz disposable container"* | milkshakes | ❌ Not a job — a preference |
| *"Keep me occupied on a boring drive, and full enough that I'm not hungry at my 10 a.m. meeting"* | banana · doughnut · bagel · Snickers · coffee · milkshake | ✅ A job |
| *"A thin sheet of material, high friction coefficient, low thermal conduction, moisture-impervious, won't rip when wrapped"* | Tyvek, or nothing | ❌ Not a job — a technical specification |
| *"Keep my family warm in a Boston winter and cool in summer, while minimising heating costs"* | blown pulp · fiberglass rolls · Tyvek · extra sweaters · dehumidifiers · **moving to San Francisco** | ✅ A job |

That last row is the point. **When "move to a different city" appears on your candidate list, you have found the real altitude** — and with it, the real competitive set.

**Why this matters for audience work:** the abstraction level determines the market size, the competitor set, and the message. Too low and you are optimising a spec sheet inside a category nobody thinks in. Too high and you are back at a life theme nobody buys against.

> **Independent corroboration — three-way.** Malcolm McDonald reaches the identical test from the market-structure tradition: **"A market is the aggregation of all products or services which customers regard as being capable of satisfying the same need."** `[E]` Osterwalder reaches it a third time from offer design, instructing profilers to work *"like ethnographers"* and forget what they sell — a business-book publisher's real competitive set is **books, consultants, YouTube and an MBA**. `[E]`
>
> **Three traditions, three methods, three decades, one rule: the market is bounded by what the customer would substitute, not by what the category is called.** This is the most heavily corroborated principle in the corpus. See `market-mapping.md` §1 and `customer-profile.md` §7.

**The climbing procedure.** The abstraction test tells you *when to stop*; it does not tell you how to climb. For that, ladder with **"why?"** — *why learn a language? → to improve the CV → why? → to earn more.* Keep going until the candidate list spans different product categories, and stop before you reach a life theme (§2). Full method: `customer-profile.md` §5.

### Test 2 — The verbs-and-nouns test

> **"If you or a colleague describes a Job to Be Done in adjectives and adverbs, it is not a valid job."** `[E]`

**"Convenience" is not a job.** It might be an *experience* that makes someone choose you, but it is not the job. A valid job is expressed in **verbs and nouns**: *"write books verbally, without typing or editing by hand."*

**Screen your job statements for:** convenient · seamless · easy · affordable · reliable · premium · efficient. Every one of these is an adjective wearing a job's clothing.

## §4 — The six-link chain

Every segment definition must contain all six links. A missing link is a hole with a predictable symptom.

```
SITUATION → TRIGGER → STRUGGLE → DESIRED PROGRESS → ALTERNATIVES → PURCHASE DECISION
```

| Link | Question | Missing → symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Situation** | What circumstances are they in? | You cannot qualify or target |
| **2. Trigger** | What changed, and when? | Infinite pipeline. Interest without timing |
| **3. Struggle** | What specifically can't they do? | Message speaks to a category, not a person |
| **4. Desired progress** | What does "better" look like *to them*? | You sell features; they wanted progress |
| **5. Alternatives** | What do they do instead — including nothing? | You lose to a spreadsheet you never mentioned |
| **6. Purchase decision** | Who decides, on what criteria, against what barriers? | Great marketing, stalled deals |

### The circumstance question set

Christensen's own list for pinning down link 1 — richer than "who are they": `[E]`

*Where are you? When is it? Who are you with? While doing what? **What were you doing half an hour ago? What will you be doing next?** What social, cultural or political pressures apply?*

Plus contextual layers: **life stage** (just out of college / midlife / nearing retirement) · **family status** (married, divorced, newborn, ageing parents) · **financial status** (underwater in debt / high net worth).

The two bolded questions are the ones nobody asks and the ones that most often locate the trigger.

### The template

```
When ______________________________________________ ,    [situation]
and then ___________________________________________ ,    [trigger]
I struggle to ______________________________________ ,    [struggle]
so I want to _______________________________________ ,    [desired progress]
because right now I ________________________________ ,    [alternative]
and I'll choose based on ___________________________ .    [decision criteria]
```

**Rule:** any blank not fillable from evidence at **L2 or above** (`evidence-ladder.md`) gets marked `L1` with the experiment attached. Do not fill it with something plausible — that is the L0 laundering the ladder exists to prevent.

## §5 — The three dimensions

Every job has all three simultaneously. `[E]`

| Dimension | Question | Example (the CPA case) |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | What practical task must get done? | "Find which campaign broke my unit economics" |
| **Emotional** | What do they want to feel, or stop feeling? | "Stop feeling like I'm burning money in the dark" |
| **Social** | How do they want to be seen? | "Not look like an amateur to my partner" |

**Consumers' social and emotional needs can far outweigh the functional ones.** Consider hiring childcare: the functional dimension matters, but *"who will I trust with my children?"* decides it. `[E]`

### The two demonstrations worth remembering

**The emotional score.** Todd Dunn, an innovation director, sat as a patient on a Utah exam table and noticed the crinkly paper — designed for the function of keeping the table clean, and in practice a generator of anxiety. Then his surgeon ignored state-of-the-art diagnostic software to sketch the knee on paper, because typing meant turning away from an anxious patient for a few seconds. *"We'd designed everything in that room from a functional perspective, but we had completely overlooked the emotional score."* `[E]`

**Pampers in China.** P&G tried to win with a ten-cent functional "containment device" and it did not sell. In a focus group a mother reported the highlight of her week was renewed intimacy with her husband — because the baby slept through the night, so she slept, so she was rested. The relaunch led on sleep and cognitive development, not absorbency. By 2013 Pampers was a top-selling brand in China at ~$1.6bn. `[E]`

**In B2B the social dimension is usually career risk:** *"will this make me look competent or reckless?"* It is why objectively better products lose, and it is invisible in every requirements document ever written.

## §6 — The four forces

Demand appears only when the forces of progress beat the forces of inertia. `[E — Moesta; Christensen]`

```
PUSH + PULL  >  ANXIETY + HABIT   →  the switch happens
PUSH + PULL  ≤  ANXIETY + HABIT   →  nothing happens, forever
```

| Force | Direction | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Push** | → | The pain of the current situation. The struggling moment |
| **Pull** | → | The attraction of the new solution |
| **Anxiety** | ← | Fear of the new: cost, learning it, the unknown, "what if it's not better?" |
| **Habit** | ← | *"I don't love it, but I'm at least comfortable with how I deal with it now"* |

### Why the blocking forces are stronger than they look

**Loss aversion is roughly twice as powerful psychologically as the allure of gains** (Kahneman & Tversky). `[E]` And the pull of the old "requires no deliberation and has some intuitive plausibility as a solution already."

**Habit is often attachment, not laziness — and this is the most under-modelled force in the set.** Bob Moesta's condo case: a Detroit developer could not convert traffic into sales. The blocker was not a missing feature. It was **the dining room table.** Buyers kept saying *"as soon as I could figure out what to do with my dining room table, then I was free to move."* Every birthday, every Christmas, homework, forts underneath — the table was the family. One interviewee needed days and multiple boxes of tissues to clear a single closet: *"Every choice felt like she was discarding a memory."*

> **"I went in thinking we were in the business of new home construction. But I realized we were instead in the business of moving lives."** — Bob Moesta

The fixes were all anxiety-and-habit fixes: shrink the second bedroom 20% to fit a full-size dining table · provide moving services · two years of storage · an on-site sorting room so decisions could wait · cut thirty pages of customisation down to **three finished variants** (which alone dropped cold-feet cancellations from five or six a month to one).

Result: the developer **raised prices by $3,500 profitably** and **grew 25% in 2007 while the surrounding market fell 49%.** `[E]`

### The diagnostic

```
FORCE PROFILE
Push    ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░  7/10   evidence: ___________  L_
Pull    ▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░  5/10   evidence: ___________  L_
Anxiety ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░  8/10   evidence: ___________  L_
Habit   ▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░  6/10   evidence: ___________  L_
        Push+Pull = 12   vs   Anxiety+Habit = 14   →  NO SWITCH
Binding constraint: ANXIETY  →  risk reversal, proof, guarantee, pilot
```

| Binding constraint | Wrong move | Right move |
|---|---|---|
| **Weak Push** | Better copy | **Wrong segment.** Copy cannot manufacture a struggling moment |
| **Weak Pull** | Discounting | Sharper differentiated value; show the after-state concretely |
| **High Anxiety** | More features | Risk reversal — guarantee, pilot, in-vertical references, migration done for you |
| **High Habit** | Persuasion | Reduce switching cost — and check whether the "cost" is emotional (the dining table), not operational |

**Companies overwhelmingly fund Pull.** Innovators "focus exclusively on the forces pushing for change... but they completely ignore the powerful forces blocking that change." `[E]` Adding benefits to a message losing to fear is spending on the wrong side of an inequality.

### The anxiety-reduction move

ING Direct opened physical **cafés** where you cannot perform teller transactions — their purpose is to reassure customers that a virtual bank is a real bank. SNHU's genuine campus and non-profit status reduce online students' fear of a fly-by-night operation. Mercer added a modelling module letting clients simulate pension buyout options before committing — "a kind of anxiety-reducing practice run." `[E]`

**Pattern: when Anxiety binds, the intervention is usually an experience, not an argument.**

## §7 — Big Hire and Little Hire

**Absent from most audience research, and it changes what counts as evidence.** `[E]`

| | **Big Hire** | **Little Hire** |
|---|---|---|
| What | The purchase | Actually consuming or using it |
| Tracked by | Almost all sales data | Almost nothing |
| Proves | They were willing to buy | **The job was real** |

> *"The Big Hire might suggest that a product has solved a customer's job, but only a consistent series of Little Hires can confirm it."*

The dress bought and never worn with the tag still on. The app downloaded and opened once. A vendor tracking downloads "will have no idea whether its app is doing a good job solving your desire for progress or not."

**Consequence for the Evidence Ladder:** a purchase is `L6`, but **repeated Little Hires are the strongest evidence obtainable** — they prove the job persisted after the money was spent. When assessing an existing customer base, ask for *usage* data, not *purchase* data. See `evidence-ladder.md` §3.

### The firing question

> **"What has to get fired for my product to get hired?"**

Something is always fired — including the option of doing nothing. Wristwatches were fired en masse when phones told the time. The buyers of Depend Silhouette fired *staying home rather than risking embarrassment*.

**Companies do not think about this enough.** They work on making their product more appealing and never ask what it must displace. And note the asymmetry: *"It's easy to fire things that simply offer functional solutions... But when the decision involves firing something that has emotional and social dimensions, that something is far harder to let go."*

## §8 — Where to find jobs: the five hunting grounds

Christensen's own generator set. `[E]` Cross-referenced to this skill's generators in `segment-generation-lenses.md`.

### 1. Jobs in your own life
*"Just look in the mirror — your life is very articulate. If it matters to you, it's likely to matter to others."*

Sal Khan made "cheap and crappier" videos than the hundreds already on YouTube — but the others "weren't focusing on the core conceptual ideas, and they definitely weren't fun." Sheila Marcelo founded Care.com out of her own childcare struggle. Airbnb began with Brian Chesky unable to afford both rent and a design conference.

**Use with care:** this is the founder-assumption risk. Treat a founder's own job as `L2` — one observed artefact — and go find the other nine.

### 2. Nonconsumption — "competing with nothing"
> *"Who wouldn't want to be competing with nothing?"*

People who hire **no solution at all** because none satisfies the job. They are invisible in every dataset, because data is generated by transactions that did not happen.

- **Airbnb:** ~40% of guests say they would not have made the trip at all, or would have stayed with family. Virtually all hosts would never have considered renting a room. `[E]`
- **Kimberly-Clark:** ~1 in 3 women over 18 has some incontinence issue; only a small percentage use any product. People typically **suffer for around two years** before buying one. They use feminine hygiene pads as a stopgap — or simply stop travelling, stop going to restaurants, stop seeing friends. The job was *"help them reclaim their lives."* Depend Silhouette did $60m in year one, 30% growth in year two, **without cannibalising existing products** — in a "mature" category. `[E]`
- **SNHU:** the online programme was competing not with local colleges but with *nothing* — people who would otherwise never resume their education.

**The diagnostic worth memorising:**
> **"If it feels like there isn't room for growth in a market, it could actually be a signal that you've defined the job poorly."** `[E]`

Maps to Blue Ocean Tier 3 (`../research/frameworks.md` A3), but sharper: nonconsumption is a *measurable* gap between people who have the struggle and people who buy anything at all.

### 3. Workarounds and compensating behaviours
> *"Whenever you see a compensating behavior, pay very close attention."*

Someone building their own solution has already paid — in hours. The workaround is a free specification.

- The "Bank of Daddy" — a father manually crediting allowance and calculating interest because banks made a child's savings account punitive. ING Direct saw the job and became the fastest-growing bank in the US; sold to Capital One for $9bn.
- Small businesses jury-rigging **Quicken** (personal finance software) for business accounts, when real accounting software existed. They did not want accounting software. **QuickBooks shipped with half the functionality at twice the price and became the global leader.**
- OpenTable, born from the phone-tag workaround of booking a table around three people's schedules.

**You will not see these "if you're not fully immersed in the context of their struggle."**

### 4. Negative jobs — what people *don't* want to do
> **"In my experience, negative jobs are often the best innovation opportunities."** `[E]`

**This generator is missing from most segmentation practice and it is flagged by Christensen as among the most productive.**

The canonical case: your child wakes with a sore throat, you are fairly sure it is strep, and the job is **"I don't want to see the doctor."** Not "get healthcare" — *avoid* the afternoon lost to a waiting room for a poke, a prod and a prescription you already anticipated. Rick Krieger founded QuickMedx after waiting hours in an ER for his son's strep test; it became CVS MinuteClinic, now 1,000+ locations.

**How to run it.** Take your category and ask: *what does the customer wish they did not have to do at all?* Then check whether removing it — rather than improving it — is the product.

### 5. Unusual uses
Church & Dwight watched what people actually did with the orange box of Arm & Hammer baking soda: added to laundry detergent, mixed into toothpaste, sprinkled on carpets, left open in the fridge. Management had never considered a job other than baking. Those observations produced the first phosphate-free laundry detergent, plus cat litter, carpet cleaner, air fresheners and deodorant. **The baking-soda box is now under 7% of Arm & Hammer's consumer revenue.** `[E]`

NyQuil users taking a couple of spoonfuls to sleep when not sick → ZzzQuil.

**Corresponds to transformation T10 in `segment-generation-lenses.md`.**

## §9 — The research method

### The minidocumentary — five questions

Christensen's framing: imagine filming a short documentary of one person struggling to make progress. Capture: `[E]`

1. **What progress is the person trying to achieve?** Functional, social and emotional dimensions.
2. **What are the circumstances of the struggle?** Who, when, where, while doing what.
3. **What obstacles are getting in the way?**
4. **Are they making do with imperfect solutions through compensating behaviour?** Using something that performs the job badly? Cobbling together multiple products? Doing nothing?
5. **How would they define "quality" for a better solution, and what tradeoffs are they willing to make?**

Question 4 is where workarounds surface. Question 5 is where the job spec begins.

### The storyboard

Talk to people as if you will storyboard their struggle afterwards. Pixar's structure: `[E]`

```
Once upon a time . . .
Every day . . .
One day . . .
Because of that, we did this . . .
Because of this, we did that . . .
Finally I did . . .
```

Airbnb storyboarded **forty-five distinct emotional moments** for hosts and guests before launching. Chesky: *"When you storyboard something, the more realistic it is, the more decisions you have to make."* `[E]`

### Depth over breadth — a correction to standard sampling

> **"One of the fundamental mistakes that many marketers make is to collect a handful of data points from a huge sample of respondents when what they really need is a huge number of data points from a smaller sample size. Great innovation insights have more to do with depth than breadth."** `[E]`

The unit is an **extended narrative with a sample size of one**, repeated and then clustered. Roughly 10 interviews of 30–60 minutes surfaces the 3–5 struggling moments covering most of a market — but only if each one goes deep.

### Recruitment

| Recruit | Why | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Recent switchers (bought within ~90 days) | Complete chain, memory intact | 1 |
| Recent buyers of a competitor | Why they chose *not* you | 2 |
| **Nonconsumers** — considered and did nothing | The true ceiling; invisible in all data | 3 |
| Recent churners | Anxiety and Habit made visible | 4 |
| Workaround builders | Lead users; a free specification | 5 |

**Do not recruit** people who are merely "interested," friends, or roles that sign off without choosing between vendors.

**The referral mechanic:** *"Who else do you know dealing with this?"* — aim to leave every interview with **2–3 new candidates.** `[E — Constable]` It compounds recruitment and measures community density at the same time.

### Interview technique — what the mattress interview teaches

Bob Moesta's published interview with a buyer who purchased a mattress at Costco. On the surface: an impulse buy. In reality **he had been thinking about it for a year**, waking with a headache, a neck ache and a backache, with total buyer's remorse over a $2,000 pillow-top bought four years earlier. `[E]`

What the interviewer actually asked about: what else was in the cart · how much milk · who was with him · whether the list was done · whether it was stressful. Seemingly irrelevant. Collectively they rebuilt the moment.

**Four lessons this skill adopts:**

1. **Build the timeline backwards from the purchase to the first passive thought.** The "impulse" is almost always the last frame of a long film.
2. **This is a direct refutation of last-click attribution.** Costco got credit for a decision that took a year and was made in a bathroom mirror.
3. **The real competitors were Advil and Red Bull** — the compensating behaviours for poor sleep. No mattress retailer's competitor set contains them.
4. **The blocking forces were removed one at a time, in the store:** his wife was physically present and gave her blessing (removing "you bought this without me"), and Costco's no-fuss returns removed the last one. *"It was easier for him to take that leap, never having lain on it, than to walk into the mattress store and lie down."*

Full scripts: `interview-and-survey-kits.md` Kits 1–2.

### Analysis: cluster, do not segment

**A genuine tension with the rest of this skill, carried openly rather than smoothed over.**

> **"Understanding jobs is about *clustering* insights into a coherent picture, rather than segmenting down to finer and finer slices."** `[E]`
> *"When we deconstruct coherent customer episodes into binary bits, such as 'male/female,' 'large company/small company,' 'new customer/existing customer,' we destroy meaning in the process."*
> *"Jobs insights are fragile — they're more like stories than statistics."*

This skill scores, ranks and tabulates. That machinery is useful and it is also a way of destroying meaning if applied too early.

**The resolution this skill adopts:**

| Phase | Mode |
|---|---|
| Discovery (Steps 2–5) | **Cluster.** Whole narratives. Resist the spreadsheet. No binary attributes yet |
| Prioritisation (Steps 6–11) | **Segment and score.** Now the machinery is appropriate |
| Delivery | **Carry the story back in.** Every scored segment ships with at least one full narrative attached |

A segment object with no story behind it is a row, not a customer. If the matrix is the only artefact, meaning was destroyed somewhere around Step 6.

## §10 — What does the product actually compete with?

The most clarifying question in the framework.

**A coffee shop does not compete only with coffee shops.** It competes with coffee at home · an energy drink · skipping coffee · a place to sit and work · a walk to clear your head · a legitimate reason to leave the office for ten minutes.

Christensen's own examples:
- **Margarine** competes with butter — but also with cream cheese, olive oil, mayonnaise (for "moisten the bread so it's easier to chew") and with **Teflon and non-stick spray** (for "help me not burn my food"), which sit in two different aisles neither visible from the dairy case. *"No customer walks into the store saying, 'I need to buy something in the yellow fats category.'"*
- **Facebook competes with cigarettes** — both hired for a mid-workday break, relaxation and a virtual water cooler.
- **Netflix,** per Reed Hastings: *"Really we compete with everything you do to relax. We compete with video games. We compete with drinking a bottle of wine. That's a particularly tough one!"*
- **BMW** concluded the real job was *mobility*, putting it against Tesla, Uber, Zipcar and Google. *"We realized we were competing with companies whose names we couldn't pronounce eighteen months ago. We needed to start benchmarking outside of our category."*
- **School,** in Christensen's most uncomfortable example: the job is *"I want to feel successful every day, and I need friends."* If school does not do it, a student can fire school and hire a gang, or a minimum-wage job and a car. *"These are the 'Snickers' and 'doughnuts' that compete against school."*

**Method.** Ask *"if we vanished tomorrow, what would you do instead?"* Take every answer seriously as a competitor — including "nothing," which is usually the market leader.

**The trap this avoids:** organising around a product category rather than a job. Ted Levitt, 1960 — the railroads declined "because they assumed themselves to be in the railroad business rather than in the transportation business." They measured themselves as if selling drills, not quarter-inch holes. `[E]`

## §11 — Jobs are enduring; solutions are not

> *"We don't 'create' jobs, we discover them. Jobs themselves are enduring and persistent, but the way we solve them can change dramatically over time."* `[E]`

Sharing information across distance: Pony Express → telegraph → air mail → email. Teenagers communicating without parental oversight: notes in the hallway → the phone cord stretched into the far corner → Snapchat.

**The instruction:** *"focus on understanding the underlying job, not falling in love with your solution for it."*

For audience work this means a job-defined segment **outlives** a product-defined one, and it is the right unit to hand to downstream strategy.

## §12 — The limits of the theory

Stated by Christensen himself. Respect them; a framework applied outside its boundary produces confident nonsense. `[E]`

**Jobs Theory is not useful when:**

- **There is no real struggle**, or existing solutions are already good enough.
- **The decision is essentially mathematical** — commodities trading, for example. There is no complex bundle of social, emotional and functional needs; there are rational decisions a computer could make.
- **The statement is a spec, a preference or a life theme** — see §2 and §3.

**And a line worth quoting to any client who asks you to lead with price:**

> **"Cost or efficiency is not a core element of a job."**

Also note the ethical boundary Christensen draws explicitly: Jobs Theory explains *why* customers choose, **not whether you should offer a solution.** Cigarettes satisfy an array of jobs. *"Ethical choices are, of course, equally important to get right."*

**On anomalies:** *"Anomalies do not disprove anything."* When you find a case the framework cannot explain, the correct response is to define a new boundary — not to discard the tool or pretend the case fits.

---

## §13 — Signal who this is *not* for

> **"It's actually important to signal 'this product is not for you' or they'll come back and say it's a crummy product."** `[E]`

Christensen's own justification for the Anti-ICP. If someone hires your product for a job it was not built for, **you risk alienating that consumer forever** — and collecting a bad review that describes a failure you never promised to avoid.

This is not brand positioning theatre. It is defect prevention. See `../checklists/red-flags-checklist.md` §H.

## §14 — Quality gate

A JTBD-defined segment is not deliverable until:

- [ ] **The abstraction test passes** — candidates come from *different product categories* (§3)
- [ ] **The verbs-and-nouns test passes** — no adjectives standing in for a job
- [ ] It is **not a need** (too generic) and **not a life theme** (too abstract) (§2)
- [ ] All **six chain links** present
- [ ] The **circumstance** is stated, not the person type
- [ ] The **trigger** is a discrete, observable, timestampable event
- [ ] All **three dimensions** filled — and the social one is not left blank in B2B
- [ ] **Four forces** scored, binding constraint named
- [ ] **"What has to get fired?"** answered
- [ ] **Big Hire *and* Little Hire** distinguished where usage data exists
- [ ] **Alternatives include "do nothing"** and at least one candidate from another category
- [ ] **Nonconsumption checked** — who has this struggle and buys nothing at all?
- [ ] At least one **full narrative** attached, not only scored attributes
- [ ] Every element carries an **evidence level**; nothing load-bearing at L0 or L1
- [ ] Language **verbatim**, not paraphrased

### The final test

Read the definition aloud and ask: **"Does this tell me when to show up, what to say, and what they'll object to?"**

If not, you have written a description of people. Go back to the chain.
