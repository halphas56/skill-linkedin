# Segment Generation Lenses — The Engine

**Purpose.** Produce 10–20 genuinely distinct candidate segments from any offer, reliably, without relying on inspiration.

**Why an engine.** Unaided brainstorming produces four segments, three of which are the same segment described differently, and none of which is unexpected. Obvious segments crowd out non-obvious ones by default. This file forces the search to be systematic.

**Hard requirement:** at least **three** candidates must come from Block C (unexpected generators), and at least **one** anti-audience must be named. A generation run that produces only obvious segments has failed, regardless of how many it produced.

---

## How to run it

1. **Establish the job first** (Block A). Everything else hangs from it.
2. **Run every lens in Blocks B and C.** Do not skip a lens because it "obviously doesn't apply" — that judgement is what produces four segments.
3. Write each candidate as a one-liner containing a **who**, a **when** and a **where**. If you cannot supply a *where*, write `WHERE: unknown` — that is the first research task.
4. **Deduplicate ruthlessly.** Two candidates are the same segment if they would receive the same message, through the same channel, and buy the same package.
5. Tag each with its generator. Provenance goes in the output so the reader can see the search was systematic.

**Time budget:** 20–40 minutes for the whole engine. It is a generator, not an analysis.

---

## Block A — Establish the job (do this once)

Before generating anyone, answer:

**A1. What is the job?**
> *When [circumstance], they want to [motivation], so they can [expected outcome].*

**A2. What is the struggling moment?** The specific thing that goes wrong and starts the search.

**A3. What are the four forces?**
- **Push** — pain of the current situation
- **Pull** — attraction of the new
- **Anxiety** — fear of switching
- **Habit** — attachment to the current mess

**A4. What is the business genuinely better at?** Best-fit customers are the people structurally forced to care about *that*. If the honest answer is "nothing yet," record it — it changes the whole engagement.

**A5. Which pressures are in play?** compliance · time · risk · status · money. Segments under two or more convert most reliably.

## Block B — Obvious generators (expect 6–12 candidates)

### B1 · Job variants
The same job in different circumstances is different segments.
> *Ask:* who has this job **weekly** vs **once a year**? Under **deadline** vs **at leisure**? **Alone** vs **as part of a team**? **For themselves** vs **on behalf of someone else**?

### B2 · Trigger events
Convert the job into a queue.
> *Ask:* what public, dated event puts someone into this circumstance? Hiring · funding · new executive · new location · regulation · licence expiry · a failure · a lost client · a move · a launch · a season · a contract renewal · a tender publication.

### B3 · Roles (B2B)
A complex B2B purchase involves 6–10 people.
> *Ask:* who **uses** it · who **pays** · who **approves** · who **influences** · who **blocks** · who has to **defend it internally**. Each is a distinct audience with a distinct fear.

### B4 · Verticals (B2B)
Only where the vertical is *causal* — regulation, workflow, terminology, seasonality — not cosmetic.
> *Ask:* in which industries does this problem take a distinctive shape that a generalist tool or provider cannot speak to?

### B5 · Company stage / size
> *Ask:* at what size does this problem appear, become acute, and get solved in-house? The band between "appears" and "solved in-house" is your market.

### B6 · Budget tiers
The same problem is bought completely differently at different price points.
> *Ask:* who buys this at 10× your price? At 1/10? What changes in the process, not just the amount?

### B7 · Awareness stage
> *Ask:* who is **problem-aware but not solution-aware** (cheap, slow, uncontested) vs **solution-aware comparing options** (contested, fast) vs **actively evaluating a competitor** (highest intent)?

### B8 · Geography / language
> *Ask:* where is this problem worse — different regulation, different infrastructure, different payment habits, an underserved language or region?

### B9 · Occasion / season
> *Ask:* is there a recurring window when demand spikes? Who is inside that window right now?

### B10 · Existing customers, re-read
If there are paying customers, this is the highest-yield lens in Block B.
> *Ask:* sort by margin, cycle length, support load, retention, referrals. What do the top decile share that nobody named? What do the bottom decile share?

## Block C — Unexpected generators (require at least 3 candidates)

Run every one. These are the arbitrage.

### C1 · Who already built a workaround?
Lead users experience mainstream needs early and in extreme form, and build their own solutions.
> *Look for:* shared spreadsheet templates · Zapier or Make chains · scripts on GitHub · "I built a thing to solve X" posts · Notion templates sold on marketplaces · internal tools someone open-sourced.
> *Why it converts:* they have already paid in hours — proven willingness to pay in the currency that never lies. Their workaround is a free specification.

### C2 · Extreme users, both ends
> *Ask:* who does this **10× more than normal**? Who **tried the category and bounced in week one**? The heavy end shows the premium ceiling; the reluctant end shows the barrier a whole tier is stuck behind.

### C3 · The "wrong customer" in competitor reviews
> *Look for:* reviews saying *"great for agencies, useless for in-house teams like mine."* That is not a feature request. It is an unaddressed segment describing itself.
> Focus on **3- and 4-star reviews** — they bought, they are honest, they are not venting.

### C4 · Adjacent industries with the same job
> *Ask:* strip the industry from the job. Now search for that job in three unrelated industries. Who faces it there, and who is serving them?

### C5 · People paying for imperfect alternatives (Tier 1 noncustomers)
> *Look for:* customers of the category's worst-reviewed but widely used product · people on the cheapest plan of an expensive tool · anyone whose renewal is described as reluctant.
> *Why it converts:* spending is proven; only the destination is in question.

### C6 · Refusers (Tier 2 noncustomers)
> *Ask:* who evaluated this category and consciously said no — because it was unacceptable, unaffordable, or too complicated? **The most under-interviewed group in commerce.** Their reason is a product and positioning specification.

### C7 · The unexplored (Tier 3 noncustomers)
> *Ask:* who has this problem and has never considered that a solution category exists at all? They sit in a different market entirely.

### C8 · People with the problem but not the vocabulary
> *Look for:* posts phrased entirely as symptoms — *"why does this always happen to me," "am I the only one who…," "is there a normal way to do this."* Search queries with no category term.
> *Why it converts:* zero competition, because competitors optimise for category keywords these people never type. Slow, but content-led acquisition works unusually well.

### C9 · Hidden B2B buyers
> *Ask:* the category markets to the most senior seat. Who are the other five to nine? The daily user · the one who gets blamed · the blocker (security, legal, finance, procurement) · the champion who must sell it upward with no materials.
> *The unexploited asset:* nobody produces *"the internal deck you can forward to your CFO."*

### C10 · High urgency, low marketing saturation
> *Ask:* where is there a dated, penalised problem that is too unglamorous for funded competitors to make content about? You buy intent-level demand at awareness-level prices.

### C11 · Expensive pain under multiple pressures
> *Ask:* who is under **two or more** of compliance · time · risk · status · money simultaneously? Compliance + time is the classic combination: forced action, on a schedule, with a budget.

### C12 · Repeat questions with no supplier
> *Ask:* which question gets asked in this community every month, and has been for two years, with no good answer? Repetition is frequency evidence. That is a market, not a thread.

### C13 · Ask your users who else should use it
If there is a user base, run the PMF survey question directly:
> *"What type of people do you think would most benefit from this?"*
> Answered by people who understand the value and have no reason to flatter you. A free unexpected-audience generator.

### C14 · Averages hiding segments
> *Ask:* what does the "average customer" look like — and how many real customers actually resemble that average? If few, the average is concealing two or more real segments.

### C15 · Clone / transposition
> *Ask:* which model is mature in one geography, language or subculture and absent in another? Then verify **why** it is absent — sometimes the gap is a wall (regulation, payment habits, purchasing power), not an opening.

### C16 · Negative jobs — what they *don't* want to do
**Christensen flags this as often the most productive generator of all, and almost nobody runs it.** `[E]`

> *"I think I have as many jobs of **not** wanting to do something as ones that I want positively to do. In my experience, negative jobs are often the best innovation opportunities."*

**The question:** what does this customer wish they did not have to do *at all*? Not "do it better" — **not do it.**

**Canonical case.** Your child wakes with a sore throat; you are fairly sure it is strep. The job is not "obtain healthcare." The job is **"I don't want to see the doctor"** — don't lose the afternoon to a waiting room for a poke, a prod and a prescription you already anticipated. Rick Krieger founded QuickMedx after hours in an ER for his son's strep test; it became CVS MinuteClinic, now 1,000+ locations inside pharmacies.

**How to run it.** Take each step of the customer's current process and ask whether removal — not improvement — is the product. The answers cluster around: waiting · explaining yourself repeatedly · making a decision you feel unqualified to make · being seen doing something · learning something you resent having to learn · admitting a problem out loud.

**Why it generates unexpected segments.** Negative jobs are invisible to feature research, because nobody asks for the absence of a step. They surface only in complaints about the *process*, never about the product.

**Output:** a segment defined by the step they most want to skip.

---

### C17 · Unusual uses
**What people do with the product that you never designed for.** `[E]`

Church & Dwight watched what customers actually did with the orange box of Arm & Hammer baking soda: added to laundry detergent, mixed into toothpaste, sprinkled on carpets, left open in the fridge. Management had never imagined a job beyond baking. Those observations produced the first phosphate-free laundry detergent, then cat litter, carpet cleaner, air fresheners and deodorant. **The baking-soda box is now under 7% of Arm & Hammer's consumer revenue.**

NyQuil users taking a couple of spoonfuls purely to sleep, while not sick → ZzzQuil.

**Where to look:** support tickets describing a use case you never built · reviews from someone clearly not your intended customer · "life hack" content featuring your category · integrations and workarounds people publish.

**Test before promoting:** an unusual use is a segment only if the abstraction test passes (`jtbd-engine.md` §3) and someone would pay for a purpose-built version.

### The nonconsumption diagnostic

Run this against every market a client calls saturated: `[E]`

> **"If it feels like there isn't room for growth in a market, it could actually be a signal that you've defined the job poorly."**

Nonconsumption is not a soft concept — it is a **measurable gap** between the number of people who have the struggle and the number who buy anything at all.

- **Airbnb:** ~40% of guests would not have made the trip, or would have stayed with family. Virtually all hosts would never have considered renting a room.
- **Kimberly-Clark:** ~1 in 3 women over 18 has some incontinence issue; only a small percentage use any product, and people typically **suffer around two years** before buying one. Meanwhile they improvise with feminine hygiene pads — or stop travelling, stop going to restaurants, stop seeing friends. Depend Silhouette did $60m in year one and 30% growth in year two **without cannibalising existing products**, in a category everyone called mature.

**The move:** size the struggle population and the buying population separately. The gap is the segment.

### C18 · Pain concentration — where does it become 5–10× worse?
**The feedback loop from pain research back into segmentation, and one of the strongest unexpected-audience generators available.** `[S]`

Most pain statements are flat: *"companies waste time on reporting."* True, useless, describes nobody.

**The concentration question asks where the same pain is disproportionately severe** — and the answer is routinely a segment nobody has named:

```
FLAT:          "Agencies spend time on client reporting."
CONCENTRATED:  "Performance agencies running 15–50 client accounts, because
                reporting effort scales with accounts while headcount doesn't."
```

**Eleven axes to walk:** company size · volume · frequency · role · maturity · industry · regulation · geography · channel · technology stack · life stage.

**Why it produces good segments by construction:** the place where a common pain becomes acute is defined by **circumstance rather than demographics**, carries **higher willingness to pay** than the flat average implies, and is **not specifically addressed** by competitors serving the flat market.

**Full method:** `pain-opportunity.md` §5.

---

### C19 · Pain threshold — the scaling breakpoint
**Finds an ICP boundary with a trigger already built into it.** `[S]`

A pain often exists at every level and only *converts* above a breakpoint:

```
 20 leads/day  →  the spreadsheet is fine
100 leads/day  →  the spreadsheet is a nightmare
```

| Vague | With threshold |
|---|---|
| "Companies that need a CRM" | "Service businesses past ~5–10 sales staff, where manual lead handoff starts causing systematic lost enquiries" |

**How to find it:** ask switchers *"at what point did the old way stop working?"* · identify the **unit that scales** (accounts, leads, staff, SKUs, locations) · find where effort stops being linear.

**The confirmation test:** people **below** the line should describe the same situation and *not* consider it a problem. **If they do consider it a problem, you have found a different pain, not a threshold.**

**What it yields:** a measurable ICP boundary · a trigger (crossing it *is* the buying event) · timing (monitor the metric) · and an anti-audience (below the line).

**Full method:** `pain-opportunity.md` §6.

---

### C20 · Pain evolution — same company, different stage
**One business is several segments over time.** `[S]`

```
Stage 1  Where do we find customers at all?
Stage 2  How do we get customers predictably?
Stage 3  How do we lower CAC?
Stage 4  How do we scale without CAC rising?
Stage 5  How do we manage attribution across 8 channels?
```

**Five different segments wearing one company name.** A message built for stage 2 is invisible to stage 4.

**The diagnostic:** *"What was your biggest problem in this area a year ago, and is it the same now?"* A changed answer locates the stage **and** gives you the trajectory.

**Three consequences:** your best customers may sit at one stage only and will **age out** — model that as graduation, not churn · the next stage is your expansion path **or your ceiling** · **stage-crossing is a trigger**, same mechanism as C19.

**Full method:** `pain-opportunity.md` §7.

## Block C2 — The Different-X transformation matrix

**Generators produce candidates from the market. Transformations produce candidates from a candidate.** This is a mechanical operator: take any segment you already have and change exactly one dimension. Most transformations yield nothing; two or three yield a segment nobody in the category is serving.

**How to run it.** Take your strongest existing segment, walk all fourteen rows, and write down anything that is not obviously absurd. Ten minutes per segment. Do not filter while generating.

| # | Transformation | Change | Question | Signal it worked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T1** | **Different user** | Who actually uses it (buyer held constant) | Who ends up using this that we never designed for? | Support tickets from a role you never targeted |
| **T2** | **Different buyer** | Who pays (user held constant) | Could someone else pay for this same person's use? | "Can my employer be invoiced?" · reimbursement questions |
| **T3** | **Different job** | The progress sought | What other job does this same product get hired for? | Reviews describing a use you never marketed |
| **T4** | **Different trigger** | The entry event | What other event creates this need? | A CEP you mapped but never targeted |
| **T5** | **Different vertical** | The industry | Who else has this job in a harsher form? | The same workflow under a different regulator |
| **T6** | **Different company size** | Scale | Does this break differently at 10 people vs 500? | The product is "too much" or "not enough" in reviews |
| **T7** | **Different maturity** | Skill level | Beginner vs. advanced — are these one market? | "Too complicated" beside "too simplistic" |
| **T8** | **Different urgency** | Crisis vs. routine | Who needs this only when something has gone wrong? | Emergency-phrased search queries |
| **T9** | **Different channel** | Where they gather | Which unexpected community discusses this? | Your topic in a forum about something else |
| **T10** | **Different use case** | How it's used | Who uses it in a way we didn't intend? | Workarounds, unusual integrations, off-label use |
| **T11** | **Different alternative** | What it replaces | What non-obvious thing does this substitute for? | "We used to just hire someone for this" |
| **T12** | **Different budget owner** | Which department pays | Could another function's budget fund this? | The same tool sold to Sales vs. Ops vs. Compliance |
| **T13** | **Different geography** | Country, region, city | Where is this problem structurally worse? | Regulation, infrastructure or cost differences |
| **T14** | **Different life event** | The personal transition | Which life change creates this need? | Moving, a child, a new job, divorce, retirement, illness |

### The three that pay most often

**T11 — Different alternative** is the highest-yield row, and the one people skip. It forces the question *"what does this product actually compete with?"* — which routinely reveals that the real competitor is a person's time, an intern, a spreadsheet, or nothing at all. See `jtbd-engine.md` §7 for the full method and the coffee-shop worked example.

**T2 — Different buyer** is the most commercially consequential. Moving from "the user pays" to "their employer pays" typically multiplies the acceptable price by an order of magnitude for identical value, because the money is coming from a budget rather than a wallet.

**T12 — Different budget owner** is the B2B version of the same move. The identical product sold into a compliance budget rather than a marketing budget faces different scrutiny, different urgency, and usually a different price ceiling.

### Worked example — the transformation applied

```
Starting segment:  Freelance designers who lose track of client revisions

T1  Different user      → agency PMs coordinating designers (they feel the pain, don't do the work)
T2  Different buyer     → the CLIENT pays for clarity on their own project
T3  Different job       → not "track revisions" but "prove what was agreed" in a dispute
T5  Different vertical  → architects, whose revision cycles are regulated and dated
T8  Different urgency   → only when a dispute has already started — highest WTP, lowest volume
T11 Different alternative→ competes with a lawyer's letter, not with project software
T14 Different life event→ going independent for the first time; no process at all yet
```

Note what T3 + T11 did together: reframed from a productivity tool into evidence for a dispute — a different job, a different alternative, a different price, and a segment (architects, T5) with regulatory dates attached.

### Rules

1. **Change exactly one dimension at a time.** Two at once produces a fantasy rather than a candidate.
2. **Every transformation output enters at `L1`** (`evidence-ladder.md`) — plausible, unobserved. It must find one artefact before it can be scored.
3. **Transformations do not get a novelty discount.** A T-derived segment is scored on the same matrix as everything else.
4. **T1, T2 and T12 all imply buyer ≠ user.** If one of them fires, flag it — you have found two audiences, not one. See `category-entry-points.md` §5.

## The bottom-up alternative

Everything above is a **top-down** engine: apply lenses, produce 10–20 candidate hypotheses, then score and eliminate. It is fast, it works on thin input, and it is the right default for this skill's typical engagement.

**There is a second route, and where the data supports it, it is more rigorous.** McDonald & Dunbar build segments **bottom-up** from 30–50 *actual purchase combinations* observed in the market, weight each on benefits sought via a 100-point allocation, then cluster into 7–10 segments. Nothing is proposed; everything is found.

| | Top-down (this file) | Bottom-up (`market-mapping.md`) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | Candidate hypothesis | Observed purchase combination |
| Needs | Days, thin input tolerated | Weeks, sales data, often commissioned research |
| Output | 10–20 candidates to score | 30–50 micro-segments → 7–10 clusters |
| Best for | Early stage, direct-to-customer, beachhead choice | Established business, channel complexity, portfolio allocation |

**They are not in conflict** — candidates are *proposed*, clusters are *found*. Where data allows, prefer found over proposed. And note that the bottom-up method is the mechanised form of Christensen's instruction to **cluster rather than segment** (`jtbd-engine.md` §9).

**Before running either engine**, check whether a market map is needed (`market-mapping.md` §3). If a meaningful share of purchases is decided by contractors, distributors or specifiers rather than end users, **you are generating candidates at the wrong junction.**

## Block D — The anti-audience (mandatory, at least one)

### D1 · The D-segment
> *Ask:* who buys, extracts no value, generates support load, and leaves negative word of mouth?

### D2 · The X-segment
> *Ask:* who would be an excellent customer but requires product work you have not done? **Roadmap signal, not current demand.** Chasing it now distorts the product for a customer you cannot yet serve.

### D3 · The wrong-trust segment
> *Ask:* who requires certifications, references or procurement clearance you cannot obtain within the runway? Not a marketing problem — a time problem.

### D4 · The free-culture segment
> *Ask:* who has real pain and real money, in a category with an entrenched norm that this should be free?

## Output format for the generation run

One line per candidate. Do not analyse yet — analysis happens after deduplication and scoring.

```
[#] [Generator] SEGMENT: who · WHEN: circumstance/trigger · WHERE: habitat or "unknown"
     Pressure(s): ______   Obvious / Unexpected
```

**Example run fragment:**

```
[1]  B2  SEGMENT: marketing leads at 50–300-person B2B cos · WHEN: inherited a channel from someone who left, board review in 6 weeks · WHERE: LinkedIn role-change filter + 3 RevOps Slack groups
     Pressure: status, time   Obvious

[7]  C1  SEGMENT: ops managers maintaining elaborate Airtable bases for this · WHEN: base broke or the person who built it left · WHERE: Airtable community, r/airtable, template marketplaces
     Pressure: time, risk   Unexpected

[11] C9  SEGMENT: IT security reviewers who block these purchases · WHEN: a vendor review lands on their desk · WHERE: security Slack groups, vendor-questionnaire threads
     Pressure: risk   Unexpected

[14] C10 SEGMENT: marketplace sellers hit by an account block · WHEN: revenue stopped today · WHERE: seller Telegram chats, complaint forum sections
     Pressure: money, time   Unexpected

[19] D1  ANTI-AUDIENCE: solo freelancers on the entry plan · 31% of tickets, 6% of revenue, highest refund rate
```

## Deduplication test

Two candidates are the **same segment** if all three hold:

1. They would receive **the same message**
2. Through **the same channel**
3. And buy **the same package**

If any one differs, keep them separate — the difference is the point.

---

## Quality gate for the generation run

- [ ] 10–20 candidates after deduplication
- [ ] At least **3** from Block C
- [ ] At least **1** anti-audience from Block D
- [ ] Every candidate has a **when**
- [ ] Every candidate has a **where**, or an explicit `WHERE: unknown` flag
- [ ] Every candidate is tagged with its generator
- [ ] No two candidates survive the deduplication test as duplicates
- [ ] At least one candidate would surprise the client

**If the last box cannot be ticked, run Block C again.** A generation run that only confirms what the client already believed has not searched — it has agreed.
