# Interview & Survey Kits — Ready-to-Use Scripts

**Purpose.** Verbatim scripts. Copy, adapt the bracketed parts, run.

**The rule that governs every script here:** ask about **what has already happened**, never about opinions or predictions. Any question containing *"would you"* produces fiction. If a transcript contains no dates and no numbers, the interview failed.

---

## Kit 1 — Switch Interview (recent buyers)

**Use when.** There are buyers from the last 90 days. The single richest source of audience truth.
**Sample.** ~10 interviews, 30–60 minutes. Themes usually recur by interview 6–8.
**Goal.** Reconstruct the timeline backwards, and measure the four forces.

### Opening
> "I'm not selling anything and there are no wrong answers. I want to understand the story of how you ended up choosing [product/category]. I'll mostly ask about what actually happened, and I may go back over the timeline a couple of times — that's normal."

### The backward walk

**Anchor at the purchase:**
1. "When did you actually buy? What day, roughly?"
2. "Walk me through the day you decided. Where were you? What were you doing?"
3. "Who else was involved in that decision?"

**Step back to the shortlist:**
4. "What else were you looking at?"
5. "What made you rule the others out?"
6. "Was there a moment you nearly chose something else? What pulled you back?"

**Step back to the search:**
7. "When did you start actively looking?"
8. "**What did you type into Google?**" *(ask verbatim — this is your copy)*
9. "Where else did you look? Who did you ask?"

**Step back to the first thought — the critical zone:**
10. "Before you started looking — when did you first think 'this needs to change'?"
11. "**What happened?**" *(this is the trigger; do not accept a vague answer)*
12. "How long between that moment and starting to look?"
13. "What was happening in your work/life that week?"

**Step back to the old solution:**
14. "What were you doing before?"
15. "How long had that been going on?"
16. "What did it cost you — time, money, stress, something else?"
17. "Why didn't you change it earlier?" *(this is the Habit force — listen carefully)*

### The four forces
18. **Push:** "What was the worst part of the old way?"
19. **Pull:** "When you first saw [product], what made you think it might work?"
20. **Anxiety:** "What worried you about switching? What could have gone wrong?"
21. **Habit:** "What did you like about the old way? What did you lose by leaving it?"

### Closing
22. "If you couldn't use it tomorrow, what would you do?"
23. "Who else do you know with this problem?" *(density measurement — the answer is data, and the introduction is a bonus)*
24. "What should I have asked you that I didn't?"

### Recording template
```
Buyer: ______  Date of purchase: ______  Segment hypothesis: ______

TIMELINE
  First thought:  [date] ___________ TRIGGER: ___________
  Started looking:[date] ___________ SEARCHED: "___________"
  Shortlist:      ___________
  Decision:       [date] ___________ DECIDED BY: ___________

FORCES
  Push:    ___________  strength 1–5: __
  Pull:    ___________  strength 1–5: __
  Anxiety: ___________  strength 1–5: __
  Habit:   ___________  strength 1–5: __

COST OF OLD WAY: ___________  (money / hours / other)
ALTERNATIVES CONSIDERED: ___________
VERBATIM QUOTES: ___________
```

## Kit 2 — Problem Interview (pre-launch, no buyers yet)

**Use when.** There is nothing to buy yet. Establishes whether the problem exists and what it costs.
**Sample.** 10–15. Recruit from the habitat, **never from friends**.
**Goal.** Facts with dates and amounts.

### Opening
> "I'm researching how people handle [broad area]. I'm not selling anything — I genuinely just want to understand how this works for you. Is it all right if I ask about specific things that have actually happened?"

### Core questions
1. "Tell me about the last time [situation] happened."
2. "Walk me through what you did."
3. "How long did that take?"
4. "How often does that come up?"
5. "What's the most annoying part of it?"
6. "**What did it cost you last time?**" — push for money or hours
7. "What have you tried to fix it?"
8. "What happened with that?"
9. "**Have you paid for anything to help with this?**" — the willingness-to-pay question
10. "If you did nothing about this for the next six months, what happens?" — **the urgency test**
11. "Who else is affected when this goes wrong?"
12. "Whose budget would fix it come from?" *(B2B)*
13. "Who else has this problem that you know?" — density + referral

### Never ask
- ❌ "Would you use a tool that…" → hypothetical
- ❌ "Do you think this is a good idea?" → invites politeness
- ❌ "How much would you pay for…" → stated preference on an imaginary object
- ❌ "Is this a big problem for you?" → yes-biased
- ❌ Anything that describes your solution before question 13

### The three signals that matter most
| Signal | What it proves |
|---|---|
| They already **built a workaround** | Lead user. Willingness to pay, in hours. |
| They already **paid** for something | Willingness to pay, in money. |
| They can state a **cost** | The problem is quantified, therefore fundable. |

Two of three → a real segment. Zero of three → interest, not demand.

## Kit 3 — PMF Survey with segmentation

**Use when.** There is an engaged user base. **The segmentation step is the whole point.**
**Sample.** Users who engaged at least **twice in two weeks**. Surveying inactive users corrupts the result.

### The four questions — verbatim
```
1. How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?
   ( ) Very disappointed
   ( ) Somewhat disappointed
   ( ) Not disappointed

2. What type of people do you think would most benefit from [product]?

3. What is the main benefit you receive from [product]?

4. How can we improve [product] for you?
```

**RU version:**
```
1. Что вы почувствуете, если больше не сможете пользоваться [продуктом]?
   ( ) Сильно расстроюсь
   ( ) Немного расстроюсь
   ( ) Не расстроюсь

2. Кому, по-вашему, [продукт] был бы полезнее всего?
3. В чём для вас главная польза от [продукта]?
4. Что нам стоит улучшить именно для вас?
```

### Analysis — in this order
1. **Overall score** = % "very disappointed". Benchmark ~40%.
2. **Segment the responses by persona.** Assign each respondent to a segment.
3. **Recalculate the score per segment.** *(Superhuman went 22% → 32% at this step alone, with no product change.)*
4. **Build the High-Expectation Customer profile** from the persona dominating the "very disappointed" group.
5. **Discard "not disappointed" entirely.** Their feedback pulls you off course.
6. **Mine "somewhat disappointed who already value the core benefit"** — their answers to Q4 are the roadmap.
7. **Harvest Q2 for unexpected audiences.** Free generator, honest respondents.
8. **Harvest Q3 for positioning language.** This is how the value should be described — in their words, not yours.

### Recording template
```
Segment          n    Very disapp.   Main benefit named     Top improvement asked
_________       __         __%       _________________      _________________
```

## Kit 4 — Willingness to Pay (Van Westendorp)

**Use when.** Two segments score similarly and need a tiebreaker; or pricing per segment is in question.
**Sample.** 30+ per segment for anything directional.

### The four questions — verbatim
```
Thinking about [clearly described product/service]:

1. At what price would this be so LOW that you'd question the quality?
2. At what price would this be a BARGAIN — great value for money?
3. At what price would this start to feel EXPENSIVE, but you'd still consider it?
4. At what price would this be TOO EXPENSIVE to consider at all?
```

**RU version:**
```
1. При какой цене вы бы усомнились в качестве — «слишком дёшево»?
2. При какой цене это выгодная покупка?
3. При какой цене это дорого, но вы бы ещё рассматривали?
4. При какой цене это слишком дорого, чтобы вообще рассматривать?
```

### Rules
- Describe the product **identically** to every segment, or you are testing the description.
- **Stated preference, not behaviour.** Never use as sole WTP evidence.
- **Always triangulate** against: what they already pay · competitor prices that have survived years · ad longevity in the segment.
- The output is a **corridor per segment**, and the comparison between corridors is the finding.

## Kit 5 — Expert Interview

**Use when.** You need market structure fast, and you have no access to buyers.
**Who.** People who sell *to* your audience but do not compete with you — adjacent vendors, consultants, association staff, trade journalists, recruiters in the vertical.

### Questions
1. "Who actually buys in this market, and who just looks?"
2. "Who buys fastest? Who wastes everyone's time?" — *their D-segment, free*
3. "How does a purchase like this normally get approved?"
4. "Who has to sign off that people forget about?" — *the blocker*
5. "What's the busy season and the dead season?"
6. "Where do these people actually find suppliers?"
7. "What's changed in the last two years?"
8. "Which part of this market is growing, and which is quietly shrinking?"
9. "Who is being badly served right now?"
10. "Who should I talk to next?"

**Caveat.** Second-hand. Experts generalise, and their generalisations are often a decade old. Use for structure, not for pain.

## Kit 6 — Manual outreach (validation, not sales)

**Use when.** Testing reachability and message resonance simultaneously. Free, fast, and the answer is unambiguous.
**Sample.** 30 named individuals. If you cannot build the list, the segment has already failed the list test.

### Template
```
Subject: [specific observable fact about them]

Hi [name],

I saw [specific, verifiable observation — a job posting, a post they wrote,
a change at their company, something in a community].

I'm researching how [their role] handle [specific problem] — not selling
anything, I'm trying to understand whether the way we're thinking about it
is right.

Would you be up for 15 minutes? Happy to share what I've found from the
other [N] conversations — a few things have been surprising.

[name]
```

### Rules
- **One specific, verifiable observation per message.** Its absence is why generic outreach fails.
- Ask for a **conversation**, not a sale.
- Offer something back — the aggregate findings are genuinely valuable and cost you nothing.
- **Comply** with anti-spam law and platform rules. This is not optional.
- **Do not automate before it works manually.**

### Reading the result
| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5+ replies from 30 | Reachable and the pain statement lands. Proceed. |
| 1–4 replies | Reachable; the message needs work. Iterate. |
| 0 replies from 30 well-researched messages | **A reachability verdict, not a copy problem.** Re-examine the segment. |

**What matters more than the rate:** what they *say*. A reply saying "that's not really our problem, the real issue is X" is worth more than five polite yeses.

## Kit 7 — Review mining protocol

**Use when.** Always. First method when there is no access to buyers.

### Procedure
1. Pick the 3 most-reviewed competitors, plus your own product if it has reviews.
2. Pull **10–15 recent reviews across the whole rating range** per product.
3. **Concentrate on 3- and 4-star reviews** — they bought, they are honest, they are not venting.
4. Tag each review: `pain · praise · switching reason · use case · segment signal`.
5. **Count theme frequency.** This converts subjective complaints into an ordered list.
6. **Capture verbatim phrasing** — this is your copy, not a paraphrase of it.
7. **Flag any reviewer who is clearly not the intended customer.** That is a segment describing itself.

### Recording template
```
| Theme | Count | Verbatim example | Which competitor | Segment signal |
|-------|-------|------------------|------------------|----------------|
```

**Surfaces.** G2 · Capterra · TrustRadius (B2B software) · Amazon, Wildberries, Ozon (consumer) · App Store, Google Play, RuStore (mobile) · Otzovik, Yandex Maps, 2GIS (RU local and general).

**Minimum defensible first pass** (RU practice, a reasonable default anywhere): ~20–30 reviews + ~10 call recordings + ~10 search queries + a set of forum and social comments.

> **Reviews are not comments, and the difference decides what you can conclude.** Reviews contain **buyers only**, written retrospectively in response to a form. Comments contain buyers, near-buyers, **refusers** and lurkers, written unprompted in the moment. If you need non-customers — and for an avatar you almost always do — run **`avatar-from-comments.md`** rather than extending this kit.

## Kit 8 — Sales call analysis

**Use when.** Recordings exist. The highest-fidelity source most businesses already own and never mine.

### Procedure
1. Take the **last 20 calls**, split won / lost / stalled.
2. Tag each by: objection raised · named alternative · trigger mentioned · who else was involved · price reaction.
3. **Compare won vs. lost on segment attributes.** This is ABCDX applied to the pipeline.
4. **Extract verbatim phrasing.** Do not skip this — the copy is in the transcripts.

### The questions the tags should answer
- What objection appears in **every** lost deal?
- What attribute do **all** won deals share?
- What alternative comes up most, and do we address it anywhere in our materials?
- At what point in the call does the energy change?

**Example finding shape:** *"Every lost deal mentioned a security review; every won deal had fewer than 50 employees."* — a segment boundary discovered from the pipeline rather than invented.

## Sample-size guidance

| Method | Minimum for a signal | Comfortable | Diminishing returns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Switch interviews | 5 | 10 | 15 |
| Problem interviews | 8 | 12 | 20 |
| PMF survey | 40 responses | 100 | 300 |
| Van Westendorp | 30 per segment | 100 | 200 |
| Review mining | 15 reviews | 40 | 100 |
| Sales calls | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| Manual outreach | 30 sent | 50 | 100 |

**The termination rule:** stop when new inputs stop changing your conclusions. Audience research has decreasing returns and a real opportunity cost — a decision at 70% confidence this week beats 90% next quarter, because the test that follows produces better evidence than more desk research would.
