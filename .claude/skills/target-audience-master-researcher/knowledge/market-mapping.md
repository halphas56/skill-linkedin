# Market Mapping & the Micro-Segment Engine

**The systematic, bottom-up route to segments.** Where `jtbd-engine.md` discovers segments qualitatively from struggling moments, this file builds them mechanically from **actual purchase combinations** observed in a market — and first establishes *where in the chain the decision is even made.*

**Source:** Malcolm McDonald & Ian Dunbar, *Market Segmentation: How to Do It and How to Profit from It* (4th ed., Wiley 2012), via McDonald's own summary paper, read in full. `[E]`

**Why it earns its place.** This skill was strong on *who has the pain* and weak on *where the buying decision physically sits in a distribution chain*. For anything sold through channels — retail, distributors, contractors, resellers, specifiers, agents — that gap is fatal, because the person with the need is frequently not the person who chooses.

---

## §1 — The market definition rule

> **"A market is the aggregation of all products or services which customers regard as being capable of satisfying the same need."** — McDonald `[E]`

**Note the convergence.** This is *the same test* as Christensen's abstraction test (`jtbd-engine.md` §3), reached independently from the market-structure side rather than the demand side. Two independent traditions, one rule: **the market boundary is drawn by what the customer would substitute, not by what the category is called.**

Within this skill's own evidence rules, that convergence is worth naming: it is a claim now corroborated across independent source types, not a single author's assertion.

**Worked example.** The in-company caterer is one option for satisfying lunch-time hunger. So are external restaurants, pubs, fast-food specialists and sandwich bars. Define the market as "staff catering" and you have hidden four of your five competitors.

### Why correct market definition is non-negotiable

McDonald lists five consequences, all of which break silently if the definition is wrong: `[E]`

1. **Share measurement**
2. **Growth measurement**
3. **Specification of target customers**
4. **Recognition of relevant competitors**
5. **Formulation of marketing objectives and strategy**

> *"Defining a market too broadly or too narrowly can both lead to meaningless statistics."*

**A market share number is only as meaningful as the market definition under it.** When a client quotes a share figure, ask what market they are measuring against before you use it for anything.

### What needs-based market definition looks like in practice

McDonald's financial-services example is the clearest demonstration available: `[E]`

| Market (needs-based) | The need |
|---|---|
| **Emergency Cash ("Rainy Day")** | Cash to cover an undesired, unexpected event — often loss of or damage to property |
| **Future Event Planning** | Schemes to protect and grow money for anticipated and unanticipated cash-calling events (car replacement, education, weddings, funerals, health care) |
| **Asset Purchase** | Cash to buy assets they require (car, house, once-in-a-lifetime holiday) |
| **Welfare Contingency** | Maintaining a desired standard of living in times of unplanned cessation of salary |
| **Retirement Income** | Maintaining a desired standard of living once salary cheques have ceased |
| **Wealth Care and Building** | Care and growth of assets, at various risk and liquidity levels |
| **Day-to-Day Money Management** | Storing and readily accessing cash for day-to-day requirements |
| **Personal Financial Protection from Motor Vehicle Incidents** | *"Currently known as car insurance."* |

That last row is the whole lesson in one line. **A product name is a supplier's category. A need is the customer's.**

## §2 — Three distinctions that stop most segmentation going wrong

### Segment ≠ Sector
> *"Many companies pride themselves on their market segmentation even though these so-called 'segments' are in fact SECTORS."* `[E]`

A **sector** is an industry or category slice — "healthcare", "SMEs", "automotive". A **segment** is a group of customers with the same or similar needs. **There are many different purchase combinations within *and across* sectors.** A client whose "segments" are all industry names has not segmented.

### A priori segmentation is "the gravest mistake of all"
> *"Most books incorrectly state that there are several bases for segmentation, such as socio-economics, demographics, geo-demographics and the like. But this misses the point totally."* `[E]`

McDonald's demolitions:
- **"Boy George and the Archbishop of Canterbury are both As, but they don't behave the same!"** (socio-economics)
- *"Nor do all 18 to 24 year old women behave the same!"* (demographics)
- *"Nor does everyone in my street behave the same!"* (geo-demographics)

And the framing rule that follows:

> **"Anyone who says 'we segment markets by ....' is totally missing the point. Any market, once correctly defined in terms of needs rather than products, consists of 100 per cent of what is bought, how it is used and why it is bought and used in these ways. The role of any supplier is to understand these behavioural patterns and to discover their rationale, rather than trying to impose some predetermined segmentation methodology onto the market."**

**You do not impose segmentation. You uncover it.**

*These schemes are not useless* — they are useful at a high level and as **descriptors**. Young marrieds are a real group with furniture, kitchen and carpet needs. But within that group sit several distinct need-sets, and those are the segments.

### Customers ≠ consumers
The parent buying breakfast cereal is an **intermediate customer**, acting as an agent for the eventual consumers. The industrial purchasing officer buying wool tops is buying against specifications that originate with the person who will eventually wear the suit.

> *"The market needs that this manufacturing company is attempting to satisfy must in the last analysis be defined in terms of the requirements of the ultimate users — the consumer — even though the direct customer is quite clearly the purchasing officer."* `[E]`

**Confining your marketing to whoever places the order is a major mistake** — and §3 is the instrument that prevents it.

## §3 — The market map

**A market map defines the distribution and value-added chain between final users and suppliers**, including the buying mechanisms in the market and the part played by influencers. `[E]`

### How to build one

1. **Plot the stages** along the chain between final users and all suppliers competing in the defined market.
2. **Draw the routes to market** — not every product goes through every stage.
3. **At each junction, list the types** of company or customer found there.
4. **Quantify each junction** — split the volume or value between the junction types.
5. **Start at the final users' junction** and work back, noting at each junction with leverage **the volume/value (or % of the total market) that is *decided* there.**
6. **Guesstimate anything you don't know — and log it as follow-up work.** McDonald is explicit that estimating is correct practice on a first pass; the gap is a research task, not a reason to stop.

> **Practical note from the source:** sketch complex junctions separately, or build an outline map and add detail only at the junctions you intend to segment.

### The leverage calculation — the point of the whole exercise

McDonald's worked example: `[E]`

```
JUNCTION            VOLUME     DECIDED HERE          REMAINING
Final users          300k      70%  = 210k           30% still to be decided
Contractors           75k      20%  =  60k           10% still to be decided
Retailers            180k      10%  =  30k            0%
Distributors         105k       —                     —
Suppliers            300k
                              ─────────────────────────────
                              300k total decided
```

> *"In Figure 11 we see a market in which **30 per cent of annual sales are decided at junctions other than the final user junction.**"*

**Read what that means.** Nearly a third of this market is won or lost by contractors and retailers, not by the people who use the product. A campaign aimed entirely at final users is fighting for 70% of the market while conceding 30% uncontested.

**Terminology:** a **market leverage point** is a junction where a meaningful share of the market's purchase decisions is actually made.

### The rule that follows

> **"Segmentation can and should be carried out at all major junctions on the market map, not just at the final user junction."** `[E]`

Each leverage junction is its own segmentation problem with its own needs. Contractors do not want what householders want.

### Why this belongs in an audience skill

| Question this skill already answered | Question the market map answers |
|---|---|
| Who has the pain? | **Who actually decides?** |
| Where do they gather? | **What share of the market does that junction control?** |
| Who is in the buying group? *(inside one company)* | **Who is in the chain?** *(across the whole route to market)* |

**The failure mode it prevents:** building a beautiful audience strategy for end users in a market where a third of purchases are specified by someone you never contacted.

### The demand-pull lesson

McDonald's fertiliser case, told as a warning: a company prospered on superior product, reaching farmers **via merchants**. Competitors copied the technology, merchants stocked the alternatives, and prices and margins collapsed.

> *"Had the fertilizer company paid more attention to the needs of its different farmer groups and developed products especially for them, based on farmer segmentation, it would have continued to create demand pull-through differentiation."* `[E]`

**Selling only to the junction that places the order leaves you defenceless the moment your product advantage is copied.** The counter-example is P&G and Wal-Mart: P&G creates demand pull by attending to consumer needs *and* serves its direct customer's needs with integrated invoicing and replenishment triggered at the till.

## §4 — Micro-segments: the bottom-up engine

**Where segments actually come from in this method.** Not from lenses applied top-down, but from **cataloguing the purchase combinations that genuinely occur** and then clustering them.

### What a micro-segment is

> *"The uniqueness of a micro-segment is that when determining which of the alternative offers to be bought, the decision-makers it represents demonstrate a similar level of interest in a specific set of features."* `[E]`

The structure:

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| **What is bought** | Applications, forms (size, colour, branded/unbranded), specifications |
| **Where** | Principal channels used |
| **When** | Once a year, weekly, seasonally, on trigger |
| **How** | Cash or credit, tender, subscription, contract |
| **Who** | Descriptors — B2B: industry classification, size of firm; B2C: life stage, age, geography, lifestyle |
| **Why** | **Benefits sought** — the hardest row, and the one that makes it a segment rather than a spreadsheet |
| *(plus)* | **Size** — volume or value this micro-segment accounts for |

**Micro-segments are actual purchase combinations that take place in a market.** They are observed, not invented.

### Volumes

- **30–50 micro-segments** is normal in most markets. `[E — the source reads "between 30 and so micro-segments"; "so" is an OCR corruption of "50", read in context. Flagged rather than silently corrected.]`
- **7–10 final segments maximum.** *"It is impracticable to deal with more than between 7 or 10 market segments."*

That ratio — roughly 40 down to 8 — is the work.

### The critical caveat McDonald flags himself

> *"Finally, and most difficult of all, each purchase combination has to have a brief explanation of the reason for this particular type of behaviour... it is often at this stage that an organization needs to pause and either commission market research or refer to its extant database of previous market research studies."* `[E]`

**The "why" row is where this method stops being desk work.** If you cannot fill it from evidence, you have a micro-segment table at `L1` (`evidence-ladder.md`) — a structured hypothesis, not a finding. Say so.

## §5 — KDFs and CPIs: features vs. benefits, made operational

Two terms that this skill previously collapsed into one, and separating them is what makes the clustering work.

### KDF — Key Discriminating Feature
Of everything customers look for in a purchase (what, where, when, how), the **KDFs are only those features customers actually use to select between the alternative offers available.** `[E]`

Most features are table stakes. **A feature everyone offers discriminates nothing** and must be excluded from the model. Screening for this alone typically halves the list.

### CPI — Critical Purchase Influence
> *"As customers only seek out features regarded as key because of the benefit(s) these features are seen to offer them, the benefits delivered by each KDF should be listed... These benefits are Critical Purchase Influences."* `[E]`

**KDF = what they look for. CPI = why they look for it.** The CPI is the level at which two apparently different customers turn out to want the same thing.

**The subtle point that is easy to miss:**
> *"For some customers it is only by **combining** certain KDFs that they attain the benefit(s) they seek — benefits should also be looked at from this perspective."*

A benefit can be delivered by a *combination* of features, none of which delivers it alone. Analysing features one at a time will miss those customers entirely.

### The 100-point allocation

> *"Once the CPIs for the market have been developed their relative importance to each micro-segment is addressed **(by distributing 100 points between the CPIs)**."* `[E]`

A **constant-sum** allocation, per micro-segment. This is what makes clustering mathematically possible: every micro-segment becomes a vector of weights over the same set of CPIs, so similarity between them can be measured rather than argued.

**Why constant sum rather than 1–5 ratings:** forcing a fixed total makes respondents trade off. Rating scales let everything be "very important," which produces no discrimination and therefore no clusters.

---

## §6 — Forming segments

1. **Score** all CPIs for each micro-segment via the 100-point allocation.
2. **Measure similarity** between micro-segments from those scores.
3. **Cluster** micro-segments with similar requirements. *"Any good statistical computer program can carry out cluster analysis"* — or do it by hand at small scale.
4. **Size each cluster** by adding the volumes or values of its constituent micro-segments.

**Preliminary Segments** — an optional earlier move: take the client's *existing* segmentation hypothesis and use it as a lens to test *"whether it stacks up."* Useful, and a diplomatic way to disprove a cherished internal model with the client's own data.

## §7 — The segment checklist (the reality check)

McDonald's Step 7. Four questions, and the fourth is the one nobody else asks. `[E]`

> 1. **Is each cluster big enough to justify a distinct marketing strategy?**
> 2. **Is the offer required by each cluster sufficiently different?**
> 3. **Is it clear which customers appear in each cluster?**
>    *If all "yes", clusters = segments.*
> 4. **Will the company change and adopt a segment focus?**

**Question 4 is a delivery gate this skill did not have.** Questions 1–3 test the segmentation. **Question 4 tests the organisation.** A segment the company will not reorganise around is not a segment — it is a slide.

McDonald is blunt about the organisational stakes: segmentation *"affects every single corporate activity, it should not be just an exercise that takes place within the marketing department"*, and *"the most senior levels of management must lead this initiative."*

**In this skill:** ask Question 4 during intake, not at delivery. If the honest answer is no, the engagement's deliverable changes — you are producing evidence to build a case for change, not a go-to-market plan.

### The three universal viability criteria

Stated separately in the same source, and consistent with the checklist: `[E]`

1. Segments should be of **adequate size** to provide the desired return for the effort.
2. Members should have **high similarity within**, and be **distinct from the rest of the market**.
3. **Criteria for describing segments must enable the company to communicate effectively with them.**

Criterion 3 is the reachability gate this skill already enforces, arrived at independently.

## §8 — The commoditisation diagnostic

**One of the most useful things in the whole source, and it reframes a complaint you will hear constantly.**

### How markets evolve

| Stage | Shape | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Homogeneous | One product, few competitors |
| Rapid growth | **Breaks into groups** | New entrants offer variations; customers now have choice |
| Maturity | Supply exceeds demand | Growth comes at the **low price end**; the **top end is immune**; **the middle suffers** |

### The trap

> *"Competing only on price is to assume that this is the main requirement of customers, whereas the truth is that this is rarely the case. It is just that a general lack of understanding about market segmentation on the part of suppliers about the real needs of customers in mature markets **forces them to trade on price, so encouraging the market to become a commodity market.**"* `[E]`

**Commoditisation is not a market condition that happens to suppliers. It is frequently a segmentation failure that suppliers cause.** McDonald: reaching "commodity" is *"by no means inevitable, and only occurs in markets where the suppliers do not understand the power of market segmentation."*

### The evidence: ICI Fertilisers

The industry — *especially the sales force* — believed **100% of farmers bought on price.** Segmentation found **seven distinct farmer types**: `[E]`

| Farmer | What they actually wanted | What the industry assumed |
|---|---|---|
| **"Arthur"** | Genuinely bought on price alone — **but only 10% of the market** | 100% |
| **"Oliver"** | Scientific farmer. Drove his fields with a satellite-linked computer analysing soil type, mixing his own N-P-K purely to maximise yield. Bought ingredients cheaply **only because no supplier had bothered to understand his needs** | "buys on price" |
| **"David"** | Show-off farmer. Wanted crops looking healthy and cows with good skins. Technical talk would switch him off instantly | "buys on price" |

> *"Every single supplier in the industry totally ignored the real needs of these farmers, and the only thing anyone ever talked about was price. The result: a market driven by price discounts, accompanied by substantial losses to the suppliers."*

ICI launched products and promotional approaches aimed at these farmer types and **became the most profitable subsidiary of ICI and the only profitable fertiliser company in the country.**

### The diagnostic to carry into every engagement

> **When a client says "in our market, everyone buys on price," treat it as a hypothesis about their research, not a fact about their market.**

The ICI number — **10% versus the 100% everyone believed** — is the reason. The people who look price-driven usually include a large group whose real need nobody ever asked about, who default to price because no supplier gave them anything else to choose on.

**Ask:** *"What proportion of your market genuinely buys on price alone, and what evidence establishes that number?"* If the answer is a sales-force impression, you have found the engagement's most valuable question.

### The straddling error

> *"Select a segment and serve it. **Do not straddle segments and sit between them.**"* `[E]`

The photocopier case: a company with **80% market share** and massive margins ignored a Japanese entrant with small photocopiers, then cut prices in response. **Within three years its share was 10%.** It had failed to recognise the market was segmented and tried to compete in all segments with one product — *"a mistake made by hundreds of erstwhile market leaders."*

Pairs with the Air Force cockpit and the milkshake (`jtbd-engine.md` §1): **the product built for everyone fits no one.** McDonald's own version of the line:

> *"There is no such thing as an average customer or consumer — it's a bit like saying my head is in the oven and my feet are in the fridge, so on average I am quite comfortable."*

## §9 — The full seven-step process

Stage 1 — **Your market and how it operates**
- **Step 1 · Market Mapping** — structure and decision makers

Stage 2 — **Customers and transactions**
- **Step 2 · Who Buys** — customer profiling (descriptors); optionally test the existing hypothesis as Preliminary Segments
- **Step 3 · What is Bought** — purchase options; list features, then narrow to **KDFs**
- **Step 4 · Who Buys What** — build the customer model; each entry is a **micro-segment**, profiled and sized

Stage 3 — **Segmenting the market**
- **Step 5 · Why it is Bought** — the benefits behind the KDFs = **CPIs**; allocate 100 points per micro-segment
- **Step 6 · Forming Segments** — measure similarity, cluster, size the clusters
- **Step 7 · Segment Checklist** — the four-question reality check (§7)

### The condensed five-step version, in McDonald's own words

> 1. Define the market to be segmented and size it (market scope)
> 2. Determine how the market works and identify who makes the decisions (market mapping)
> 3. Develop a representative sample of decision-makers based on differences they see as key (including what, where, when and how), note who they are (demographics) and size them
> 4. Understand their real needs (why they buy, the benefits sought)
> 5. Search for groups with similar needs

## §10 — When to use this instead of, or alongside, the JTBD engine

**Honest positioning: this is the heavyweight tier.** McDonald himself says *"professional market segmentation is hard work, and is time-consuming."* Most users of this skill are a founder with a fortnight, not a corporate team with a research budget.

| Use **`jtbd-engine.md`** when | Use **this file** when |
|---|---|
| Early stage, no customer base | Established business with sales data |
| Need depth on one struggling moment | Need coverage of a whole market |
| Direct-to-customer | **Sold through channels, distributors, specifiers or contractors** |
| Days available | Weeks available |
| Qualitative evidence is all there is | Volumes and values exist or can be estimated |
| Choosing a beachhead | Allocating resources across a portfolio |

**They are complementary, not competing.** The strongest engagement runs:

1. **Market definition** (§1) — both traditions agree on the test
2. **Market map** (§3) — find the leverage junctions *before* deciding whom to research
3. **JTBD depth** at the chosen junction — struggling moments, four forces, verbatim language
4. **Micro-segments** (§4) if data supports it — otherwise the lens generators
5. **Clustering** (§6) — Christensen's "cluster, don't segment," mechanised
6. **Both checklists** — this skill's scoring plus McDonald's four-question reality check

**Note the reconciliation on counts:** this skill generates **10–20 candidate segments** as *hypotheses to be scored and eliminated*. McDonald derives **7–10 segments** from *30–50 observed micro-segments*. These are not in conflict — they are different objects. Candidates are proposed; clusters are found. Where data allows, prefer found over proposed.

## §11 — Quality gate

- [ ] **Market defined by need**, not by product category — and the substitution test run (§1)
- [ ] Any **market share figure** the client quotes has a stated market definition under it
- [ ] **"Segments" checked against "sectors"** — are these need groups or industry slices? (§2)
- [ ] **No a priori bases** used as the segmentation logic; demographics used only as descriptors
- [ ] **Customer vs consumer** distinguished — whose needs ultimately drive the specification?
- [ ] **Market map drawn** to the final user, with routes to market
- [ ] Each junction **quantified**, with estimates flagged as follow-up work
- [ ] **% of the market decided at each junction** calculated — and the non-final-user total stated explicitly
- [ ] Segmentation considered at **every leverage junction**, not only at the end user
- [ ] KDFs screened — **table-stakes features excluded**
- [ ] CPIs derived from KDFs, including **benefits delivered only by combinations**
- [ ] **100 points allocated** per micro-segment across CPIs
- [ ] Clusters **sized** by summing constituent volumes
- [ ] **7–10 segments maximum** in the final answer
- [ ] **All four checklist questions** answered — including *"will the company actually change?"*
- [ ] If the client says "everyone buys on price," that claim has been **tested, not accepted** (§8)
- [ ] Every element carries an **evidence level**; a micro-segment table with a guessed "why" row is `L1`

### The test

> **"Positioning is easy. Market segmentation is difficult. Positioning problems stem from poor segmentation."** — McDonald `[E]`

If a client arrives complaining that their messaging is not landing, the fault is usually upstream of the message. Check the segmentation before rewriting a single line of copy.
