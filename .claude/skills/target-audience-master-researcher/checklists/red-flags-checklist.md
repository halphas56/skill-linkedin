# Red Flags Checklist — Warning Signs the Audience Is Weak

**Use:** run before delivering any audience recommendation, and whenever a client's existing audience definition needs auditing. Each flag has a **tell** (how you spot it), a **mechanism** (why it fails), and a **repair**.

**Severity:**

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 🔴 | **Fatal.** Do not recommend this segment until repaired. |
| 🟠 | **Serious.** Recommend only with the flag stated in the output. |
| 🟡 | **Caution.** Note it; it changes tactics, not the decision. |

---

## A. Definitional red flags

### 🔴 A1 — "Everyone with this problem"
**Tell.** Write the promise. No segment would find it surprising.
*RU form:* «наша ЦА — все, у кого есть эта проблема».
**Mechanism.** No message can be written, no channel chosen, no proof assembled. A message that excludes nobody attracts nobody.
**Repair.** Apply beachhead conditions — same product, same sales process, word of mouth between them. Narrow until all three hold.

### 🔴 A2 — Demographics only
**Tell.** The definition is age, gender, income and location, with no circumstance.
*RU form:* «женщины 25–45, доход средний, интересуются саморазвитием».
**Mechanism.** Demography does not explain buying and actively **averages two real audiences into one fictional one** — the milkshake failure.
**Repair.** Define by job and circumstance. Keep demographics only as ad-platform targeting parameters, clearly labelled as such.

### 🔴 A3 — No *when*
**Tell.** The definition names people but no circumstance, trigger or moment.
**Mechanism.** Without a circumstance you have a list, not demand. Demand is created by a struggling moment.
**Repair.** Add the trigger: what happened in the week before they started looking?

### 🔴 A4 — No *where*
**Tell.** No habitat named, or only platform names ("LinkedIn," "Telegram").
**Mechanism.** You do not set the channel's rules. An audience with no named venue is a fantasy with demographics attached.
**Repair.** Name specific instances — this Slack group, these six creators, this register. Run the Friday test.

### 🟠 A5 — Persona theatre
**Tell.** A hobby, a stock photo, and no decision criterion. *«Марина, 34, любит йогу»* attached to a B2B brief.
**Mechanism.** A biography generates no decisions. Personas work only when they capture the buying decision.
**Repair.** Rebuild as Five Rings: priority initiative, success factors, perceived barriers, buyer's journey, decision criteria. Delete every attribute that changes no decision.

### 🟠 A6 — The definition is really two segments
**Tell.** The same package would have to be shaped two different ways to serve it; or the pain statement needs an "or" in it.
**Mechanism.** Averaging two segments produces messaging that lands with neither.
**Repair.** Split, score separately, choose.

---

### 🔴 A7 — Wrong level of abstraction
**Symptom.** The job statement is really a product spec or a preference. Every alternative you can name comes from the **same product category**.

**Test.** List everything the person could hire to make this progress. *"A 12-oz chocolate milkshake in a disposable container"* → all candidates are milkshakes → not a job. *"Keep me occupied on a boring drive and full until 10 a.m."* → banana, doughnut, bagel, Snickers, coffee → a job. `[E — Christensen]`

**Why it matters.** The abstraction level sets the market size, the competitor set, and the message. Too low and you are optimising a spec inside a category the buyer does not think in.

**Fix.** Go up one level and re-list the alternatives. You have arrived when candidates come from genuinely different categories — and the strongest sign is when *"do something else entirely"* appears on the list.

---

### 🟠 A8 — The job is described in adjectives
**Symptom.** The definition contains *convenient · seamless · easy · affordable · reliable · premium · efficient*.

**Why it matters.** *"If you or a colleague describes a Job to Be Done in adjectives and adverbs, it is not a valid job."* `[E — Christensen]` **"Convenience" is not a job** — it may be an experience that causes someone to choose you, but it cannot be targeted, tested or timed.

**Fix.** Rewrite in **verbs and nouns**. What are they trying to *do*, to *what*?

---

### 🟠 A9 — A need or a life theme wearing a job's clothes
**Symptom.** *"They need to grow their business"* (need — true on most days, gives no direction) or *"they want to feel like a good parent"* (life theme — important, abstract, will not trigger a purchase).

**Test.** A **need** is true of the person regardless of circumstance. A **life theme** cannot be attached to a moment. A **job** has a circumstance and a clock.

**Fix.** Ask what obstacle blocks that theme, in what specific circumstance. That obstacle is the job.

---

### 🔴 A10 — "Segments" that are actually sectors
**Symptom.** The segment list is industry names: healthcare, retail, SMEs, automotive.

**Why it matters.** *"Many companies pride themselves on their market segmentation even though these so-called 'segments' are in fact SECTORS."* `[E — McDonald]` A segment is a group with the same or similar **needs**; there are many different purchase combinations **within and across** sectors.

**Fix.** Ask what the customers in each "segment" actually want, and whether that differs from the next one. If two sectors want the same thing, they are one segment. If one sector contains three need-sets, it is three.

---

### 🔴 A11 — A priori segmentation
**Symptom.** The segmentation logic *is* the demographic scheme — socio-economics, age bands, geodemographics, purchase volume.

**Why it matters.** McDonald calls this *"the gravest mistake of all."* *"Boy George and the Archbishop of Canterbury are both As, but they don't behave the same."* And: **"Anyone who says 'we segment markets by ....' is totally missing the point."** `[E]`

**Fix.** Uncover the purchase combinations that actually occur, then cluster by need. Demographics come back afterwards as **descriptors** — the labels that make a found segment reachable, never the logic that created it.

---

### 🟠 A12 — Market defined by product, not need
**Symptom.** The market is named after what the client sells: "the pensions market", "the CRM market", "our catering business".

**Why it matters.** *"A market is the aggregation of all products or services which customers regard as being capable of satisfying the same need."* `[E]` Define staff catering as a market and you have hidden the restaurants, pubs, fast-food outlets and sandwich bars that compete for the same lunch-time hunger. **Every share and growth figure computed on a wrong definition is meaningless.**

**Fix.** Run the substitution test. Independently confirmed by Christensen's abstraction test — if all the alternatives are in your own category, the boundary is drawn wrong.

---

### 🟠 A13 — "Everyone in our market buys on price"
**Symptom.** Stated as a fact about the market, usually sourced to the sales force.

**Why it matters.** In the ICI Fertilisers case the entire industry believed this. **The genuinely price-driven segment was 10% of the market.** `[E]` The rest looked price-driven because *no supplier had ever offered them anything else to choose on* — including a scientific farmer optimising yield with satellite soil analysis, whom everyone had filed under "buys cheap."

**Why it is a red flag and not a finding:** a lack of segmentation understanding *"forces them to trade on price, so encouraging the market to become a commodity market."* Commoditisation is frequently self-inflicted.

**Fix.** Ask: *"What proportion genuinely buys on price alone, and what evidence establishes that number?"* Treat the answer as the engagement's most valuable open question.

---

### 🔴 A14 — The profile was built with one eye on your own offer
**Symptom.** Every pain listed happens to be one this product relieves. Every gain happens to be one it creates.

**Why it matters.** This is the **conforming-data fallacy applied to profiling** — you will find exactly what you set out to find, and the research will feel like confirmation. Osterwalder's instruction is to work *"like ethnographers"* and **temporarily forget what you sell**. `[E]` A business-book publisher's readers choose between books, consultants, YouTube and an MBA; a profile limited to book-related jobs misses the actual decision.

**Detection.** Cover the product name and read the profile. If a stranger could reverse-engineer what you sell from it, it was written backwards.

**Fix.** Rebuild from the customer's circumstance outward, with the product hidden. Then check fit afterwards — and expect not to answer every pain. *"Удовлетворить их все невозможно."*

---

### 🟠 A15 — Pains and gains carry no numbers
**Symptom.** "Takes too long." "Too expensive." "Wants better performance." "Wants a pay rise."

**Why it matters.** None of these can be ranked, compared across segments, or used to size the economic value of the problem. **Every adjective has a number behind it.** `[E]`

**Fix.** *"Waiting is a waste of time"* → after how many minutes? → **"losing more than X minutes."** *"Improve performance"* → to what level? → **"raise X above Y."** *"A pay rise"* → to what amount? This is how you learn how the customer measures success and failure — which is the specification for the promise, the proof, the price and the guarantee.

---

### 🟠 A16 — Functional jobs only
**Symptom.** The profile is a list of tasks. The social and emotional columns are empty or generic.

**Why it matters.** *"Социальные или эмоциональные задачи оказываются важнее лежащих на поверхности функциональных задач. Задача «хорошо выглядеть в глазах окружающих» может быть важнее задачи поиска удачного технического решения."* `[E]` In B2B the unstated emotional job is usually career risk, and it decides deals that look purely technical.

**Also check:** jobs and outcomes have not been merged. Jobs are the work being done; gains are the results wanted. Merged, neither is actionable.

**Fix.** Ask what happens to *them personally* if this goes wrong, and how they would explain the choice to their boss, their client or their spouse.

## B. Demand red flags

### 🔴 B1 — Interest confused with intent
**Tell.** The evidence is engagement — followers, likes, saves, "this is so useful!" comments.
*RU form:* «у нас 40 тысяч подписчиков, но никто не покупает» — these two facts are consistent, not contradictory.
**Mechanism.** People buy an identity cheaply and a transformation rarely. In B2B, engagement often comes from competitors and peers.
**Repair.** Re-score against completed transactions. Run a costly-action test — email, booking, deposit. Never a click.

### 🔴 B2 — No evidence of buying behaviour
**Tell.** Nobody in the segment can name the last comparable thing they paid for.
**Mechanism.** Past spending in a category is the only reliable predictor of future spending. Everything else is inference.
**Repair.** Find the existing budget line, or reclassify the segment as unvalidated and name the experiment.

### 🔴 B3 — Ability without willingness
**Tell.** The segment obviously has money and the category has a strong free-culture norm.
**Mechanism.** Ability and willingness fail independently. Enterprises with millions refuse to pay $50 for a category they have decided should be free.
**Repair.** Test willingness directly — price corridor research plus what they already pay. If the norm is entrenched, change segment or change the category framing.

### 🟠 B4 — Chronic pain mistaken for urgency
**Tell.** Real pain, described vividly, tolerated for years.
**Mechanism.** Chronic pain looks like urgency in interviews and behaves nothing like it in pipelines. It produces enthusiastic conversations and no purchase orders.
**Repair.** Find the acute sub-segment — the moment the chronic condition becomes dated. Same people, different circumstance.

### 🟠 B5 — Zero search volume in an *existing* market
**Tell.** Established category, competitors exist, nobody searches for it.
**Mechanism.** In an existing market, absent search means absent demand. *(In a new market it means nothing — check the market type before panicking.)*
**Repair.** Verify market type first. If existing, treat as a demand verdict.

### 🟡 B6 — Everyone is satisfied
**Tell.** Review mining returns no consistent complaints.
**Mechanism.** An overserved market. Not fatal, but it means differentiation from above is unavailable.
**Repair.** Attack from below — cheaper, simpler, for the segment that finds the incumbents excessive.

## C. Reachability red flags

### 🔴 C1 — The list test fails
**Tell.** "Where would you find 200 of them by Friday?" has no answer.
**Mechanism.** Unreachable is unreachable. No amount of pain or money compensates.
**Repair.** Find a reachable **proxy** — an observable attribute correlating with the segment — or name the asset that must be built and reclassify as a future target.

### 🔴 C2 — Targeting a psychological state
**Tell.** The definition is built on values, ambition or attitude. "Ambitious people who value quality."
**Mechanism.** No platform has a checkbox for it and no list exists.
**Repair.** Convert to a behavioural or habitat proxy: what do these people *do* or *subscribe to* that others do not?

### 🟠 C3 — Channel-audience mismatch
**Tell.** The audience lives on video; the expert refuses to appear on camera. Or the only viable channel is one the business cannot operate.
**Mechanism.** Rarely stated aloud, routinely fatal. Free channels are paid for in a currency this business may not have.
**Repair.** Either change channel, change the person producing, or change segment. State the mismatch explicitly in the output.

### 🟠 C4 — Community bans vendors
**Tell.** The habitat is dense and perfect, and its rules prohibit commercial participation.
**Mechanism.** The only compliant move is not to be there. One bad pitch ends access permanently.
**Repair.** Partner with a member, sponsor officially, or reach the same people via a different venue.

### 🟡 C5 — Dense but tiny
**Tell.** Three venues cover the segment; they total 400 people.
**Mechanism.** Not fatal — sometimes ideal for high-ticket — but it caps the business.
**Repair.** Check the model-market arithmetic. If price × 400 × capture rate does not clear the requirement, this is a beachhead, not a market.

## D. Economic red flags

### 🔴 D1 — ARPU cannot fund the channel
**Tell.** A high-touch sales motion is required by a segment paying a low monthly price.
**Mechanism.** Channel-model fit failure. Structural; marketing skill does not repair it.
**Repair.** Raise price, change segment, or find a self-serve motion. All three are strategy changes, not tactics.

### 🟠 D2 — One-off purchase with no referral loop
**Tell.** The need does not recur and the category is private or competitively sensitive.
**Mechanism.** Forces pure acquisition economics — the hardest game there is.
**Repair.** Not disqualifying. But the strategy must be search-and-referral, never retention. Treating a once-in-a-lifetime audience as a subscription audience is a strategy error.

### 🟠 D3 — Sales cycle exceeds runway
**Tell.** Nine-month committee cycles; four months of cash.
**Mechanism.** Arithmetic, not marketing.
**Repair.** Find a faster-closing sub-segment to fund the slower one, or change segment.

### 🟡 D4 — Uncountable
**Tell.** Nobody can estimate how many exist, even to an order of magnitude.
**Mechanism.** A segment you cannot count is usually a segment you cannot target — sizing doubles as a reachability test.
**Repair.** Build a bottom-up estimate. If it cannot be built, that is the finding.

## E. Competitive and positioning red flags

### 🔴 E1 — Positioning survives the swap test
**Tell.** Substitute the top competitor's name; the statement still reads true.
**Mechanism.** There is no differentiation, therefore no reason to choose you, therefore price becomes the only variable.
**Repair.** Return to competitive alternatives → unique attributes → differentiated value. Delete every value claim a competitor could honestly make.

### 🟠 E2 — "We have no competitors"
**Tell.** The client says it. *RU form:* «прямых конкурентов у нас нет».
**Mechanism.** Almost always means the alternative is inertia — and inertia is undefeated by default. Or it means a new market, which is a different and more expensive game.
**Repair.** Ask "what would they do if you didn't exist?" and treat the answer as the competitor.

### 🟠 E3 — Phantom competitors
**Tell.** The competitor list contains companies you have never actually lost a deal to.
**Mechanism.** You position against an imaginary fight and lose the real one to a spreadsheet you never mentioned.
**Repair.** Cross out every competitor with no lost deal attached. Work with what remains.

### 🟠 E4 — Copying a competitor's audience
**Tell.** The segment was chosen because a competitor targets it.
**Mechanism.** You inherit their segment, claims and weaknesses, and surrender difference. They may also be wrong.
**Repair.** Use competitor research to find who they **ignore** — read their reviews for customers they clearly did not design for.

### 🟡 E5 — Maximum market sophistication
**Tell.** Every claim in the category has been made; all competitor messaging is identical.
**Mechanism.** Direct claims no longer create contrast.
**Repair.** A **new mechanism** or a **narrower audience**. Nothing else works at this stage.

## F. Process red flags

### 🔴 F1 — Assumptions dressed as findings
**Tell.** Confident statements with no source. A scoring matrix that is mostly unevidenced cells.
**Mechanism.** The one unforgivable error in this discipline: presenting a hypothesis as a recommendation.
**Repair.** Mark every unevidenced cell. If more than half are assumptions, relabel the deliverable a research plan. That is a legitimate output.

### 🔴 F2 — Fabricated proof
**Tell.** A case study, statistic or testimonial that cannot be traced to a real event.
**Mechanism.** Business-ending risk, not a shortcut.
**Repair.** Replace with `[PROOF NEEDED: how to obtain it]`. Always.

### 🟠 F3 — Founder assumption never tested
**Tell.** The whole segment rests on "I know this market."
**Mechanism.** Founder confidence correlates with proximity to the problem, not with market size.
**Repair.** Convert every founder claim into a hypothesis with a named source or a named experiment.

### 🟠 F4 — Research that never terminates
**Tell.** Three weeks in, no decision, and new findings only confirm.
**Mechanism.** Decreasing returns with real opportunity cost. Analysis paralysis is a documented failure mode of this exact work.
**Repair.** Minimum defensible pass, then decide, then test. 70% confidence this week beats 90% next quarter — the test produces better evidence than more desk research would.

### 🟠 F5 — Halo scoring
**Tell.** The favoured segment scores 4–5 on every criterion.
**Mechanism.** Row-wise scoring produces halo effects. Real segments have trade-offs.
**Repair.** Re-score column-wise: every segment on criterion 1, then criterion 2, and so on.

### 🟡 F6 — No anti-audience named
**Tell.** The output recommends who to pursue and never who to refuse.
**Mechanism.** Without exclusion there is no position, no qualification speed and no margin protection.
**Repair.** Name the D-segment, in resource terms: *"this group is 30% of inbound, 8% of margin, 40% of support."*

---

## G. Regime red flag

### 🔴 G1 — Narrow targeting in a mass-market regime
**Tell.** The client is an established brand in a mass consumer category with distribution already solved, and the recommendation proposes abandoning most of the market.
**Mechanism.** In established consumer categories, growth comes from reaching more category buyers — especially light buyers — not from deepening loyalty in a narrow segment. Competing brands' customer bases are near-interchangeable.
**Repair.** Name the regime before recommending. Narrow the *acquisition mechanics*; keep the *brand assets* broad and consistent. The symmetrical error — telling a bootstrapped consultant to reach all category buyers — is equally fatal.

## H. The Anti-ICP — a deliverable, not a warning

Sections A–G catch weak segments during analysis. **The Anti-ICP is the artefact you hand the client**: an explicit, defensible description of who they should refuse, and the machinery to actually refuse them.

**Every engagement produces one.** A recommendation without an Anti-ICP has not made a choice — it has expressed a preference.

**The strongest argument for it is not margin — it is defect prevention:**

> *"It's actually important to signal 'this product is not for you' or they'll come back and say it's a crummy product."* `[E — Christensen]`

If someone hires your product for a job it was not built for, **you risk alienating them permanently** and collecting a public review describing a failure you never promised to avoid. Refusing the wrong customer protects the reputation you need for the right one.

**Framing rule:** the Anti-ICP is a **resource-allocation decision, never a judgement about people.** State it in numbers — share of inbound, share of margin, share of support load — and it becomes an operational fact rather than an insult. This matters for how it reads in a client's deck, and it matters for whether they can act on it.

### The nine disqualifier patterns

| # | Pattern | Why it destroys value | Detection question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI-1** | **Strong pain, no money** | The most seductive trap in the discipline. Maximum gratitude, zero revenue | "What have they paid for anything comparable?" |
| **AI-2** | **Has money, problem isn't a priority** | Ability without urgency. Infinite pipeline, no close | "What's already on their list this quarter?" |
| **AI-3** | **Hard to identify and find** | Every acquisition is bespoke; nothing compounds | "Where is the list? Can I name 200 of them?" |
| **AI-4** | **Very high CAC** | Each customer costs more than they return, and volume makes it worse | "What did the last five cost to acquire?" |
| **AI-5** | **Low LTV** | One small purchase, no expansion, no repeat | "What's the second purchase, and when?" |
| **AI-6** | **Highly specific requirements** | Custom work masquerading as product; the roadmap gets hijacked by one account | "How much of this is reusable for anyone else?" |
| **AI-7** | **Low retention** | Churn eats acquisition; you are filling a bucket with a hole | "Why did the last ones leave?" |
| **AI-8** | **Entrenched incumbents** | Switching costs and relationships exceed your differentiation | "What would it take for them to leave their current vendor?" |
| **AI-9** | **Enormous trust required before purchase** | Time-to-credibility exceeds runway. Structurally not a marketing problem | "What proof have they actually accepted before, and how long did it take?" |

### Reading the combinations

Individual patterns are survivable. Certain pairs are not:

| Combination | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **AI-1 + AI-3** (no money, hard to find) | Refuse outright. Expensive to reach, nothing at the end |
| **AI-4 + AI-5** (high CAC, low LTV) | Structural loss. No marketing skill repairs the arithmetic |
| **AI-6 + AI-5** (custom work, low LTV) | An agency business wearing product clothing |
| **AI-8 + AI-9** (incumbents + trust) | Not accessible to a young business at any budget |
| **AI-2 + AI-7** (not a priority, churns) | They never wanted it; they were persuaded |

### The Anti-ICP profile

```
ANTI-ICP
Who to refuse:        ______________________________
Patterns present:     AI-__, AI-__
Currently:            __% of inbound · __% of revenue · __% of support load
Cost of serving them: ______________________________
What we give up:      ______________________________   (be honest — refusal costs something)

DISQUALIFYING QUESTIONS  (for the first call — each must be answerable in one sentence)
1. ______________________________
2. ______________________________
3. ______________________________

OPERATIONAL EXCLUSIONS
Negative keywords:      ______________________________
Ad exclusion audiences: ______________________________
Lead-form disqualifiers:______________________________
Copy that repels them:  ______________________________   (say who this is NOT for, explicitly)
Referral script:        ______________________________   (who to send them to instead)
```

### The two special cases

**The X-segment.** Would be an excellent account, but needs product work you have not done — large corporates, government, regulated buyers. **This is a roadmap signal, not current demand.** Refuse it *for now*, name the condition that would change the answer, and put it in the backlog rather than the pipeline. `[E — ABCDX]`

**The D-segment.** No real need. Even if they buy, they extract no value and generate a negative experience — a bad review, a refund, a support burden, and a case study you can never use. **These are actively harmful sales**, and the strongest argument for the Anti-ICP is that some revenue costs more than it pays. `[E — ABCDX]`

### The referral move

The best Anti-ICP does not just refuse — it **redirects**. Name who these people should go to instead: a cheaper tool, a different category, a partner, a free resource.

This costs nothing, converts a rejection into goodwill, generates reciprocal referrals from whoever receives them, and — the part that surprises clients — produces some of the warmest word of mouth available, because being told honestly "we're not right for you" is memorable in a way that a sales pitch never is.

## I. The organisational gate

**Three questions test the segmentation. The fourth tests the company.** `[E — McDonald, Step 7]`

> 1. Is each segment **big enough** to justify a distinct strategy?
> 2. Is the offer required by each segment **sufficiently different**?
> 3. Is it **clear which customers** fall in each one?
> 4. **Will the company change and adopt a segment focus?**

### 🔴 I1 — The company will not actually change
**Symptom.** The client wants a segmentation but has no intention of altering the product, the pricing, the sales script, the channel or the org chart.

**Why it matters.** Segmentation *"affects every single corporate activity"* and *"should not be just an exercise that takes place within the marketing department."* A segment nobody will reorganise around is **a slide, not a segment.**

**Fix — and this is a scoping decision, not a delivery one.** Ask question 4 **during intake.** If the honest answer is no, the deliverable changes: you are building the evidence case for change, not a go-to-market plan. Both are legitimate. Confusing them wastes the engagement.

### 🟠 I2 — Straddling
**Symptom.** One offer aimed at two segments, positioned between them.

**Why it matters.** *"Select a segment and serve it. Do not straddle segments and sit between them."* A photocopier incumbent with 80% share competed in all segments with one product and **fell to 10% within three years.**

**Fix.** Pick, or build genuinely separate offers. The middle of two segments is occupied by nobody.

### 🟡 I3 — More than ten segments
**Symptom.** The deliverable lists fifteen.

**Why it matters.** *"It is impracticable to deal with more than between 7 or 10 market segments."* Beyond that, none of them gets a real strategy.

**Fix.** Cluster further, or declare a portfolio priority and treat the rest as unserved.

## J. Pain red flags

Full method: `../knowledge/pain-discovery.md`.

### 🔴 J1 — The complaint was recorded as the pain
**Symptom.** The pain list is what people said first.

**Why it matters.** *"What appears as the problem on the surface may be only a symptom."* `[E]` The complaint explains a support ticket; the root explains a purchase.

**Fix.** Ladder — *"why does that matter to you?"* — until the answer stops changing. Stop **one rung above a life theme**.

---

### 🔴 J2 — Threat raised without efficacy
**Symptom.** Copy that names the pain vividly and is vague about the fix.

**Why it matters.** High threat with low efficacy produces **fear control**: defensive avoidance, denial, reactance. The reader defends against *the message*, not the problem. `[E — EPPM]` **This is not merely unethical; it is predicted to fail.**

**And it fails on layout.** A truthful page whose relief sits below the fold is fear control regardless of intent.

**Fix.** Every sentence naming a pain must be within sight of a credible, achievable mechanism. Supply **both** halves of efficacy — "the fix works" *and* "you specifically can do this."

---

### 🔴 J3 — A pain used in acquisition with no mechanism or no proof
**Symptom.** Fields 10 or 11 of the pain map are empty and the pain is in the ad anyway.

**Fix.** It belongs in research, roadmap, and content that does not sell. Not in acquisition.

---

### 🟠 J4 — A desire, objection or feature request filed as a pain
**Test.** *Has this already cost them something specific that they can name?* A desire costs nothing yet. An objection appears only after you show up. A feature request is a solution wearing a problem's clothes — ladder it.

---

### 🟠 J5 — Divergent roots averaged
**Symptom.** Ten people laddered from one complaint to three different roots, and the deliverable reports one.

**Why it matters.** **That is three segments.** Averaging them produces a fictional person — the milkshake error at pain level.

---

### 🟠 J6 — The TAS used to rank pains
**Symptom.** A weighted segment score applied to a pain list.

**Fix.** Use the **PPS**. TAS ranks segments; PPS ranks pains inside a segment. Different objects.

---

### 🔴 J9 - Mentions counted and called importance
**Symptom.** A ranked pain list built by tallying reviews, comments or search volume.

**Why it matters.** Griffin & Hauser tested exactly this and found **high-priority needs are not mentioned more often than low-priority ones**. `[E]` Counting measures **prevalence**. Importance has to be asked for.

**Fix.** Report prevalence and severity in separate columns. Never sum them, never infer one from the other.

---

### 🔴 J10 - Pain scored once
**Symptom.** A single number ranking pains.

**Why it matters.** It blends *how much this hurts* with *whether it's a market*, and hides the quadrant that matters - **real suffering with no budget**, the trap that most resembles a discovery.

**Fix.** Two scores, reported with the quadrant named.

---

### 🟠 J11 - Complaining treated as evidence
**Symptom.** "Twenty people complained about this" presented as validation.

**Why it matters.** Complaining is **B1** - the weakest rung there is. A built workaround costs hours; a job posting costs a salary.

**Fix.** Record the highest **behavioural rung** observed. If nobody built a workaround, ask why not.

---

### 🟠 J12 - Cost of inaction asserted without arithmetic
**Symptom.** A confident annual figure with no working shown.

**Fix.** Show the multiplication and source every multiplier. `3 people x [HOURS UNKNOWN] x 52` is more honest than a confident total.

---

### 🟠 J13 - Empty "evidence against" column
**Symptom.** Only supporting evidence appears.

**Fix.** Write *"searched, none found"* so a reader can tell a clean bill from an unexamined one.

---

### 🔴 J14 - Fabricated customer quote
**Symptom.** A vivid, idiomatic quote with no source or date.

**Why it matters.** An LLM generates perfect customer quotes because a good quote is what the surrounding text calls for. It is still fabrication, and it is the fastest way to make a research document worthless.

**Fix.** Every quotation mark traces to an artefact with a source and date, or it loses its quotation marks.

### 🔴 J15 - Borrowed credential

**Symptom.** *"По моему опыту таких проектов…"* · *"In my experience…"* · *"Обычно в таких нишах…"* · *"I've seen this many times."*

**Why it matters.** It is **J14 without the quotation marks** — the same substitution of authority for evidence, minus the artefact that would let a reader check it. It survives review precisely because it looks like modesty rather than a claim, and a fabrication rule written around statistics and quotes does not catch it.

**Found by the 2026-08-27 cold run**, in a response that was otherwise disciplined enough to refuse the client's premise and specify the right success metric. **The strongest answers are where this hides**, because a run confident enough to contradict a client is confident enough to reach for standing.

**Fix.** Replace the standing with the mechanism. *"In my experience fear-led funnels lose money"* becomes *"fear without efficacy produces avoidance rather than purchase, so expect refunds concentrated in the first 14 days — measurable as revenue net of refunds at 60 days."* **The second sentence is checkable and the first is not, and that is the entire difference.**

## Delivery gate

Do not deliver an audience recommendation until:

- [ ] No 🔴 flags remain unrepaired, **or** each one is stated explicitly in the output as a condition
- [ ] Every 🟠 flag present is named in §2 (Key Assumptions) or §14 (what would make this wrong)
- [ ] The anti-audience is named
- [ ] The trade-off is named
- [ ] The load-bearing assumption is identified with the cost of checking it
- [ ] At least one thing in the report would disappoint the client if they were honest with themselves

**That last one matters.** An audience report that flatters every prior belief has not done any work. If nothing in the analysis surprised or cost anyone anything, re-examine it before sending.
